Formula and Notation Reference

Trap

This course reuses four letters for completely different jobs, and the formulas sit only a few pages apart. Fix these now:

  • k is address lines in address space (2k locations), pipeline stages in (k + n − 1), and ways per set in a set-associative cache. Three meanings, three topics.
  • n is a bit width in every integer-range formula, but the number of tasks in the pipeline formula. In (k + n − 1), n is not a bit count.
  • M is the mantissa in IEEE-754 but the number of memory blocks in the cache formulas.
  • T alone is the clock cycle time in CPU performance, but Tc and Tm are memory access times in cache. Different quantities, both in the same units.
  • e is the true exponent and E the stored one. They differ by the bias, which is the whole point of the field.

When a question hands you a letter, name the topic first, then the meaning.

The named laws, stated

A paper may ask you to state a law rather than use it, and a formula on its own does not answer that. Each of these is stated as a law should be: the claim in words, the conditions it holds under, the formula, and what it implies.

The laws, stated

Amdahl's law. The gain in overall performance from improving one part of a system is limited by the fraction of the total time that part accounts for. If a fraction f of the execution time is made s times faster and the rest is unchanged, the overall speedup is S = 1 / ((1 − f) + f/s); as s grows without bound, S tends to 1 / (1 − f), a ceiling no improvement to that part can pass.

The principle of locality of reference. Over any short interval of its execution, a program references only a small and predictable subset of its address space. It has two forms: temporal (a location used now is likely to be used again soon) and spatial (locations near one just used are likely to be used soon). It is the assumption the whole memory hierarchy rests on.

De Morgan's laws. The complement of a product is the sum of the complements, and the complement of a sum is the product of the complements: (A · B)' = A' + B' and (A + B)' = A' · B'. Breaking a bar over a group flips the operator underneath it.

The complement law. A variable ORed with its complement is always 1, and ANDed with its complement is always 0: A + A' = 1, A · A' = 0.

Recall and check

A question gives you k = 4 and n = 100. What are k and n, and which formula is this?

Answer

It depends on the topic, which is exactly the trap. If the question is about a pipeline, k is 4 stages and n is 100 tasks, so the time is (4 + 100 − 1) = 103 cycle times. If it is about a cache, k = 4 would be the associativity (4-way). If it is about the address bus, k = 4 lines would reach only 24 = 16 locations. Name the topic before you name the letter.

Exam Day Strategy

Knowing the material and scoring the material are different skills. This section is the second one: how to spend the two hours, which questions to take, and how to write an answer so that every mark it has earned is actually given.

By the end of this section you can budget the paper by the minute, choose your optional questions on evidence rather than instinct, structure an answer so partial credit is unavoidable, and recognise the time sinks that cost students the most.

The shape of the paper

The paper runs 2 hours for 70 marks. Question 1 is compulsory and carries 30 marks. You then answer any two of the remaining questions, each worth 20 marks. Question 1 is the broad one, usually several short parts spread across the whole syllabus; the optional questions go deeper on fewer topics.

TeachThe two-hour budget

70 marks in 120 minutes is about 1.7 minutes per mark. Do not spend it evenly; spend it deliberately:

MinutesSpend it onWhy
0 to 5Read the whole paper. Mark every part you can definitely do. You cannot choose your best two questions without seeing all of them.
5 to 55Question 1 (30 marks, about 50 minutes). Compulsory and broad, so it is where marks are most spread out and most certain.
55 to 85First optional (20 marks, 30 minutes). Take the stronger of your two choices first, while you are fresh.
85 to 115Second optional (20 marks, 30 minutes). Keep to the clock even if unfinished; a started part scores, a skipped one cannot.
115 to 120Check: units, carries, and that you answered exactly two optionals. The cheapest marks on the paper are the ones you already earned and lost.

If a part is costing more than double its mark value in minutes, leave a gap and move on. Come back only if time remains.

Choosing your two optional questions

TeachChoose on evidence, not on the topic name

Students pick the question whose topic feels familiar, then discover part (c) is the one they cannot do. Choose like this instead:

  • For each candidate question, read every part and tally the marks you are confident of. Not the marks you might get: the ones you are sure of.
  • Take the two questions with the highest confident tallies, even if the topics feel less comfortable.
  • Prefer questions whose parts are numeric with a stated method (cache fields, CPI, IEEE, pipeline timing). These are self-checking, so you know when you are right.
  • Beware a question with one huge part: a single 12-mark part you stall on is far more dangerous than four 5-mark parts.

Answer exactly two. Writing a third wastes minutes you needed elsewhere, and you cannot rely on the best two being the ones marked.

Writing an answer that collects its marks

Must memoriseThe four-line answer

For every numeric part, write these four things, in this order. Each is worth marks on its own, even when the final number is wrong:

  1. The formula, stated in symbols before any number goes into it.
  2. The substitution, showing each number in the place of its symbol.
  3. The arithmetic, in steps a marker can follow.
  4. The answer with its units, and a sentence saying what it means.

Memory hook: formula, substitute, work, units. A wrong final number with the first three shown usually keeps most of the marks; a bare correct number with none of them can still lose some.

TeachMarks are scoring points, so count them

The mark allocation tells you how many distinct things to say. Treat it as a checklist, not a suggestion:

  • 2 marks, "differentiate X and Y": one sentence on each, ideally with the question each answers. Two points, two marks.
  • 3 marks, "compare, include a diagram": two differences and the diagram. The diagram is a scoring point; students skip it under time pressure and lose it.
  • "State two aspects": write exactly two, clearly numbered. A vague paragraph containing both may be read as one.
  • "Explain" versus "state": "state" wants the fact, "explain" wants the fact and the reason. If the verb is "explain", write a "because".

When a part is worth more marks than you have points, you are missing something: look for an example, a diagram, or a consequence to add.

TeachWhen you are stuck
  • State your assumption and continue. If a question is ambiguous (which AMAT organisation, whether the block is in words or bytes), write "assuming look-through" and solve it. A clearly stated assumption is usually accepted; a blank is never.
  • Do the parts out of order. Nothing requires part (a) first. Bank (c) if (c) is the one you can do.
  • If a later part needs an earlier answer you could not get, invent a plausible value, say "taking X = ... from (a)", and carry it through. Method marks still apply.
  • Convert everything to one unit first. Most stalls in cache and performance questions are unit confusion, not ignorance: KB against bytes, ns against s, words against bytes.
Trap

The four most expensive habits in this paper. (1) Answering three optional questions and running out of time on all of them. (2) Doing arithmetic in your head and writing only the final number, so a slip costs the whole part instead of one mark. (3) Skipping the diagram when a question says "include a diagram", which is a guaranteed mark left behind. (4) Giving a number with no units: 5.2 is not an answer, 5.2 ns is.

Recall and check

You have 12 minutes left, one 8-mark part untouched, and a 4-mark part you rushed. What do you do?

Answer

Start the 8-mark part. Write its formula, substitute, and take the arithmetic as far as you can: an untouched part scores zero, while a started one collects method marks almost immediately. Only if it collapses should you return to polish the 4-mark part.
