Foundations and Data

Floating Point, IEEE 754

One 32-bit pattern has to hold both a tiny number like 0.0000001 and a huge one like 6 billion. Whole-number binary cannot stretch that far, so a cleverer layout is used, the same trick you already know from science class. The exam asks it both ways: a decimal to its 32-bit pattern, and a hex pattern back to decimal. One method each, and it is the same three fields every time.

TeachIt is just scientific notation, in binary

You already do this in decimal. To write 6,020,000 compactly you put it as 6.02 × 106: a sign (it is positive), a fraction (6.02, the meaningful digits), and an exponent (6, how far the point moved). Floating point stores a number in exactly this shape, but in base 2: a sign, a fraction, and a power of 2. Nothing more.

Those three pieces are the three fields, stored left to right: sign (s), then exponent (E), then mantissa M (the fraction, also called the significand).

  • Single precision: 32 bits = 1 sign + 8 exponent + 23 mantissa. It covers roughly 10±38.
  • Double precision: 64 bits = 1 sign + 11 exponent + 52 mantissa. It covers roughly 10±308.

Normalising is the one preparation step. In decimal scientific notation you insist on exactly one non-zero digit before the point (6.02, never 60.2 or 0.602). In binary you do the same: slide the point until the number reads 1.something × 2e. Since the only non-zero binary digit is 1, a normalised binary number always starts "1.", and that fact is about to save you a bit.

TeachThe two tricks: the hidden 1, and the bias

Two small tricks trip up every beginner, so meet them head on. Both have a reason.

  • The hidden 1. A normalised number always starts with "1." (from the step above). Storing that 1 would waste a bit on something you already know, so it is left out and just assumed. That is the "hidden 1": when you read a pattern you put a "1." back in front of the mantissa; when you write one you drop the leading 1 and keep only what follows the point. It buys you one extra bit of precision for free.
  • The bias. The exponent can be negative (for a small number like 2−3) or positive (for a big one). Rather than give the exponent its own sign bit, we add a fixed number, the bias, so the stored exponent is always a positive count. Bias is 127 for single, 1023 for double. It is like storing temperatures as "degrees above −127" so a minus sign is never needed: to get the true exponent you subtract the bias again.

Put together, the value a pattern stands for is ± 1.M × 2(exponent field − bias). Learn that one line and both directions fall out of it.

Must doEncode and decode
  • Decimal to IEEE (single): (1) sign bit from the sign. (2) Convert the magnitude to binary. (3) Normalise to 1.something × 2e by moving the point. (4) Exponent field = e + 127, in 8 bits. (5) Mantissa = the "something" after the point, padded with zeros to 23 bits (the leading 1 is dropped, it is the hidden 1). (6) Put the 32 bits together and group in fours for hex.
  • IEEE (hex) to decimal: (1) write the 32 bits, split as 1 / 8 / 23. (2) Sign bit gives the sign. (3) Real exponent e = exponent field − 127. (4) Value = ± 1.M in binary × 2e (remember to put the hidden 1 back).

Memory hook: "sign, exponent, mantissa", and the exponent is always stored plus the bias, read minus the bias.

Worked examplethe method, vertically

Convert C1400000 (single precision) to decimal. This is the X the exam sets in Question 4a; you will sit it verbatim in the final mock.

Given C1400000h, single precision
Formula value = (−1)sign × 1.M × 2(exp field − 127)
C1400000 = 1 10000010 100 0000...
  sign 1  ->  negative
  exp field 1000 0010 = 130,  e = 130 - 127 = 3
  mantissa 1.100... = 1 + 1/2 = 1.5
  value = -1.5 x 2³ = -1.5 x 8 = -12
C1400000 = −1.5 × 23 = −12

Now redo it: exponent field minus 127, then read 1.M as a binary fraction (first bit a half, next a quarter).

Your turnencode a clean number

Encode +6.0 in single precision. (Hint: 6 in binary is 110; normalise it.)

Answer

6 = 110 = 1.10 × 22. Sign = 0. Exponent field = 2 + 127 = 129 = 1000 0001. Mantissa = 100…0 (23 bits). Pattern: 0 10000001 10000000000000000000000 = 40C00000. Check by decoding: +1.5 × 22 = 6.

Your turnMCQ, the facts

(1) How many exponent bits in single precision? (2) The bias in double precision? (3) How many bits is double precision? (4) A floating-point number is stored as which order of fields?

Answer

(1) 8. (2) 1023. (3) 64. (4) sign, then exponent, then mantissa (the exam phrases the answer as "sign, mantissa, exponent", meaning those three parts with the sign first; the true field order on the chip is sign, exponent, mantissa).

Trap

Do not forget the bias: the exponent you compute is e + 127 going in, and field − 127 coming out. And the leading 1 is hidden, so when you read the mantissa put a "1." in front of it. Single precision has range about 10±38; double about 10±308.
