Floating Point, IEEE 754

How a computer stores numbers with a fractional part and a huge range, like 64.2 or −1240.56. The exam asks it both ways: a decimal to its 32-bit pattern, and a hex pattern back to decimal. One method each, and it is the same three fields every time.

TeachWhy, and the three fields

Floating point exists to hold very large and very small values in a few bits: its strength is range, not exact decimals. A number is stored as three fields, left to right: sign, then exponent, then mantissa (also called the fraction).

  • Single precision: 32 bits = 1 sign + 8 exponent + 23 mantissa. It covers roughly 10±38.
  • Double precision: 64 bits = 1 sign + 11 exponent + 52 mantissa. It covers roughly 10±308.

The value is ± 1.mantissa × 2exponent. The leading 1 is always there for a normalised number, so it is not stored (the "hidden 1"). The exponent is stored with a bias added so it can be negative without a sign of its own: bias is 127 for single, 1023 for double.

Must doEncode and decode
  • Decimal to IEEE (single): (1) sign bit from the sign. (2) Convert the magnitude to binary. (3) Normalise to 1.something × 2e by moving the point. (4) Exponent field = e + 127, in 8 bits. (5) Mantissa = the "something" after the point, padded with zeros to 23 bits. (6) Put the 32 bits together and group in fours for hex.
  • IEEE (hex) to decimal: (1) write the 32 bits, split as 1 / 8 / 23. (2) Sign bit gives the sign. (3) Real exponent e = exponent field − 127. (4) Value = ± 1.mantissa in binary × 2e.

Memory hook: "sign, exponent, mantissa", and the exponent is always stored plus the bias, read minus the bias.

Worked examplehex to decimal (a 2025/2026 number)

Convert C1400000 (single precision) to decimal. This is the X from Question 1a / 4a of the recent papers.

  1. Hex to bits, split 1 / 8 / 23: sign = 1; exponent field = 1000 0010; mantissa = 100 0000…
  2. Sign 1 means negative. Exponent field 1000 0010 = 130, so e = 130 − 127 = 3.
  3. Mantissa 1.100… = 1 + 1/2 = 1.5, so value = −1.5 × 23 = −1.5 × 8 = −12.
C1400000 = −1.5 × 23 = −12

Now redo it: the whole trick is exponent field minus 127, then read 1.mantissa as a binary fraction.

Your turnencode a clean number

Encode +6.0 in single precision. (Hint: 6 in binary is 110; normalise it.)

Answer

6 = 110 = 1.10 × 22. Sign = 0. Exponent field = 2 + 127 = 129 = 1000 0001. Mantissa = 100…0 (23 bits). Pattern: 0 10000001 10000000000000000000000 = 40C00000. Check by decoding: +1.5 × 22 = 6.

Your turnMCQ, the facts

(1) How many exponent bits in single precision? (2) The bias in double precision? (3) How many bits is double precision? (4) A floating-point number is stored as which order of fields?

Answer

(1) 8. (2) 1023. (3) 64. (4) sign, then exponent, then mantissa (the exam phrases the answer as "sign, mantissa, exponent", meaning those three parts with the sign first; the true field order on the chip is sign, exponent, mantissa).

Trap

Do not forget the bias: the exponent you compute is e + 127 going in, and field − 127 coming out. And the leading 1 is hidden, so when you read the mantissa put a "1." in front of it. Single precision has range about 10±38; double about 10±308.
