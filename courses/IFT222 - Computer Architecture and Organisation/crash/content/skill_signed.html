Signed Numbers

This is the most asked numeric topic, and the one most people find confusing. So we do it as a programme: many tiny steps, and you check each one before moving on. Go slowly. By the last frame the moves will feel obvious.

Work it frame by framecover, work, then slide down to check

Cover the page just below the first line with a card or your hand. Read a frame, do the small task, then slide the card down to the next line to check your answer. One step at a time, and do not skip.

Start with 8 bits and no sign yet. The patterns run 0000 0000, 0000 0001, all the way up to 1111 1111. That top one, 1111 1111, is 28 − 1. Write its value in decimal, then slide down.

255. Every one of the 256 patterns is a positive number; there is no minus sign anywhere yet. To make room for negatives we sacrifice the top (leftmost) bit and let it carry the sign: a top bit of 0 means positive. So a top bit of 1 must mean what?

negative. So just by glancing at 1010 1010 (top bit 1) you know it is a negative number. The only question left in the whole topic is which pattern to use for a given negative. The scheme real computers use is called two's complement, and the next few frames build it slowly. Read on.

Picture the 8 bits as a car odometer. It counts up 0000 0000, 0000 0001, and on to 1111 1111, then rolls back round to 0000 0000. Now turn it one step backwards from 0000 0000. What pattern do you land on?

1111 1111. One step below zero. So it is natural to call 1111 1111 the value −1. Step back once more, to 1111 1110, and that is −2. Negatives are just the patterns you meet counting down from the top. Counting back once more, what pattern is −3?

1111 1101. Counting all the way down would be slow, so here is the shortcut to build any negative: write the positive, flip every bit, then add 1. Try it on −1. Start with +1 = 0000 0001. Flip every bit and write the result.

1111 1110. Now the second half of the shortcut. Add 1 to 1111 1110. What do you get?

1111 1111. That is the same pattern the odometer gave for −1, so the shortcut is trustworthy. Say it like a chant: "flip every bit, add one." Now your turn, all by yourself: make −5 in 8 bits. Start with +5 = 0000 0101. Flip, then add 1.

1111 1011. (0000 0101 flips to 1111 1010, and adding 1 gives 1111 1011.) That pattern is −5. Notice you only ever do three things: positive, flip, add one. Read on.

Now the other direction: reading a negative pattern back into a number. The trick is the very same one. Take 1111 1011 (top bit 1, so it is negative). Flip it and add 1 to find its size. Flip 1111 1011, add 1, and say what size you get.

0000 0101 = 5, so the value is −5. (Flip 1111 1011 to 0000 0100, add 1 to get 0000 0101.) Writing a negative and reading one use the same move, which is why it is worth learning well. Read on.

Why did the world settle on this scheme? Two reasons. It has only one zero (just 0000 0000), and the ordinary adder does subtraction for free, because a − b is the same as a + (−b). Here is the proof. Add −5 (1111 1011) to +5 (0000 0101), and drop any carry that falls off the top. What 8-bit pattern is left?

0000 0000. (1111 1011 + 0000 0101 = 1 0000 0000; throw away the carry and you have 0000 0000, which is zero.) A number plus its negative gives zero, which is exactly what a negative should do. The scheme really works. Read on.

Last piece: the range. Half the patterns are negative and half positive, but zero uses up one positive slot, so there is always one more negative than positive: −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1. In 16 bits that is −32 768 to 32 767. So in 8 bits the range is −128 to what?

127. (−27 to 27 − 1 is −128 to 127.) That is two's complement, start to finish. Read on.

Two older schemes you only need to recognise for the objective test. Sign-magnitude: a sign bit then the plain size, so −42 is 1 then 010 1010 = 1010 1010; its drawback is two zeros (+0 and −0). One's complement: negate by flipping every bit only (no add 1); it needs an end-around carry when adding and also has two zeros. Two's complement beat both by adding that 1. How many zeros does sign-magnitude have?

two (+0 and −0). That is every idea in this topic. Now watch all the moves used together in the worked example below, then sit it yourself with the answer covered.

Must dothe moves on one card
  • Write a negative: positive in n bits, flip every bit, add 1.
  • Read a negative (top bit 1): flip, add 1 for the size, then put a minus in front.
  • Add: add the patterns, drop any carry off the top (no end-around carry).
  • Range in n bits: −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1 (16 bits: −32 768 to 32 767).

Memory hook: one's complement is "flip"; two's complement is "flip and add one".

Worked examplethe method, vertically

Store −182, 487 and −1023 in a 16-bit two's-complement machine (binary and hex) and add (−182) + 487. The exam sets exactly this; you will sit it verbatim in the final mock at the back. Here is how you write it on paper, one move at a time.

Given 16 bits, two's complement · −182, 487, −1023
Formula range = −215 to 215 − 1 · negative = flip the positive and add 1 · group 4 bits per hex digit
Range: -2¹⁵ to 2¹⁵ - 1  =  -32768 to 32767   (all three fit)

487  =  0000 0001 1110 0111  =  01E7

-182:  +182  =  0000 0000 1011 0110
     invert  =  1111 1111 0100 1001
        + 1  =  1111 1111 0100 1010  =  FF4A

-1023: +1023 =  0000 0011 1111 1111
     invert  =  1111 1100 0000 0000
        + 1  =  1111 1100 0000 0001  =  FC01

(-182) + 487:
     1111 1111 0100 1010
   + 0000 0001 1110 0111
   ---------------------
   1 0000 0001 0011 0001   drop the carry off the top
     0000 0001 0011 0001   =  256+32+16+1  =  305
Range −32768 to 32767 (all fit); 487 = 01E7, −182 = FF4A, −1023 = FC01; (−182) + 487 = 305.

Now redo it: positive, invert, add 1, every time. Practise with −75 in 8 bits (answer 1011 0101).

Your turnread three patterns

In 8 bits: (1) what decimal is 1000 0000 in two's complement? (2) what is the two's complement of 0010 1100? (3) what decimal is 1010 1010 in sign-magnitude?

Answer

(1) top bit 1 so negative; flip to 0111 1111, add 1 = 1000 0000 = 128, so −128. (2) flip 0010 1100 to 1101 0011, add 1 = 1101 0100. (3) sign bit 1 (negative), magnitude 010 1010 = 42, so −42.

Your turnMCQ, quick facts

(1) How many representations of zero in sign-magnitude? (2) Which scheme needs an end-around carry? (3) The one's-complement pattern 1110 0011 is which decimal? (4) Why is two's complement used?

Answer

(1) two (+0 and −0). (2) one's complement. (3) top bit 1 so negative; flip 1110 0011 to 0001 1100 = 28, so −28. (4) simpler and faster hardware (one zero, and add and subtract share one circuit).

Trap

Reading a negative two's-complement pattern, do not just read the magnitude bits off; you must flip and add 1 first. Overflow means the true result is too big or too small for the range, not division by zero. And the end-around carry belongs to one's complement only; two's complement simply discards the top carry.
