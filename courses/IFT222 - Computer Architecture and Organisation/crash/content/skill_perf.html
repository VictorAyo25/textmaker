The Machine

CPU Performance

How fast a processor runs is a calculation, not a mystery. Two formulas do almost everything: the CPU-time equation and Amdahl's law. This is the most heavily weighted numeric topic on the recent paper.

Must doThe performance formulas

A program's time is how many instructions it runs, times the average cycles each takes, times how long a cycle lasts:

  • CPU time = IC × CPI × T, where IC is the instruction count, CPI is the average cycles per instruction, and T is the clock cycle time. Since T = 1 / clock rate, this is also CPU time = (IC × CPI) / clock rate.
  • Average CPI = Σ (fraction of each type × its cycles), a weighted average. The fractions add to 1.
  • MIPS = clock rate / (CPI × 106), millions of instructions per second.
  • Improvement = (old − new) / old, always measured against the old time.

So performance is not set by clock speed alone: it also depends on the instruction count and the CPI (and behind CPI, the cache, branch prediction and instruction-level parallelism). That is why "judge a processor by clock frequency only" is wrong.

Worked exampleon the 2025/2026 paper (Q1d)

One million instructions, by addressing mode: Register 40% at 1 cycle, Immediate 20% at 1, Direct 15% at 2, Indirect 10% at 4, Indexed 10% at 3, Relative 5% at 2. Find the average CPI and total cycles; then if indirect is optimised to 2 cycles, the new CPI and the percent improvement.

  1. Average CPI = weighted sum = 0.40(1) + 0.20(1) + 0.15(2) + 0.10(4) + 0.10(3) + 0.05(2) = 0.40 + 0.20 + 0.30 + 0.40 + 0.30 + 0.10 = 1.70.
  2. Total cycles = IC × CPI = 1 000 000 × 1.70 = 1 700 000.
  3. Optimise indirect from 4 to 2 cycles: its share falls from 0.10(4) = 0.40 to 0.10(2) = 0.20, a drop of 0.20. New CPI = 1.70 − 0.20 = 1.50.
  4. Improvement = (1.70 − 1.50) / 1.70 = 0.20 / 1.70 = 11.76%.
CPI 1.70; total cycles 1 700 000; new CPI 1.50; improvement 11.76%.

Now redo it: average CPI is just a weighted average. To measure a change, recompute CPI with the one changed cost and compare against the old.

Must doAmdahl's law

Amdahl's law: if you speed up only part of a task, the part you did not touch limits the gain. Let f be the fraction of the time spent in the part you improve, and let that part become s times faster. Then the overall speedup is

S = 1 / ( (1 − f) + f / s )

As s grows without limit, the speedup can never beat 1 / (1 − f), because the untouched fraction (1 − f) still takes its full time. Memory hook: the slow part you left alone sets the ceiling, no matter how fast you make the rest.

Worked exampleon the 2025/2026 paper (Q3c)

A cache is 10 times faster than main memory, and the CPU finds what it needs in cache 85% of the time. Find the overall speedup.

  1. The sped-up fraction is f = 0.85 (the cache hits); the speedup of that part is s = 10.
  2. S = 1 / ((1 − 0.85) + 0.85 / 10) = 1 / (0.15 + 0.085) = 1 / 0.235.
  3. = 4.26.
S = 1 / (0.15 + 0.085) = 4.26, far below 10, because the 15% of slow accesses dominate. That is exactly Amdahl's point.

Now redo it: the "10 times faster" is s, not the answer; only the 85% gets divided by s, and the other 15% stays at full cost.

Your turnMCQ, performance

(1) A benchmark runs 12 billion instructions at 4 GHz with an average CPI of 1.5. Execution time? (2) Why should performance not be judged by clock frequency alone?

Answer

(1) time = (IC × CPI) / clock rate = (12×109 × 1.5) / (4×109) = 18 / 4 = 4.5 seconds. (2) because it also depends on CPI, instruction count, cache behaviour and instruction-level parallelism, not clock speed by itself.

Trap

Improvement is measured against the old time, (old − new)/old, not the new. In Amdahl's law, f is the fraction sped up and only that part is divided by s; a "10 times faster" cache with 85% hits gives about 4.26, not 10.
