The Machine

CPU Performance

How fast a processor runs is a calculation, not a mystery. Two formulas do almost everything: the CPU-time equation and Amdahl's law. This is the most heavily weighted numeric topic on the recent paper.

Everything about processor speed comes from one equation. A program's running time is three numbers multiplied together: CPU time = IC × CPI × T. IC is the instruction count (how many instructions actually run), CPI is the average cycles per instruction, and T is how long one clock cycle lasts. Say what each of the three, IC, CPI and T, stands for, then slide down.

instruction count, average cycles per instruction, clock cycle time. The clock rate you see advertised (say 4 GHz) is cycles per second, so one cycle lasts T = 1 / clock rate. Put that in place of T and write CPU time using clock rate.

CPU time = (IC × CPI) / clock rate. So a faster clock shortens the time, but a smaller instruction count or a smaller CPI does too. All three matter. Read on.

Look closer at CPI. Instructions are not all equal: a register add may take 1 cycle, an indirect memory access 4. The average CPI weights each type by how often it appears, adding up (fraction of the type × its cycles) over all types. The fractions must add to 1. Read on.

Try a tiny one. A program is 70% simple instructions at 1 cycle each and 30% at 3 cycles each. Work out the average CPI: (0.70 × 1) + (0.30 × 3).

1.60. (0.70 × 1 = 0.70, and 0.30 × 3 = 0.90, giving 1.60.) Notice 0.70 and 0.30 add to 1, exactly as the fractions must. Read on.

A second speed measure is MIPS, millions of instructions per second: MIPS = clock rate / (CPI × 106). A faster clock or a lower CPI both raise it. If the clock rate is 2 × 109 and CPI is 2, find the MIPS.

1000 MIPS. (2 × 109 divided by (2 × 106) gives 1000.) Read on.

When you improve something, always measure the gain against the OLD time: improvement = (old − new) / old. Old time 200, new time 150: what fraction is the improvement?

0.25, that is 25%. ((200 − 150) / 200 = 50 / 200 = 0.25.) Divide by the old value, never the new. Read on.

So is a chip fast just because its clock is fast? No. Because time = (IC × CPI) / clock rate, the instruction count and CPI count just as much, and behind CPI sit cache behaviour, branch prediction and instruction-level parallelism. Name one reason a high-clock chip can still be slow.

a high CPI (or a large instruction count, or poor cache or branch behaviour). That is why "judge a processor by clock frequency only" is wrong. Read on.

Now the second big formula, Amdahl's law. Suppose you speed up only PART of a task. The part you did not touch still takes its full time, so it caps the gain. Let f be the fraction of the original time spent in the part you improve, and let s be how many times faster you make that part. In your own words, what does f stand for?

the fraction of the time spent in the part you speed up. That part's time shrinks from f to f / s, while the rest, (1 − f), is unchanged. If the whole job was time 1, the new time is (1 − f) + f / s, and speedup is old over new. Write the speedup S.

S = 1 / ((1 − f) + f / s). Old time 1 on top, new time (1 − f) + f / s on the bottom. Read on.

Push s as large as you like, so f / s shrinks towards 0. Then S climbs only as far as 1 / (1 − f), because the untouched (1 − f) never goes away. Memory hook: the slow part you left alone sets the ceiling. With f = 0.85, what is the best speedup possible, even with an infinite s?

about 6.67. (1 / (1 − 0.85) = 1 / 0.15 = 6.67.) Now a real, finite case: a cache 10 times faster (s = 10) that the CPU hits 85% of the time (f = 0.85). First find the new time, (1 − f) + f / s. Compute 0.15 + 0.85 / 10.

0.235. (0.15 + 0.085 = 0.235.) So S = 1 / 0.235. Finish it: what is S?

about 4.26. (1 / 0.235 = 4.26.) Far below the cache's 10 times, because the 15% of slow accesses dominate, which is exactly Amdahl's point. The reference card and the two worked examples below sit both of these formulas; do them with the answers covered.

Must dothe performance formulas on one card
  • CPU time = IC × CPI × T = (IC × CPI) / clock rate (since T = 1 / clock rate).
  • Average CPI = Σ(fraction of each type × its cycles), and the fractions add to 1.
  • MIPS = clock rate / (CPI × 106).
  • Improvement = (old − new) / old, always against the old time.
  • Amdahl's law: S = 1 / ((1 − f) + f / s), ceiling 1 / (1 − f).

Memory hook: the slow part you left alone sets the ceiling, no matter how fast you make the rest.

Worked examplethe method, vertically

One million instructions, by addressing mode: Register 40% at 1 cycle, Immediate 20% at 1, Direct 15% at 2, Indirect 10% at 4, Indexed 10% at 3, Relative 5% at 2. Find the average CPI and total cycles; then optimise indirect to 2 cycles and give the improvement. The exam sets exactly this; sit it verbatim in the final mock.

Given IC = 1 000 000 · the share and cycle cost of each mode
Formula CPI = Σ(fraction × cycles) · total = IC × CPI · improvement = (old − new) / old
Avg CPI = 0.40x1 + 0.20x1 + 0.15x2 + 0.10x4 + 0.10x3 + 0.05x2
        = 0.40 + 0.20 + 0.30 + 0.40 + 0.30 + 0.10
        = 1.70

Total cycles = 1 000 000 x 1.70 = 1 700 000

Indirect 4 -> 2:  new CPI = 1.70 - 0.10x4 + 0.10x2
                          = 1.70 - 0.20
                          = 1.50

Improvement = (1.70 - 1.50) / 1.70 = 0.20 / 1.70 = 11.76%
CPI 1.70; total cycles 1 700 000; new CPI 1.50; improvement 11.76%.

Now redo it: weighted average, then recompute with the one changed cost.

Worked examplethe method, vertically

A cache is 10 times faster than main memory, and the CPU finds what it needs in cache 85% of the time. Find the overall speedup. The exam sets exactly this; sit it verbatim in the final mock.

Given f = 0.85 (cache hits) · s = 10
Formula S = 1 / ((1 − f) + f/s)
S = 1 / ((1 - 0.85) + 0.85/10)
  = 1 / (0.15 + 0.085)
  = 1 / 0.235
  = 4.26
S = 4.26, far below 10, because the 15% of slow accesses dominate. That is exactly Amdahl's point.

Now redo it: the "10 times faster" is s, not the answer; only the 85% gets divided by s, the other 15% stays at full cost.

Your turnMCQ, performance

(1) A benchmark runs 12 billion instructions at 4 GHz with an average CPI of 1.5. Execution time? (2) Why should performance not be judged by clock frequency alone?

Answer

(1) time = (IC × CPI) / clock rate = (12×109 × 1.5) / (4×109) = 18 / 4 = 4.5 seconds. (2) because it also depends on CPI, instruction count, cache behaviour and instruction-level parallelism, not clock speed by itself.

Trap

Improvement is measured against the old time, (old − new)/old, not the new. In Amdahl's law, f is the fraction sped up and only that part is divided by s; a "10 times faster" cache with 85% hits gives about 4.26, not 10.
