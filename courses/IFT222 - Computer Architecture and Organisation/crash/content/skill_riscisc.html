RISC, CISC and the Rest

Two design philosophies, plus the loose theory ideas the objective test dips into. Mostly describing and comparing, which means fast, reliable marks if you know the contrasts cleanly.

TeachRISC versus CISC

CISC (complex instruction set computer) has many, powerful, variable-length instructions, some doing a lot of work each (including direct memory-to-memory operations). Programs are short but the hardware is complex, instructions take several cycles, and it is harder to pipeline. Its instructions can resemble high-level language constructs, which eases the compiler. It suited the old days of slow, expensive memory.

RISC (reduced instruction set computer) has few, simple, fixed-length instructions, each ideally one cycle, with memory touched only by load and store (a load-store design), many registers, and hardwired control. Programs are longer but the hardware is simple, fast, and pipelines cleanly. It minimises chip area and power and leans on the compiler.

Examples. RISC: ARM, MIPS, SPARC, PowerPC. CISC: Intel x86, VAX, the Motorola 68000.

TeachA few loose ideas the MCQs use
  • SIMD (single instruction, multiple data): one instruction acts on many data items at once, to accelerate data-parallel work (graphics, matrices).
  • Domain-specific architecture: a processor tailored to one job (for example AI matrix work) can be far more efficient than a general one for that job.
  • Abstraction: layering the design (the ISA is the key layer) lets hardware and software teams work independently while staying compatible.
  • Backward compatibility: keeping a stable ISA while the organization underneath evolves is what lets old software still run on new chips.
  • Architecture versus engineering: computer architecture is the conceptual, operational design; computer engineering is the broader design, build and integration of the hardware.
Your turnMCQ, RISC/CISC and friends

(1) Variable-length instructions go with RISC or CISC? (2) A chip that minimises area and power and leans on the compiler follows which philosophy? (3) Why does SIMD exist? (4) What gives backward compatibility across processor generations?

Answer

(1) CISC (RISC is fixed-length). (2) RISC. (3) to accelerate data-parallel computations. (4) a stable ISA while the organization evolves underneath.
