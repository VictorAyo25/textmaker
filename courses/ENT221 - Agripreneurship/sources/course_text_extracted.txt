  1.1.1 
Agriculture and Agripreneurship  
 
Agriculture is a broad field that encompasses a wide range of activities, industries, 
and sectors. Agriculture is the science, art, and practice of cultivating plants and 
raising animals for food, fibre, medicine, and other products used to sustain and 
enhance human life. Here are some of the key aspects of agriculture: 
1. Crop Production – Growing cereals, fruits, vegetables, and cash crops. 
2. Animal Husbandry – Rearing livestock like cattle, poultry, sheep, and fish. 
3. Soil Management – Maintaining soil fertility and preventing erosion. 
4. Irrigation and Water Management – Efficient use of water for farming. 
5. Agricultural Engineering – Development of farming tools, machinery, and 
infrastructure. 
6. Agroforestry – Combining trees and crops for sustainable farming. 
7. Agricultural Economics – Business aspects of farming, including trade 
and markets. 
8. Food Science and Technology – Processing, preserving, and improving 
food products. 
9. Sustainable and Organic Farming – Eco-friendly practices to protect the 
environment. 
10. Plant Breeding - Developing new crop varieties to increase yield, re-
sistance to pests, or environmental tolerance. 
11. Pest and Disease Management - Research into controlling pests, dis-
eases, and weeds in crops and livestock. 
These areas are interconnected, and each contributes to the overall efficiency, sus-
tainability, and success of agriculture worldwide.

  1.1.2 
Agripreneurship 
 
Agripreneurship is simply entrepreneurship in agriculture. An Agripreneur is some-
one who identifies opportunities in agriculture and agribusiness and uses entrepre-
neurial skills to develop innovative solutions, manage risks, and build profitable, 
sustainable agricultural ventures. 
Agripreneurship focuses not only on traditional farming but also on the many as-
pects of the agricultural value chain, including food processing, distribution, tech-
nology, and services. It involves finding creative ways to add value to agricultural 
products or services, thereby making the agricultural sector more efficient, sustain-
able, and commercially viable. 
Key Components of Agripreneurship 
Innovation in Agriculture: Agripreneurs often seek new technologies, methods, or 
business models to improve agricultural productivity, efficiency, and sustainability. 
This could involve using smart farming technologies, such as drones or sensors, to 
optimise crop production or develop innovative agricultural products. 
Value Addition: Agripreneurs focus on transforming raw agricultural products into 
value-added goods. For example, a simple fruit like a mango can be processed into 
products like dried mango, mango jam, or mango juice, adding more value to the 
original fruit. 
Sustainability: A key aspect of agripreneurship is adopting practices that are not 
only economically viable but also environmentally sustainable. This includes organic 
farming, water-efficient irrigation, waste reduction, and the development of eco-
friendly packaging solutions.

  2.1.1 
What is Fish Farming? 
 
AQUACULTURE: It is the controlled cultivation/rearing of aquatic plants and animals 
in a water environment for commercial, recreational, and scientific purposes. 
Fish farming is a branch of Aquaculture (known as Pisciculture) that deals with the 
science and art of rearing fish. It can also be the breeding, rearing, and harvesting 
of fish in controlled environments such as ponds, tanks, or cages in rivers and 
oceans. 
Typically, there are different departments in a fish farm, namely: 
 Hatchery (labour room): production of baby fishes (fingerlings/juveniles).  
 Grow-out: rearing of fingerlings/juveniles (baby fish) to mature, market-
able adult sizes. 
 Processing: adding value to the adult fish. 
 Feed mill (kitchen): production of fish feed (food) for the fish. 
 Administrative; running the day-to-day operation of the farm. 
 
  2.1.2 
Importance of Fish Farming 
 
The principal aim is to produce high-quality fish food for human consumption, with 
emphasis on a high protein and low cholesterol content. The cholesterol-related fat 
in fish is primarily Omega-3 fatty acids. These are healthy unsaturated fats that 
promote heart health. 
Unlike saturated fats found in red meat, fish fats are beneficial and play a crucial 
role in brain function, reducing inflammation, and supporting overall cardiovascular 
health. 
 It prevents extinction of fish species by making fish seed (fingerling /ju-
veniles) available all year round, especially where there is over-exploita-
tion and unfavourable climatic conditions. 
 Fish farming generates revenue by creating jobs for both local and inter-
national markets 
 New discoveries are known through fish culture research  
 Fish culture provides recreational activities.

  2.1.4 
Water Quality and Aquarium Management  
 
Water quality in fish farming refers to the optimal physical, chemical, and biological 
parameters of water that influence fish health, growth, and overall productivity. In 
simple terms, it can be said to be the standard of water that will make the fishes to 
thrive or live a happy life.  It is important to understand that the entire aquatic en-
vironment is an ecosystem that comprises the fish and other living organisms, plants 
and animals. The aquatic habitat is the environment in which the fish live; therefore, 
maintaining optimal water conditions is crucial for preventing diseases, ensuring 
efficient growth, and maximising yields. 
Poor water quality leads to stress, disease outbreaks, slow growth, and high mortal-
ity rates. 
Good water quality ensures healthy fish, high productivity, and profitability. 
Water quality management is one of the core areas to master for a successful fish 
farming business. 
Now, we are going to look at a few specific examples of these three parameters. 
1. PHYSICAL PARAMETER 
a) Temperature: This is the degree of hotness or coldness of the water. Key 
facts about Temperature are: 
 It affects fish metabolism, feeding, breeding and growth rates. 
 Warmer water holds less oxygen: As temperature increases, the solubility 
of oxygen in water decreases. This means that even if oxygen is being 

===== PAGE 16 =====
 
ENT221| Agripreneurship 
 
14 
produced (e.g., through photosynthesis), the water cannot hold as much 
of it. 
 Optimal temperature varies by species (e.g., tilapia thrives at 25–30°C 
b) Turbidity (Water Clarity): This has to do with the clarity of the water. 
 Excess turbidity (due to suspended particles or algae) can block sunlight 
and reduce oxygen production. Excess phytoplankton in a pond can 
cause major changes in oxygen levels, affecting fish differently during 
the day and at night. 
 During the day, dissolved oxygen increases because of photosynthesis, 
which benefits the fish. However, algae blooms can block sunlight from 
reaching deeper parts of the pond, affecting submerged plants and other 
organisms. 
 At night, oxygen decreases because of the lack of photosynthesis; both 
the phytoplankton and fish respire, consuming oxygen. If the oxygen 
level is low (Hypoxia), the fish may become stressed, come to the surface 
gasping for air, or even die in severe cases. Long-term effects are eu-
trophication and Toxin production by some phytoplankton species, like 
blue-green algae, which produce toxic materials harmful to fish and 
other aquatic life. 
 Ideal levels depend on species but should allow sufficient light penetra-
tion. 
2. CHEMICAL PARAMETER 
a) pH (Acidity/Alkalinity Level) 
 Ideal range: 6.5–8.5 for most fish. 
 Extreme pH levels cause stress, poor growth, and even death. 
 Correction can be rectified by the addition of synthetic inorganic chem-
icals. 
b) Dissolved Oxygen (DO) 
 Essential for fish respiration and overall health. 
 Minimum requirement: 5–8 mg/L (varies by species). 
 Low DO can lead to stress, disease, and mortality. 
 Managed by water exchange and aeration. 
c) Ammonia (NH3) and Nitrite (NO2-) 
 Produced from fish waste and uneaten feed. 
 High ammonia (>0.05 mg/L) is toxic and can cause gill damage. 
 Nitrite levels should remain below 0.1 mg/L to prevent poisoning.

15 
d) Nitrate (NO3-) 
 Less toxic but high levels (>50 mg/L) can affect fish health. 
 Controlled through water exchange and plant filtration in integrated sys-
tems. 
e) Carbon (IV) oxide (CO₂) 
 High CO₂ levels (>20 mg/L) can suffocate fish by interfering with oxygen 
uptake. 
 Managed through aeration and water exchange. 
f) Iron: This can be seen as silvery films on the surface of the water; if the water 
is left standing in a bucket for a long time, the presence of brownish sediment 
at the bottom depicts the presence of iron. 
The effect of Iron is stated below: 
Toxicity to Fish 
 Excessive iron (>0.5 mg/L) can be toxic to fish, causing stress, reduced 
growth, and gill damage. 
 Iron deposits on gills reduce oxygen absorption, leading to hypoxia (ox-
ygen deficiency). 
Water Quality Degradation 
 High iron levels promote bacterial growth, especially Iron Bacteria (Gal-
lionella, Leptothrix), which form slimy biofilms that clog gills and reduce 
oxygen availability. 
 Causes water discolouration (reddish-brown stains), reducing transpar-
ency and light penetration, which can hinder aquatic plant and algae 
growth. 
 
Impact on Fish Health 
 Long-term exposure can lead to iron toxicity, affecting metabolism and 
causing organ damage. 
 Symptoms include lethargy, loss of appetite, difficulty breathing, and ab-
normal swimming. 
Filter Clogging and System Damage 
 In Recirculating Aquaculture Systems (RAS) and ponds with aeration, iron 
oxidises and precipitates, forming rust-like deposits. 
 This can clog filters, pipes, and aeration systems, reduce their efficiency 
and increase maintenance costs.

Decreased Water pH Stability 
Iron oxidation can alter water chemistry, making pH unstable, which fur-
ther stresses fish. 
Acceptable Iron Levels for Fish Farming 
 Safe limit: < 0.1 mg/L (ideal) 
 Moderate risk: 0.1 – 0.5 mg/L 
 High risk: > 0.5 mg/L 
How to Reduce Iron in Fish Farming Water 
 Aeration & Oxidation – Expose water to air to convert soluble iron (Fe²⁺) into 
insoluble iron (Fe³⁺), which can be filtered out. 
 Filtration – Use sand filters, activated carbon, or iron-removal filters to trap 
precipitated iron. 
 Chemical Treatment – Adding lime or potassium permanganate can help re-
move excess iron. 
 Water Source Selection – Use surface water or pre-treated well water to avoid 
high iron content. 
 Regular Water Testing – Monitor iron levels using test kits or lab analysis. 
 
3. BIOLOGICAL PARAMETER 
Biological parameters refer to the living organisms present in water that can affect 
fish health, growth, and overall farm productivity. Maintaining a balanced biological 
environment is crucial for disease prevention, water quality management, and sus-
tainable fish farming. 
Key Biological Parameters in Fish Farming 
1. Plankton (Phytoplankton & Zooplankton) 
 Role: Essential for natural fish feed and oxygen production. 
 Ideal Levels: Moderate plankton blooms improve water quality, but ex-
cessive growth (eutrophication) reduces oxygen and causes fish stress. 
 Risk: Algal blooms (e.g., cyanobacteria) can produce toxins, reduce oxy-
gen at night, and cause fish kills. 
2. Microbial Load (Bacteria, Viruses, Fungi, and Protozoa) 
 Role: Beneficial bacteria (e.g., Nitrosomonas & Nitrobacter) help in am-
monia and nitrate breakdown.

 Risk: 
o Harmful bacteria (Aeromonas, Vibrio, and Pseudomonas) cause infec-
tions. 
o Viruses (Iridovirus, Koi herpesvirus) lead to high fish mortality. 
o Fungi (Saprolegnia) cause skin and gill infections. 
3. Pathogens and Parasites 
 Risk: 
o Parasitic worms & protozoa (Ichthyophthirius, Gyrodactylus) weaken 
fish. 
o High organic matter increases the risk of disease outbreaks. 
 Control Measures: 
o Regular pond cleaning and water exchange. 
o Use of probiotics and biosecurity measures. 
o Proper stocking density to reduce stress. 
4. Organic Matter & Decomposers 
 Role: Natural decomposition helps recycle nutrients. 
 Risk: Excess fish waste, uneaten feed, and dead plants lead to oxygen de-

 Control Measures: 
o Avoid overfeeding. 
o Use biofilters in Recirculating Aquaculture Systems (RAS). 
o Maintain aeration to enhance decomposition. 
5. Macrophytes (Aquatic Plants & Weeds) 
 Role: Provide shelter and oxygen. 
 Risk: Overgrowth competes with fish for oxygen and harbours predators. 
 Control Measures: Manual removal, biological control (grass carp), or 
herbicides (if needed). 
  
Importance of Biological Parameters 
 Maintain water quality and ecological balance. 
 Reduce disease outbreaks and fish mortality. 
 Enhance natural feed availability and farm productivity.

  2.1.5 
Aquarium Management 
 
An Aquarium is a “beauty to behold”, looking at the air bubbles coming out of the 
air pump, different colourful ornamental fish, and decorations. 
Managing an aquarium requires consistent care and attention to maintain a healthy 
environment for your fish and plants. 
These few steps will guide you to manage an Aquarium effectively: 
1. Setting up the Aquarium 
 Substrate & Decorations – Use gravel, sand, or a planted substrate and 
include decorations for fish to hide and explore. 
 Condition the Water – Dechlorinate tap water and ensure proper tem-
perature before adding fish. 
 Glass Sealing-Make sure your glass is of the required thickness and 
properly sealed to avoid leakage of water. 
 Know your fish species-Do not stock two types of fish that cannot co-
habit together, where one is aggressive to the other. 
2. Daily Maintenance 
 Feed Fish Properly – Feed small amounts 1-2 times a day and avoid over-
feeding to prevent water pollution. 
 Observe Your Fish – Watch for signs of stress, disease, or unusual behav-
iour 
3. Weekly Maintenance 
 Partial Water Changes – Replace 20-30% of the water weekly to remove 
toxins. 

===== PAGE 21 =====
 
ENT221| Agripreneurship 
 
19 
 Check Water Parameters – Test for pH, ammonia, nitrites, and nitrates to 
ensure a healthy environment 
 Clean the Glass & Decorations – Use an algae scraper, salt-sterilised foam 
or magnetic cleaner to remove algae buildup. 
4. Monthly Maintenance 
 Filter Maintenance – Rinse filter media in tank water (not tap water) to 
preserve beneficial bacteria 
 Inspect Equipment – Check heaters, filters, pumps and lights to ensure 
they are functioning properly. 
5. Long-Term Care 
 Monitor Fish Health – Quarantine new fish before adding them to the 
main tank to prevent diseases 
 Trim or replace Plants – If you have live plants, trim or replace them as 
needed to maintain a balanced ecosystem.

  2.1.6 
Fish Behaviour, Causes and Remedies 
 
Fishes, like every other living organism, have unique characteristics or behaviours 
that respond to various internal and external factors. 
In other words, fish “talk” by expressing itself through their behavioural patterns.

Oil Palm Cultivation 
 
Oil palm is a tropical crop that thrives in lowland regions with high rainfall, abundant 
sunlight, and deep, fertile, well-drained loamy soils. 
Optimal conditions for oil palm cultivation include: 
 Temperature: 25–34°C 
 Annual rainfall: 2000–2500 mm 
 Soil pH: 5.0–6.0 
Oil palm can also grow in seasonally wet or dry areas, although yields may be lower 
under such conditions. 
The key stages of oil palm cultivation include: 
 Propagation 
 Nursery management 
 Field establishment 
 Agronomic practices 
 Harvesting 
 
Propagation  
Oil palm is commonly propagated through seeds. In research and specialised agri-
cultural practices, vegetative propagation through tissue culture may also be used. 
The Dura and Pisifera varieties are crossed to produce the hybrid Tenera variety, 
which is the most commercially desirable type due to its higher oil yield. To break 
seed dormancy, oil palm seeds are treated at 40°C for approximately 70–80 days 
before planting. 
 
Nursery Management 
Oil palm seedlings are raised in black polythene bags for about 12–15 months be-
fore they are transplanted to the field. 
The polythene bags are perforated at the bottom to allow excess water to drain out. 
An ideal nursery site should: 
 Be located near a reliable source of water 

===== PAGE 39 =====
 
ENT221| Agripreneurship 
 
37 
 Be easily accessible to both workers and vehicles 
 Be relatively close to the field where the seedlings will be planted 
Nursery establishment should begin during the early rainy season. 
Important nursery practices include: 
 Manual weeding of polythene bags 
 Application of fertilisers at a rate of 56 g per seedling bag 
 Mulching to conserve moisture and suppress weeds 
 Shading during the dry season to reduce water stress 
 Application of insecticides to control pests. 
 
Field Establishment 
Proper site selection is essential for successful oil palm cultivation. Suitable land 
should have flat, rolling, or gently sloping terrain. 
Land preparation involves: 
 Clearing vegetation 
 Ploughing the land 
 Pegging planting positions 
The recommended spacing for oil palm is 9 m × 9 m in a triangular pattern, which 
results in approximately 143 palms per hectare. 
Planting is usually done at the beginning of the rainy season to ensure adequate 
soil moisture. 
Agronomic Practices 
Routine agronomic practices are necessary to ensure healthy growth and optimal 
yield. 
These practices include: 
 Weeding 
 Fertilizer application 
 Pruning 
 Pest and disease control 
A commonly recommended fertilizer for oil palm is NPKMg (12:12:17:2).

Oil Palm Fruit Types 
 
Fruit types refer to the external morphology of the oil palm fruit, which includes the 
following: 
 Nigrescens: These are the ordinary fruits which are black, with ivory col-
our towards the base when unripe, but turn red when ripe, with the top 
half remaining black in colour. 
 Virescens: The fruits are green in colour when unripe due to a marked 
absence of the anthocyanin pigment. They, however, change when ripe 
to light, reddish orange with greenish tips. 
 Albescens: Fruits are white in colour when unripe, due to the marked 
absence of carotenoid, but turn pale yellow when ripe. 
 Mantle or Poisson fruits: These are fruits with fleshy, oily outer growth 
that have developed from the carpel that surrounds the main part of the 
fruit. They increase the mesocarp's overall oil content. 
 Parthenocarpic Fruits: These are fruits with no seeds.

  3.2.1 
Processing of Palm Fruits 
 
The operations involved in the processing of palm fruits to palm oil and palm kernel 
are: 
a. Sterilisation: Once the Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFBs) are harvested, they are trans-
ported to the mill where they undergo sterilisation. Sterilisation of palm fruits or 
bunches before milling for the purpose of oil extraction is achieved by boiling. 
The purpose of this is to: 
i. Disinfects the fruits by killing the pathogens and inactivating the enzymes 
that could degrade the oil.  
ii. Loosening the fruits from the bunches, making it easier to separate them. 
iii. Reduce microbial contamination, ensuring better oil quality. 
Sterilization Can be carried out in pots, drums or sterilisation chambers as done 
in mills, using steam under pressure (around 140 -150°C for 60 -90 minutes). This 
process is crucial in controlling the oil’s quality by minimising the formation of 
free fatty acids. 
b. Threshing: This is the removal of fruits from sterilized bunches stalks. Fruits di-
gest easily when they are hot and well- sterilized. 
c. Digesting (milling of fruits): This is the milling of sterilised fruits in a digester to 
break down the cell walls to separate the mesocarp from the kernel (de-pulping). 
The process is complete when no streak of coloured outer skin remains distin-
guishable. 
d. Pressing or Extraction: The digested fruit is then subjected to mechanical press-
ing. The oil is extracted from the fruit pulp(mesocarp) through a series of screw 
presses, hydraulic presses, or centrifugal presses. These presses apply high pres-
sure to release crude palm oil (CPO). 
e. Clarification: After pressing, the crude palm oil is mixed with water and heated 
to separate solids, such as fibers and palm oil sludge, from the oil. This is 
achieved by boiling and skimming. The clarified oil after cooling is stored in con-
tainers for proper packaging and marketing

Factors Affecting Oil Quality 
 
Several factors influence the quality of crude palm oil, including: 
 Harvesting time: Overripe or under-ripe fruits lead to poor oil quality

 Sterilisation time and temperature: Over or under-steaming can affect oil 
yield and quality. 
 Pressing conditions: High- pressure pressing is key to extracting maxi-
mum oil without damaging the oil's quality. 
 Storage and handling: Proper storage of the oil after extraction prevents 
oxidation and spoilage.

By-products of Oil Palm Processing 
 
Oil palm processing generates several by-products, which are often used for other 
applications or as part of waste management strategies: 
a) Empty Fruit Bunches (EFB): These are the bunches that remain after the 
fruit is stripped. EFB can be used as fertiliser or in the production of bio-

Effective management and recycling of these by-products are essential for reducing

Agribusiness Opportunity 
 
An agribusiness opportunity is a potentially profitable business idea within the ag-
ricultural sector. It may involve production, processing, distribution, marketing, or 
service provision related to agriculture. The criteria for evaluating a successful agri-
business are market demand, cost of inputs, profit margin, Scalability, environmen-
tal and social impact, policy and regulatory compliance. 
 Market Demand: The extent to which there is a consistent and growing need 
for the product or service in the target market. It is the level of consumer 
interest and willingness to purchase agricultural products or services at a 
given price over a specific period. 
 Cost of Inputs: These are expenses incurred for all the resources needed to 
produce agricultural goods. These inputs are essential for farm operations 
and agribusiness enterprises, and their costs have a significant impact on 
profitability, productivity and competitiveness. Some examples of input 
costs are seeds, fertilisers, pesticides and herbicides, labour costs, machinery 
and equipment, transportation, energy and fuel consumption, etc. 
 Profit Margin: The difference between production costs and sales revenue, 
indicating the financial viability of the business. It is the percentage of reve-
nue that remains as profit after all costs and expenses (particularly input 
costs) are deducted. 
 Scalability: The potential for an agricultural enterprise to grow in size, out-
put, revenue or market reach and expand its operations, markets, or product 
lines efficiently without a proportional increase in costs or a drop in effi-
ciency or quality. 
 Environmental and Social Impact: The sustainability of the business in terms 
of environmental stewardship and its contribution to the local community 
and society at large. 
 Policy and Regulatory Compliance: Adherence to local, national, and inter-
national laws, standards and policies and regulations governing agricultural 
practices, trade, land use, labour, environment and public health. Compli-
ance is not optional; it is a critical part of risk management, market access, 
and business sustainability.

Value Addition in Agriculture 
Value addition is the process of changing or transforming a product from its 
original state to a more valuable state, often by increasing shelf life, enhancing 
quality, or creating new uses. Value addition is also the process of increasing the 
economic value and consumer appeal of an agricultural commodity.  
Value addition in agribusiness refers to the process of increasing the economic 
value and consumer appeal of an agricultural commodity by altering its current 
place, time, or form. Examples are milling wheat into flour, processing fresh to-
matoes into paste or ketchup, and packaging and branding of fruits.  
Importance of Value Addition 
a. It leads to job creation, such as processing, packaging, and logistics in 
rural areas. 
b. It reduces perishability and wastage 
c. It allows for off- season sales through preservation 
d. It serves as a source of empowerment for women and youth. 
e. It allows access to premium markets 
f. It brings about a more stable income through product diversification. 
g. It creates a higher profit margin than raw produce 
Types of Value Addition in Agribusiness 
Value addition in agribusiness can be achieved in several ways depending on the 
stage of the supply chain, market demand and available technology. Some of the 
types of value addition are: 
a. FORM VALUE ADDITION: This is the process of changing the physical 
form or structure of a product to make it more useful and convenient. 
Examples are turning milk into cheese, yogurt e.t.c, milling wheat into 
flour, processing tomatoes into ketchup or puree. 
b. PLACE VALUE ADDITION: This is the process of making the product 
available at a location where it is more valuable or of easy access to the 
consumer. Examples are transporting fresh produce from rural farms to 
urban supermarkets, establishing roadside farm outlets or local farmer 
markets. 
c. TIME VALUE ADDITION: This is the process of storing produce to make 
it available at a time when it will be scarce or more in demand. Examples 
are cold storage of seasonal fruits to sell off-season, grain storage to 

===== PAGE 64 =====

avoid distress sales during harvest glut, and processing and canning 
foods for year-round availability. 
d. CONVENIENCE VALUE ADDITION: This is the process of making the 
product easier to use, cook or consume. Examples are ready-to-eat or 
ready-to-cook meals, pre-cut and packaged vegetables, instant mixes 
such as puff-puff mix, mosa mix, spice mixes. 
e. PACKAGING AND BRANDING VALUE ADDITION: This is the process 
of improving presentation and creating a distinct market identity for a 
product. Examples are: vacuum sealed packaging to enhance shelf life, 
eco-friendly or resealable packaging for convenience. 
f. NUTRITIONAL VALUE ADDITION: This is the process of enhancing the 
health or nutritional benefits of a product. Examples are fortified cereals 
with vitamins and minerals, organic or chemical-free food products, glu-
ten-free or low-fat product variants  
Forms of Value Addition 
a. PRIMARY FORM: This is a form of value addition that involves simple 
processes carried out at the farm level. Examples are cleaning, sorting, 
and grading. 
b. SECONDARY FORM: This is the second form of value addition that in-

c. TERTIARY FORM: This is another form of value addition that involves ad-
vanced processing and branding. Examples are packaging, labelling and 
retailing. 
Value Addition Process

 Processing: includes cleaning, drying, milling or cooking 
 Packaging: must be attractive, informative and safe for storage.

Production and Operations Management 
 
Production in agribusiness is any activity related to growing crops and rearing ani-
mals, while operations deal mainly with activities that include planning, input man-
agement, processing, logistics, and quality control. 
Therefore, production and operations management in agribusiness refers to the 
planning, implementation, and control of all activities involved in the transformation 
of agricultural inputs into valuable outputs like crops, livestock products, and pro-
cessed foods. It ensures efficiency, sustainability, and profitability across the value 
chain. In agribusiness, production and operations management ensure that re-
sources (land, labour, capital, inputs) are used optimally to produce high-quality 
agricultural products efficiently and sustainably. 
COMPONENTS OF PRODUCTION AND OPERATIONS MANAGEMENT 
a. Production Planning: crop and livestock are selected based on market de-
mand, climate, soil type and resource availability. Resources such as land, 
labour, capital, equipment and inputs (fertilisers and seeds) are allocated, 
aligning production with optimal planting/ harvesting or breeding periods. 
b. Operations scheduling and control: irrigation, feeding, harvesting, pest con-
trol are weekly and daily operations ensuring consistency in crop/ livestock 
health and yield. 
c. Supply chain and Logistics: Post-harvest handling, such as sorting, cleaning, 
packing and storage, is essential for perishables to maintain quality during 
transportation to markets, processors or end consumers. 
d. Technology and innovation: sustainable practices such as conservation till-
age, irrigation, and integrated pest management 
e. with the use of precision agriculture (GPS, drones, IoT) are used for real-time 
decision making, record keeping, planning, and performance analysis. 
f. Cost control and Efficiency: Measuring yields, input & output ratios, and cost 
per unit helps to minimise waste and optimise input use. 
g. Compliance and Standards: This is adhering to local and international food 
safety standards and regulations.

Mesocarp: The fleshy middle layer of the oil 
palm fruit, from which palm oil is extracted. 
Palm Oil: Oil extracted from the mesocarp of 
the oil palm fruit, widely used in cooking, cos-
metics and industrial products. 
Palm Kernel Oil: Oil obtained from the seed 
(kernel) of the oil palm fruit, used in baking, 
soap production and cosmetics. 
Palm Kernel Cake: A solid by- product left after 
oil extraction from palm kernels, used as live-
stock feed and organic fertilizer. 
Palm Pressed Fiber: Fibrous material remaining 
after oil extraction from the fruit pulp, used as 
biofuel and animal bedding. 
Empty Fruit Bunches: The remains of fruit 
bunches after the fruits are removed, used for 
compost, bioenergy, or biodegradable materi-
als. 
Tenera: A hybrid oil palm variety derived from 
Dura and Pisifera, known for high oil yield and 
thin shell; preferred for farming. 
Dura: Oil palm variety with thick shell and lower 
oil yield.  
Pisifera: A shell-less or thin-shelled oil palm va-
riety with poor fruit development; used in 
breeding programs. 
Intercropping: Growing other crops like maize 
or cassava alongside young oil palm trees to 
maximize land use and maintain fertility. 
Pruning: The removal of dead or excess fronds 
(leaves) to encourage healthy growth and easier 
harvesting of oil palms. 
Harvesting Hook: A curved tool used to harvest 
oil palm fruit bunches, especially from tall trees. 
Chisel: A flat-bladed tool used for harvesting 
fruit bunches from shorter oil palm trees. 
Mulching: Covering the soil surface with materi-
als like dry leaves to conserve moisture, sup-
press weeds, and reduce erosion. 
Parthenocarpic Fruit: Fruits that form without 
fertilization and thus contain no seeds. 
Anthocyanin: A pigment responsible for the 
purple/black coloration in some oil palm fruit 
types like Nigrescens. 
Biogas: A renewable energy source produced 
from the decomposition of organic waste like 
palm oil mill effluent. 
Soil pH: A measure of soil acidity or alkalinity; 
oil palm grows best in slightly acidic soils (pH 
5.0 – 6.0). 
Triangular Spacing: A planting method in oil 
palm cultivation where each tree is planted at 
equal distance forming an equilateral triangle 
Sterilization: The process of steaming or boiling 
fresh fruit bunches to kill   pathogens, loosen 
fruits from the bunch, and improve oil quality. 
Threshing: The removal of fruits from sterilized 
bunch stalks. 
Digesting: The mechanical process of breaking 
down the fruit to separate the mesocarp from 
the kernel. 
Clarification: A process that involves heating 
and skimming to separate oil from water, solids, 
and impurities. 
Fibre Seperator: A machine that removes fiber 
residues from the palm

Free Fatty Acids: substances that form when oil 
degrades, indicating poor quality if levels are 
high. 
Jute Bags: Sacks made of coarse fiber used for 
storing dried palm kernels. 
Biofuel: Renewable energy source produced 
from organic matter, including oil palm by-prod-
ucts like EFB and PPF. 
Agribusiness: The business sector encompass-
ing farming and related activities including seed 
supply, agrochemicals, machinery, processing 
and marketing. 
Agribusiness Innovation: The use of new ideas, 
methods, or technologies to improve agricul-
tural productivity, efficiency, and competitive-
ness. 
Scalability: The ability of a business to expand 
in size, output or market reach without propor-
tional increases in cost or loss in quality. 
Market Demand: The level of consumer interest 
and need for a product or service at a particular 
time and price. 
Agricultural Value Chain: The full range of activ-
ities involved in moving a product from the farm 
to the consumer, adding value at each stage. 
Supply Chain: the entire process involved in 
handling, transporting, and delivering agricul-
tural products from farm to market or end user. 
Precision Agriculture: A farming management 
concept that uses GPS, sensors and data analyt-
ics to optimize field level management of crops 
and inputs. 
IoT (Internet of Things): Interconnected devices 
that collect and share data such as sensors for 
monitoring soil moisture and temperature in 
real time. 
Infrastructure: Basic physical systems such as 
roads, power supply, and logistics that support 
value addition and distribution. 
Farm Management Software: Digital tools that 
help farmers keep records, plan activities, ana-
lyse performance, and make informed deci-
sions. 
Post-Harvest Losses: Reduction in quantity or 
quality of agricultural products after harvesting, 
often due to poor storage or handling. 
Cost Control: the practice of managing and re-
ducing operating expenses in agribusiness to in-
crease profitability.
