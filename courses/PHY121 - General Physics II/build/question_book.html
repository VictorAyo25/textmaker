1.3easyReference sheet R.4

What distinguishes a conductor from an insulator?

  • ✓In a conductor charge moves freely; in an insulator charge is bound and cannot move freelyCorrect. Mobility, not quantity.
  • ✗A conductor has charge and an insulator has noneWrong. Both are full of charge; an insulator's simply cannot travel.
  • ✗A conductor is always metal and an insulator is always plasticWrong. Those are examples, not the definition.
  • ✗A conductor stores charge and an insulator releases itWrong. That describes a capacitor, and inaccurately.
  • ✗An insulator has no electronsWrong. Every atom has electrons, bound ones included.

Whether the charge can MOVE. Both have plenty of charge in them; the difference is mobility. It is why a conductor has no interior field, the free charges rearranging until it cancels, and why an insulator can instead polarise, its bound charges shifting slightly to form dipoles without ever leaving home.

1.8easyReference sheet R.1

Which statement is the starting point of all electrostatics?

  • ✓Like charges repel and unlike charges attractCorrect. Opposites attract; the same signs push apart.
  • ✗Like charges attract and unlike charges repelWrong, and exactly reversed.

1.26mediumReference sheet R.1

Coulomb’s law is an inverse square law. If the distance between two charges is TRIPLED, what happens to the force?

  • ✓It falls to one ninthCorrect. Three squared is nine, so the force drops to a ninth.
  • ✗It falls to one thirdWrong, and it is the trap: falling to a third is what the POTENTIAL does, because V goes as 1/r rather than 1/r2.
  • ✗It falls to one sixthWrong. No inverse power gives a sixth for a tripling.
  • ✗It becomes three times largerWrong direction. Moving charges apart weakens the force.
  • ✗It is unchangedWrong. Distance is in the formula, so it certainly changes.

F = k q1 q2 / r2, so the force depends on the SQUARE of the distance. Tripling r multiplies r2 by 9, so the force falls to one ninth. The same reasoning applies to the electric field E = kQ/r2, but NOT to the potential V = kQ/r, which would fall only to one third.

1.27mediumReference sheet R.2

1.28mediumReference sheet R.1

What does the principle of superposition tell you to do, and what does it apply to?

  • ✓Compute each contribution one at a time and then add them, which works for forces, fields and potentialsCorrect, and the fact that it covers all three is what makes it so useful.
  • ✗Compute all contributions at once, which works only for forcesWrong on both counts: the method is one at a time, and it is not restricted to forces.
  • ✗Add the charges first and then apply one formula, which works only for fieldsWrong. Adding the charges first only works in special symmetric cases and is not the principle.
  • ✗Take only the largest contribution and ignore the restWrong. Every contribution counts.
  • ✗Multiply the individual contributions togetherWrong. Contributions add, they do not multiply.

One at a time, then add. Superposition is what lets a many-charge problem be broken into a series of two-charge problems, and it holds for forces, for fields and for potentials alike. Remember that forces and fields are VECTORS, so they add as vectors, while potentials are scalars and add as plain numbers.

  • ✓1.26 x 105 N, directed toward the 1 C chargeCorrect. 1.26 x 105 N, and attraction because the signs are opposite.

1.39mediumLecture slides, module 1, slide 64

If F be the electrostatic force between two charges in air, then the force between the charges when air is replaced by a medium of dielectric constant K is:

  • ✓F/KCorrect. The dielectric screens the charges from each other, so the force falls, and it falls by exactly K because ε sits on the bottom of Coulomb's law.
  • ✗F/√KWrong. The direction is right, the force does fall, but nothing here takes a square root. K divides the force once, not by √K.
  • ✗√K FWrong twice: it makes the force BIGGER, and it uses a square root that appears nowhere in Coulomb's law.
  • ✗KFWrong, and it is the trap. K multiplying the force is what happens to CAPACITANCE, where ε sits on top. Here it sits underneath, so K divides instead.
  • ✗F, unchangedWrong. Something physical changed in the gap: the molecules polarise and screen the charges, so the force cannot be unaffected.

Picture it first, then the formula. Two charges sit in air, a certain distance apart, pulling on each other with force F. Now slide a slab of glass or plastic into the gap between them. Nothing else changes: same charges, same distance. The slab is made of molecules, and each molecule has a positive end and a negative end. Put them near a charge and they all TURN, lining up with their opposite ends facing it. That is called POLARISATION, and the effect is a thin layer of opposite charge sitting between the two originals, partly hiding each one from the other. Each charge now sees less of its partner than it did through air. So the force goes DOWN. That alone rules out three of the five options. By how much? By exactly K, the dielectric constant of the slab. F becomes F/K. Glass has K around 5, so the force drops to about a fifth. The formula agrees, and here is the one line that makes it obvious. In F =q₁q₂4πεr², the permittivity ε sits UNDERNEATH. Adding the slab multiplies ε by K. Multiplying the bottom of a fraction by K divides the whole thing by K. So F becomes F/K. The contrast worth carrying: in capacitance, C = εA/d, that same ε sits ON TOP, so the same slab MULTIPLIES the capacitance by K instead. Same material, same K, opposite effect, purely because of which side of the fraction ε is on.

Speed tip. In a vacuum everything is c = 3 × 10⁸. E/B = c for a plane wave, and c = fλ ties frequency to wavelength.

A radio transmitter produces an electromagnetic wave with an electric field amplitude of 0.080 V/m. What is the corresponding magnetic field amplitude?

  • ✓2.67 x 10-10 TCorrect. 0.080 divided by 3.0 x 108.
  • ✗2.4 x 107 TWrong. This MULTIPLIES by c instead of dividing, which is the commonest slip here.
  • ✗2.67 x 10-8 TWrong by a factor of 100 in the exponent.
  • ✗0.080 TWrong. That is the electric amplitude repeated, in the wrong unit.
  • ✗3.0 x 108 TWrong. That is the speed of light itself, labelled as a field.

In an electromagnetic wave the two fields are locked together by E = cB, so B = E over c = 0.080 / 3.0 x 108 = 2.67 x 10-10 T. The magnetic amplitude always comes out tiny beside the electric one, by a factor of the speed of light, which is why radio work quotes E rather than B.

A laser pointer emits 5.0 mW of power in a beam with cross-sectional area 3.0 x 10-6 m². Calculate the intensity.

  • ✓1667 W/m2Correct. 5.0 x 10-3 divided by 3.0 x 10-6.
  • ✗1.5 x 10-8 W/m2Wrong. This MULTIPLIES the power by the area instead of dividing.

Intensity is power per unit area: I = P over A = 5.0 x 10-3 / 3.0 x 10-6 = 1667 W/m². The interpretation the slide draws is worth keeping: despite a tiny power of only 5 mW, this beats the roughly 1000 W per square metre of full sunlight, because the energy is concentrated into a very small area. That is why a laser pointer is dangerous to the eye and a 5 mW lamp is not.

11.4hardLecture slides, module 5, slide 40

In an electromagnetic wave the electric and magnetic contributions to the energy density are equal.

Answer: true

True, and it follows from E = cB. The energy density is u = one half ε₀ E² plus B² over 2 μ₀. Substituting B = E/c and c = 1 over the square root of μ₀ ε₀ makes the second term identical to the first, so the energy is shared exactly evenly between the two fields and u = ε₀ E² = B² over μ₀. The magnetic amplitude LOOKS tiny beside the electric one, but it carries just as much energy.

In Ey = E-nought sin(kx − omega t), the wave number is k = 2 π over {{1}} and the angular frequency is omega = 2 π f.

Answer:

λ, the wavelength. Both symbols carry the same 2 π: k packs 2 π into one wavelength of SPACE, omega packs 2 π into one period of TIME. Ey and Bz share the identical sin(kx − omega t), which is why the two fields peak together and vanish together.

11.6hardLecture slides, module 5, slide 33

In the plane wave Ey = E-nought sin(kx − omega t) and Bz = B-nought sin(kx − omega t), the electric field points along y, the magnetic field along z, and the wave travels along x.

Answer: true

True, and the three directions are mutually perpendicular by construction. The subscript on each field names its own direction, and the x inside the bracket names the direction of travel. Hold your thumb, first finger and second finger at right angles: E, B and the direction of travel, never two of them along the same line.

11.7hardLecture slides, module 5, slide 42

The intensity of an electromagnetic wave, I = one half ε₀ c E-nought squared, is measured in watts per square {{1}}.

Answer:

Watts per square metre: power spread over area. The half is there because the field oscillates, so the AVERAGE of the squared sine over a cycle is one half, not one. The same one half turns up in one half C V² and one half L I², always for the same reason.

11.8hardLecture slides, module 5, slide 42

Doubling the peak electric field of a wave doubles its intensity.

Answer: false

It QUADRUPLES it. I = one half ε₀ c E-nought squared, and the field is squared, so twice the field gives four times the intensity. That is why a lamp twice as bright does not feel twice as fierce, and why halving the field cuts the power to a quarter.

In free space Maxwell's equations give a wave equation whose speed is v =1the square root of μ₀ ε₀. Evaluating it with μ₀ = 4 π x 10-7 and ε₀ = 8.85 x 10-12 gives:
