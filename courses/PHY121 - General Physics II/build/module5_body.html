Foundations F.10

Rate of change, and the flux that changes

Module 5 rests on one new idea: a quantity that changes in time can do the same job as a current. To read it, you only need to be comfortable with what "rate of change" means. You already met this in F.7 and F.8; here it is, aimed straight at Maxwell.

Teach · What d/dt actually asks

The symbol dΦ/dt reads "the rate at which Φ is changing, per second." It is a slope: how fast a quantity climbs or falls as the clock ticks. If a flux of 0.030 Wb falls to 0.010 Wb in 0.050 s, then

dΦ/dt = (change in Φ) / (time taken) = (0.010 − 0.030) / 0.050 = −0.40 Wb/s.

The minus sign says Φ is falling. The size of the rate, 0.40 Wb/s, is what drives the physics. This is the exact quantity inside Faraday's law, and (with E in place of B) inside Maxwell's new term.

Teach · The curly ∂ is the same idea, hedged

In the differential form of Maxwell's equations you will see ∂E/∂t instead of dE/dt. The curly ∂ is a partial derivative. It means exactly "rate of change with time" but adds one promise: hold position fixed while you differentiate. A wave depends on both where you are (x) and when it is (t); the partial symbol just says which one you are varying. For this course you may read ∂/∂t as "how fast it changes in time, at a fixed point." Nothing more is required of you.

Trap · A steady quantity has zero rate

If E or Φ is constant, then dE/dt = 0 and the whole new term vanishes. This is why the old Ampere's law worked perfectly for steady currents: nothing was changing, so Maxwell's extra piece was silently zero. The new physics only appears when something changes. Keep this in your pocket. It answers half the conceptual questions on this module.

Fill in the gap · Rate of change
  1. The quantity dΦ/dt is the at which the flux Φ changes each second.
  2. The curly symbol ∂/∂t means rate of change in time at a point.
  3. If E does not change, then dE/dt = , and Maxwell's new term contributes nothing.

Answers

1. rate.   2. fixed.   3. zero. A steady field induces nothing, the same lesson as Faraday's law.

Foundations F.11

Divergence and curl, just enough to read Maxwell

This one page is optional. The exam works almost entirely with the integral form. Read this only so that the differential form of Maxwell's equations, when you meet it, is a picture and not a wall of symbols.

Teach · Divergence asks: is this point a source?

The divergence, written ∇·E (the symbol ∇, "del", is just shorthand for these operations; you never have to compute it by hand here), measures whether field lines are being created at a point. Positive divergence means lines spray outward from there, like water from a tap, a source. Zero divergence means every line that enters also leaves. Gauss's law for electricity, ∇·E = ρ/ε₀, simply says: electric field lines are born on charge (here ρ, the Greek letter rho, is the charge density, meaning charge per unit volume). Gauss's law for magnetism, ∇·B = 0, says: magnetic field lines are never born anywhere, there are no magnetic charges, so every line is a closed loop.

Teach · Curl asks: does this point stir?

The curl, written ∇×E, measures whether the field circulates around a point, like a tiny whirlpool. Faraday's law ∇×E = −∂B/∂t says a changing magnetic field stirs the electric field into loops. The Ampere-Maxwell law ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t says both a current and a changing electric field stir the magnetic field into loops (here J, the current density, means current per unit area). That is the whole content of the differential form: divergence for the two Gauss laws, curl for the two circulation laws.

Recall and check

Which Maxwell equation is written with ∇· = 0, and what does that zero mean physically?

Answer

∇·B = 0, Gauss's law for magnetism. The zero means no point is ever a source of magnetic field lines: there are no magnetic monopoles, so field lines always close on themselves.

Unit 5.1

Maxwell's equations

Learning outcomes: identify the flaw Maxwell found in Ampere's law; define the displacement current and state the corrected Ampere-Maxwell law; state all four Maxwell equations and read their physical meaning.

Teach · You already have three and a half laws

Across Modules 1 to 4 you built four great results without ever seeing them side by side:

  • Gauss's law for electricity, which says charges make electric fields (Unit 1.4).
  • Gauss's law for magnetism, which says there are no magnetic charges, so ∮B·dA = 0 (Unit 4.2).
  • Faraday's law, a changing magnetic field makes an electric field (Unit 4.3).
  • Ampere's law, currents make magnetic fields (Unit 4.2).

Four laws, and they look complete. Maxwell's genius was to notice that the last one is broken, and that repairing it produces light. That is the entire story of this unit.

Teach · Ampere's law, as you learned it
∮ B · dl = μ₀ Ienc

Reading the symbols: the circle on the integral sign, ∮, means "add up all the way around a closed loop"; dl is one small step along that loop; and Ienc is the current enclosed by (threaded through) the loop. So the law says: add up the magnetic field around any closed loop, and you get μ₀ times the current threaded through the loop. It worked perfectly for every problem you solved: straight wires, solenoids, toroids, any steady current. So what could possibly be wrong with it?

Teach · The charging-capacitor problem

Ampere's law contains a hidden assumption: that "the current threaded through the loop" is a definite number. Take a capacitor being charged. Current I flows in the wire. Draw one Amperian loop around that wire, and now ask: which surface do I stretch across the loop to count the current?

  • Surface S₁ cuts the wire. The current I pierces it, so ∮B·dl = μ₀I ≠ 0.
  • Surface S₂ bulges out and passes between the plates. No charge crosses the gap, so Ienc = 0, and ∮B·dl = 0.

⚠ A logical contradiction

Same loop. Same law. Two different answers: μ₀I on one surface, 0 on the other. A law of physics cannot give two answers for one physical situation. Something is missing.

Teach · Maxwell's insight

Between the plates there is no moving charge, but there is a changing electric field, growing as the capacitor charges. Maxwell proposed that a changing electric field produces a magnetic field, exactly as a current does. He gave this changing-field effect a name (the displacement current) and added it to Ampere's law so that both surfaces give the same answer.

Must-memorise · The displacement current
Id = ε₀ (dΦE / dt)

Id is the displacement current, in amperes (A)
ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, the permittivity of free space
ΦE is the electric flux, the electric field multiplied by the area it passes through (ΦE = EA for a uniform field); dΦE/dt is its rate of change, in V·m/s

Memory hook: a changing E counts as a current. No charge moves, yet it does everything a current does to the magnetic field. The ε₀ is the price of admission. It converts a changing flux into an equivalent current.

Must-memorise · The Ampere-Maxwell law
∮ B · dl = μ₀ ( Ienc + ε₀ dΦE/dt )

Memory hook: old Ampere plus one new term. On surface S₁ the first term does the work (real current, changing flux zero); on surface S₂ the second term does the work (no current, but changing flux). Both surfaces now give μ₀I. The contradiction is gone.

Worked example · Displacement current in a charging capacitorSlide

The question. A capacitor with circular plates of radius 10 cm is being charged by a steady current of 2.5 A. (a) What is the displacement current between the plates? (b) At what rate is the electric field between the plates changing?

  1. Part (a). By charge conservation, whatever current flows in the wire must be continued between the plates by the displacement current. So Id = Iconduction = 2.5 A. This is the whole point of the fix: the two currents match, so the magnetic field is continuous. No calculation needed for part (a).
  2. Part (b). Convert the plate radius: r = 10 cm = 0.10 m. The plate area is A = πr² = π(0.10)² = 3.14 × 10⁻² m². The centi trap again: 10 cm = 0.10 m, and it is then squared. Get this wrong and the answer is off by a factor of 100 (or 10 000).
  3. Between parallel plates E is uniform across the area A, so the flux is simply ΦE = EA. Put this into Id = ε₀ dΦE/dt = ε₀ A (dE/dt). A is constant, so it comes outside the rate of change. Only E changes.
  4. Rearrange for dE/dt (rearrange first, substitute second, see Foundations F.2): dE/dt = Id / (ε₀ A).
  5. Substitute: dE/dt = 2.5 / [ (8.85 × 10⁻¹²)(3.14 × 10⁻²) ] = 2.5 / (2.78 × 10⁻¹³).
I_d = 2.5 A dE/dt = 9.0 × 10¹² V/(m·s)

Now redo it: I recomputed the denominator as ε₀A = 2.779 × 10⁻¹³ and the rate as 8.99 × 10¹² V/(m·s), so 9.0 × 10¹² to two significant figures is correct. That is nine trillion volts per metre every second, an enormous rate, and exactly what is needed to make a "current" of 2.5 A out of nothing but a changing field.

Trap · The displacement current is not a flow of charge

Nothing crosses the gap between the plates: no electrons, no ions, nothing. The displacement current is not charge in motion; it is the magnetic effect of a changing electric field, dressed up in the units of a current so it can be added to Ampere's law. Yet by charge conservation its size equals the real conduction current in the wire, which is why the magnetic field joins up smoothly. Hold both facts at once: not a real current, but exactly as large as one.

Memory hook: two about sources (Gauss E, Gauss B), two about change (Faraday, Ampere-Maxwell). Only one of the four carries a term Maxwell added (the last one) and that one term is what makes light possible.

Teach · The same four laws in differential form

If you have read Foundations F.11, the differential form is just the same four statements written point-by-point instead of loop-by-loop.

#Integral formDifferential form
I∮ E · dA = Qenc/ε₀∇ · E = ρ/ε₀
II∮ B · dA = 0∇ · B = 0
III∮ E · dl = − dΦB/dt∇ × E = − ∂B/∂t

Divergence for the two Gauss laws (sources), curl for the two circulation laws (change). That is all the differential form is.

Teach · The beautiful symmetry

Look at the two "change" laws side by side, stripped to their hearts:

Faraday

A changing B creates E.

∮ E·dl = − dΦB/dt

Ampere-Maxwell

A changing E creates B.

∮ B·dl = μ₀ε₀ dΦE/dt

A changing B makes E; a changing E makes B. Each field, by changing, gives birth to the other. This mutual creation is the engine that drives an electromagnetic wave, as the next unit shows.

Recall and check

A parallel-plate capacitor is charged by a constant current of 2 A. What is the displacement current between the plates, and why?

Answer

Exactly 2 A. By charge conservation the displacement current between the plates must equal the conduction current in the wire, otherwise the magnetic field would jump discontinuously as you slid the surface across.

Unit 5.2

Electromagnetic waves

Learning outcomes: explain how Maxwell's equations predict a self-sustaining wave; state the speed of light from μ₀ and ε₀; use the plane-wave results E = cB and c = fλ; state the energy density and intensity (Poynting vector) of an EM wave.

Teach · The chain reaction

Put the two "change" laws together and something remarkable happens. A changing electric field creates a magnetic field. But that magnetic field is itself changing, so it creates an electric field. Which is changing, so it creates a magnetic field. And so on, forever, with neither field needing charges or wires to keep it alive:

They feed each other. They sustain each other. They propagate through space. That is a wave, an electromagnetic wave, and it needs no medium at all.

Teach · The speed falls out of the mathematics

In free space (no charges, no currents) Maxwell's equations combine into a wave equation for E:

∂²E/∂x² = μ₀ε₀ (∂²E/∂t²)

Compare this with the standard wave equation ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²)(∂²y/∂t²). Matching the two, the quantity μ₀ε₀ must play the role of 1/v². So the wave travels at v = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). Now put the numbers in.

Must-memorise · The speed of light
c = 1 / √(μ₀ε₀) = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s

μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H m⁻¹, the permeability of free space
ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, the permittivity of free space

Memory hook: two constants you measured with capacitors and coils (nothing about light) combine to give the speed of light exactly. That is the moment electricity, magnetism and optics became one subject.

Worked example · Computing the speed of lightSlide

The question. Evaluate c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) from the constants alone.

  1. Form the product μ₀ε₀ = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.112 × 10⁻¹⁷. 4π ≈ 12.566, so 12.566 × 8.85 ≈ 111.2; and 10⁻⁷ × 10⁻¹² = 10⁻¹⁹. Together 111.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.112 × 10⁻¹⁷.
  2. Take the square root: √(1.112 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 3.335 × 10⁻⁹.
  3. Invert: c = 1 / (3.335 × 10⁻⁹) = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s.
c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s, the measured speed of light exactly

Now redo it: I recomputed 1/√(μ₀ε₀) independently and obtained 2.9986 × 10⁸ m/s, so 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s is right to two figures. Maxwell wrote in 1865 that light "consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena." He predicted EM waves from pure mathematics; Hertz confirmed them in 1887, eight years after Maxwell's death.

Must-memorise · The plane-wave solution
Ey = E₀ sin(kx − ωt)    Bz = B₀ sin(kx − ωt)

E₀, B₀ are the field amplitudes
k = 2π/λ is the wave number (rad/m)
ω = 2πf is the angular frequency (rad/s)
λ = wavelength, f = frequency

Memory hook: E and B share the same sine: same k, same ω, same phase. They peak together and vanish together. One wave, two fields locked in step.

Must-memorise · The two locking relations
E = cB   and   c = fλ

Memory hook: at every instant the field magnitudes are tied by E = cB, so E is always the much bigger number (c is huge). And speed = frequency × wavelength ties how fast it wiggles to how long each wave is. These two one-liners answer most numerical questions on this unit.

Teach · What the wave looks like

Three facts to read straight off the picture: E and B are perpendicular to each other; both are perpendicular to the direction of travel (the wave is transverse); and they are in phase, rising and falling together.

Worked example · From E₀ to B₀Slide

The question. A radio transmitter produces an electromagnetic wave with an electric field amplitude of 0.080 V/m. What is the corresponding magnetic field amplitude?

  1. Use the locking relation E₀ = cB₀, and rearrange for B₀ = E₀ / c. Rearrange first: divide both sides by c. Only then substitute.
  2. Substitute: B₀ = 0.080 / (3.0 × 10⁸).
B_0 = 2.67 × 10⁻¹⁰ T

Now redo it: 0.080 / (3.0 × 10⁸) = 2.667 × 10⁻¹⁰ T, confirming the slide. Notice how tiny B is compared with E. That factor of c between them is exactly why we usually notice the electric part of light and rarely the magnetic part.

Teach · The electromagnetic spectrum

Radio, microwave, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are the same physical wave. They differ only in frequency (and therefore wavelength, since c = fλ). All of them travel at c in vacuum.

Higher frequency means shorter wavelength and more energy per photon, which is why gamma rays are dangerous and radio waves are not, though both are "light."

Must-memorise · Energy density
u = ½ε₀E² + B²/(2μ₀)   →   u = ε₀E² = B²/μ₀

Memory hook: the electric and magnetic halves are always equal (because E = cB and c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀)), so the total is just twice either one. You may quote whichever single form is handy.

Must-memorise · Intensity and the Poynting vector
{{SVG:poynting}}

The Poynting vector gives the rate of energy flow per unit area, pointing along the direction of travel:

S = (1/μ₀) E × B

The symbol × is the vector cross product: E × B is a new vector pointing perpendicular to both E and B, which is exactly the direction the wave carries its energy.

The average power per unit area (the intensity) of a sinusoidal wave is

I = ½ε₀cE₀² = cB₀²/(2μ₀) = E₀²/(2μ₀c)

Memory hook: S points where the energy goes, the same way the wave moves. The factor of ½ is the average of sin², exactly as the ½ in ½CV² came from a quantity that climbed from zero.

Worked example · Intensity and field of a laser beamSlide

The question. A laser pointer emits 5.0 mW of power in a beam of cross-sectional area 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ m². Calculate (a) the intensity and (b) the electric field amplitude of the beam.

  1. Part (a). Intensity is power per unit area: I = P/A = (5.0 × 10⁻³) / (3.0 × 10⁻⁶). Convert first: 5.0 mW = 5.0 × 10⁻³ W. The area is already in SI.
  2. I = 1.67 × 10³ = 1667 W/m².
  3. Part (b). Rearrange I = ½ε₀cE₀² for the amplitude: E₀ = √( 2I / (ε₀c) ).
  4. Substitute: E₀ = √( 2(1667) / [(8.85 × 10⁻¹²)(3.00 × 10⁸)] ) = √( 3334 / 2.655 × 10⁻³ ).
I = 1667 W/m² E_0 ≈ 1120 V/m

Now redo it: I get I = 1666.7 W/m² and E₀ = 1120.5 V/m, confirming the slide. Despite only 5 mW of power, the intensity beats full sunlight (~1000 W/m²) because the energy is concentrated into a pinpoint area. That concentration is exactly why a low-power laser can still damage the eye.

Trap · Amplitude, average, and the factor of ½

The formula I = ½ε₀cE₀² uses the amplitude E₀ (the peak value) and carries a ½ because it is the time-average of a sin² that swings between 0 and 1. If a question gives you an rms field instead, the ½ disappears (I = ε₀c Erms²), because the averaging is already baked in. Read which field you were given before you reach for a formula.

Recall and check

An FM radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz. What is the wavelength of its wave?

Answer

Use c = fλ, so λ = c/f = (3.0 × 10⁸)/(100 × 10⁶) = 3.0 m. A three-metre wave, which is why FM aerials are about that size.

Must-memorise · Module 5 key takeaways
  1. Maxwell's equations are four equations that completely describe classical electromagnetism; the key addition was the displacement current in the Ampere-Maxwell law.
  2. The displacement current Id = ε₀ dΦE/dt is the effect of a changing electric field, not a flow of charge; it restores continuity and symmetry.
  3. EM waves arise from the mutual induction of E and B, and travel at c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum.
  4. EM waves are transverse: E, B and the direction of travel are mutually perpendicular, with E = cB at all times.
  5. The spectrum runs from radio to gamma rays, all the same wave, all at c, differing only in frequency.
