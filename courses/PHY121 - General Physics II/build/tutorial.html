Tutorial · Capacitance

Capacitor networks and energy

Four networks and two single-capacitor problems. The whole game is: collapse the network to one Ceq, then apply Q = CeqV or W = ½CeqV².

Q1 · Capacitor networkSLIDE 3

As printed. "Calculate (a) the equivalent capacitance and (b) the total charge stored when connected to a 24 V battery."

Break it down. Given C₁ = 3 µF in parallel with (C₂ = 6 µF in series with C₃ = 2 µF), across V = 24 V. Find Ceq and Q.

  1. Series pair first. For two in series, product over sum: Cs = (6 × 2)/(6 + 2) = 12/8 = 1.5 µF.
  2. C₁ is in parallel with that pair, so capacitances add: Ceq = 3 + 1.5 = 4.5 µF.
  3. Total charge from the battery: Q = CeqV = (4.5 × 10⁻⁶)(24).
C_eq = 4.5 µF Q = 108 µC

Shortcut. Two in series is "product over sum"; here 6 and 2 give 12/8 = 1.5 without touching reciprocals. Add the parallel arm (+3) to get 4.5, then Q = 4.5 × 24 = 108 µC in one line. Parallel adds, series is product-over-sum for exactly two.

Q2 · Capacitor networkSLIDE 3

As printed. The slide draws this second network but prints no question sentence and shows no solution for it. The standard ask for a drawn network is its equivalent capacitance, so that is what we find.

Break it down. Given (C₁ = 1 µF in series with C₂ = 5 µF), in parallel with C₃ = 8 µF. Find Ceq. No voltage is given, so charge and energy cannot be found; Ceq is all the figure supports.

  1. Series pair: Cs = (1 × 5)/(1 + 5) = 5/6 = 0.833 µF. Note it is below the smaller of the two, as a series result always is.
  2. In parallel with C₃: Ceq = 0.833 + 8 = 8.83 µF.
C_eq = 8.83 µF

Shortcut. A tiny capacitor in series with a big one is dominated by the tiny one: 1 in series with 5 is a hair under 1 (here 0.83). The 8 in parallel then dominates the total, giving just under 9. If your answer were above 9 or below 8, you would know it was wrong before computing a digit.

Q3 · Capacitor networkSLIDE 4

As printed. "Calculate (a) the equivalent capacitance and (b) the total charge supplied by a 48 V battery."

Break it down. Given [C₁ = 6 µF in parallel with (C₂ = 3 µF series C₃ = 6 µF)] in series with C₅ = 12 µF, across V = 48 V. (The slide labels the last capacitor C₅, not C₄; kept as printed.) Find Ceq and Q.

  1. Innermost group first. Series pair C₂, C₃: (3 × 6)/(3 + 6) = 18/9 = 2 µF.
  2. C₁ in parallel with it: 6 + 2 = 8 µF. Call this block Cp.
  3. Cp is in series with C₅ = 12: Ceq = (8 × 12)/(8 + 12) = 96/20 = 4.8 µF.
  4. Charge supplied: Q = CeqV = (4.8 × 10⁻⁶)(48).
C_eq = 4.8 µF Q = 230.4 µC

Shortcut. Always collapse the innermost group first, then treat it as one component. 3-and-6 series is product-over-sum = 2; parallel with 6 is 8; 8-and-12 series is 96/20 = 4.8. Three product-over-sum moves, no reciprocals written down.

Q4 · Capacitor network, energySLIDE 4

As printed. "Calculate (a) the equivalent capacitance and (b) the total energy stored when connected to a 30 V source."

Break it down. Given [C₁ = 4 µF in parallel with C₂ = 8 µF] in series with C₃ = 5 µF, across V = 30 V. Find Ceq and the stored energy W.

  1. Parallel pair adds: Cp = 4 + 8 = 12 µF.
  2. In series with C₃ = 5: Ceq = (12 × 5)/(12 + 5) = 60/17 = 3.53 µF.
  3. Energy stored: W = ½CeqV² = ½(3.53 × 10⁻⁶)(30)² = ½(3.53 × 10⁻⁶)(900).
C_eq = 3.53 µF W = 1.59 mJ

Shortcut. For energy, keep Ceq as the exact fraction 60/17 F and feed it straight into ½CV²: W = ½ × (60/17 × 10⁻⁶) × 900 = 27000/17 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.59 mJ. Squaring the 30 to 900 first, then one multiply, avoids rounding Ceq too early.

Q6 · Capacitance from stored energySLIDE 6

As printed. "What is the capacitance of a capacitor having a stored energy of 2.25 mJ after being charged with a 30-volt source?"

Break it down. Given W = 2.25 mJ = 2.25 × 10⁻³ J and V = 30 V. Find C. This is the same question as the CBT item in Part VIII; solve it cold.

  1. Start from the energy formula and make C the subject: W = ½CV², so C = 2W/V².
  2. Substitute: C = 2(2.25 × 10⁻³)/(30)² = (4.5 × 10⁻³)/900.
  3. Divide: 4.5/900 = 5 × 10⁻³, times 10⁻³ gives 5 × 10⁻⁶ F.
C = 5 µF

Shortcut. C = 2W/V². Double the energy (4.5 mJ), divide by 30² = 900: 4.5/900 = 5, and mJ over unity leaves microfarads. 5 µF in one breath.

Q9 · Parallel-plate capacitor with dielectricSLIDE 9

As printed. "The area of each plate of a parallel plate capacitor is 4 x 10⁻² m². If the thickness of the dielectric placed in between the plates is 10⁻³ m and the relative permittivity of the dielectric is 7, then the capacitance of the capacitor is?"

Break it down. Given A = 4 × 10⁻² m², d = 10⁻³ m, εr = 7, and ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m. Find C.

  1. With a dielectric, C = εrε₀A/d. The dielectric multiplies the vacuum value by εr.
  2. Substitute: C = (7)(8.85 × 10⁻¹²)(4 × 10⁻²)/(10⁻³).
  3. Fronts: 7 × 8.85 × 4 = 247.8. Exponents: −12 − 2 − (−3) = −11. So C = 247.8 × 10⁻¹¹.
C = 2.48 × 10⁻⁹ F = 2.48 nF

Shortcut. Group A/d first: (4 × 10⁻²)/(10⁻³) = 40. Then C = εrε₀ × 40 = 7 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 40 = 2479 × 10⁻¹² F ≈ 2.48 nF. One geometry factor times the permittivities.

Tutorial · Electrostatics

Fields, potential, flux and charge

Point-charge fields and potentials, a force balance, a vector magnitude, Gauss-style flux, and counting charge. Constants: k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² (the slides use 9.0 × 10⁹ in places; both are shown), e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Q5 · Oil drop held stationarySLIDE 5

As printed. "Calculate the electric field in which a charged oil drops of mass 8 mg and charged +9 [µ]C will be held stationary."

Break it down. Given m = 8 mg = 8 × 10⁻⁶ kg and q = +9 µC = 9 × 10⁻⁶ C (the prefix did not embed on the slide; µC is taken, matching the companion oil-drop item and the deck's other charges; if it were nC the field would be a thousand times larger, and that reading is noted below). Take g = 9.8 m/s². Find E.

  1. Held stationary means the upward electric force balances the downward weight: Fe = Fg.
  2. With Fe = qE and Fg = mg, this is qE = mg, so E = mg/q.
  3. Substitute: E = (8 × 10⁻⁶)(9.8)/(9 × 10⁻⁶) = (78.4 × 10⁻⁶)/(9 × 10⁻⁶).
E = 8.71 V/m (if q = 9 nC instead: E = 8.71 × 10³ V/m)

Shortcut. The powers of ten cancel top and bottom (both 10⁻⁶), so it is just (8 × 9.8)/9 = 78.4/9 = 8.71 V/m directly. The direction is upward, to oppose gravity on a positive charge.

Q7 · Magnitude of a field vectorSLIDE 7

As printed. "Calculate the magnitude of the electric field intensity represented by [the vector] where the components are: E_x = 6, E_y = 8, E_z = −2."

Break it down. Given the components (6, 8, −2). Find the magnitude |E|.

  1. The magnitude of a vector is the square root of the sum of the squares of its components: |E| = √(Ex² + Ey² + Ez²).
  2. Square each (the sign of Ez disappears): 6² + 8² + (−2)² = 36 + 64 + 4 = 104.
  3. Take the root: √104.
|E| = √104 = 10.2

Shortcut. Spot the 6, 8 as two legs of a 3-4-5 triangle scaled by 2: 6² + 8² = 100 already. The −2 adds only 4 more, so √104 is a touch above 10. No sign matters; you are squaring everything.

Q8 · Field near a conducting sphereSLIDE 8

As printed. "A conducting sphere of radius 0.25 m carries a total charge of 6 × 10⁻⁶ C. Calculate the electric field at a point 0.50 m from the center of the sphere."

Break it down. Given radius R = 0.25 m, Q = 6 × 10⁻⁶ C, field point at r = 0.50 m from the centre. Since r > R, the point is outside the sphere. Find E.

  1. Outside a uniformly charged sphere, the field is exactly that of a point charge at the centre: E = kQ/r². (The radius 0.25 m only matters for checking the point is outside; it is.)
  2. Substitute: E = (8.99 × 10⁹)(6 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.50)² = (8.99 × 10⁹)(6 × 10⁻⁶)/0.25.
  3. Fronts: 8.99 × 6 = 53.94, so numerator 53.94 × 10³ = 5.394 × 10⁴; divide by 0.25 (multiply by 4).
E = 2.16 × 10⁵ N/C

Shortcut. Outside a sphere, treat all its charge as sitting at the centre and use kQ/r². Dividing by 0.25 is multiplying by 4, so E = 4 × 5.394 × 10⁴ = 2.16 × 10⁵ N/C. The radius is a distractor once you have checked r > R.

Q10 · Electric fluxSLIDE 10

As printed. "A uniform electric field E = 200 N/C passes through a surface of area 2 m² at 0°. Find Flux."

Break it down. Given E = 200 N/C, A = 2 m², and the angle 0°. The angle in the flux formula is between the field and the surface normal; "at 0°" here means the field is straight through, along the normal. Find Φ.

  1. Electric flux: Φ = EA cos θ, with θ the angle to the normal.
  2. At θ = 0°, cos 0 = 1, so the flux is maximal: Φ = EA.
  3. Substitute: Φ = (200)(2)(1) = 400.
Φ = 400 N·m²/C

Shortcut. Field straight through a surface (θ = 0) means just E × A, no trig: 200 × 2 = 400. Only reach for cos θ when the field is tilted. Watch the convention: if a question gives the angle to the surface instead of the normal, use sin.

Q11 · Charge of a system of electronsSLIDE 11

As printed. "What is the charge of a system that has 6.2 x 10¹⁸ electrons?"

Break it down. Given n = 6.2 × 10¹⁸ electrons, each carrying e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Find the total charge Q.

  1. Total charge is the count times the charge per electron: Q = ne.
  2. Substitute the magnitude: Q = (6.2 × 10¹⁸)(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹).
  3. Fronts: 6.2 × 1.602 = 9.93. Exponents: 18 − 19 = −1. So Q = 9.93 × 10⁻¹ = 0.993 C.
Q = −0.99 C (magnitude 0.99 C; negative, as these are electrons)

Shortcut. Multiply the fronts and subtract exponents: 6.2 × 1.6 ≈ 9.9, and 10¹⁸/10¹⁹ = 10⁻¹, so ≈ 0.99 C. Because they are electrons the sign is negative; magnitude is what the question wants.

Q12 · Absolute potential at two distancesSLIDE 12

As printed. "What is the absolute potential at each of the following distances from a charge of +4 [µ]C in air: r = 5 cm and r = 20 cm?"

Break it down. Given Q = +4 µC = 4 × 10⁻⁶ C, k = 9.0 × 10⁹ (the slide's value), r₁ = 5 cm = 0.05 m, r₂ = 20 cm = 0.20 m. Find V at each. This is the CBT "potential at two distances" item; solve it cold.

  1. Potential of a point charge: V = kQ/r. Note it goes as 1/r, not 1/r² (that is the field, a classic trap).
  2. At r₁ = 0.05 m: V₁ = (9.0 × 10⁹)(4 × 10⁻⁶)/0.05 = (3.6 × 10⁴)/0.05.
  3. At r₂ = 0.20 m: V₂ = (3.6 × 10⁴)/0.20. Since r₂ is four times r₁, V₂ is one quarter of V₁.
V₁ = 720 kV (at 5 cm) V₂ = 180 kV (at 20 cm)

Shortcut. Compute the numerator kQ once (3.6 × 10⁴), then just divide by each r. Four times the distance means one quarter the potential, so once you have 720 kV the second answer is 720/4 = 180 kV with no fresh arithmetic.

Q14 · Field from a point chargeSLIDE 14

As printed. "A charge of 8 x 10⁻⁸ C is 0.40 m from a charge of −2.0 x 10⁻⁸ C. Determine the electric field produced by the negative charge at the location of the positive charge."

Break it down. Given the source is the negative charge q = −2.0 × 10⁻⁸ C, and the field point is 0.40 m away (where the +8 × 10⁻⁸ C sits). The +8 × 10⁻⁸ C is a distractor: a charge does not feel its own field, and the question asks only for the field the negative charge makes. Find E.

  1. Field of a point charge, using the magnitude of the source: E = k|q|/r².
  2. Substitute: E = (9.0 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁸)/(0.40)² = (9.0 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁸)/0.16.
  3. Fronts: 9.0 × 2.0 = 18, so numerator 18 × 10¹ = 180; divide by 0.16.
E = 1125 N/C (directed toward the negative charge)

Shortcut. Use only the source charge and the separation: k|q|/r² = 180/0.16 = 1125 N/C. The other charge and its sign are noise for the magnitude; the field of a negative charge simply points inward, toward it.

Tutorial · Circuits

Series resistance and the real cell

A series current, then two problems on a cell's internal resistance, where the terminal voltage is always less than the e.m.f.

Q13 · Current in a series circuitSLIDE 13

As printed. "Given a circuit where R1, R2 and R3 are in series with a voltage source of 48 V, such that R1 = 7 [Ω], R2 = 6 [Ω] and R3 = 3 [Ω] calculate the current that flows through R3?"

Break it down. Given three resistors in series, R₁ = 7 Ω, R₂ = 6 Ω, R₃ = 3 Ω, across V = 48 V. Find the current through R₃. In a series circuit the same current flows through every element, so "through R₃" just means the circuit current.

  1. Series resistances add: RT = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ = 7 + 6 + 3 = 16 Ω.
  2. Ohm's law for the whole loop: I = V/RT = 48/16.
I = 3 A (the same through R₃ and every resistor)

Shortcut. Add the resistors (16) and divide into the source (48/16 = 3). The trap the question sets, "through R₃", changes nothing: series means one current everywhere. Only in parallel would the branch matter.

Q16 · E.m.f. from internal resistanceSLIDE 16

As printed. "In a circuit, the external resistor has a value of 5 [Ω]. The internal resistance of the cell is 0.5 [Ω]. If the current flowing is 1 A. Calculate the e.m.f of the cell."

Break it down. Given external R = 5 Ω, internal r = 0.5 Ω, current I = 1 A. Find the e.m.f. E. The e.m.f. drives current through the total resistance, inside and outside the cell.

  1. The e.m.f. pushes current through both resistances in series: E = I(R + r).
  2. Total resistance: R + r = 5 + 0.5 = 5.5 Ω.
  3. Substitute: E = (1)(5.5).
E = 5.5 V

Shortcut. E = I(R + r): add the two resistances (5.5) and multiply by the current (1). The internal resistance is why the terminal voltage (IR = 5 V) falls short of the 5.5 V e.m.f. by exactly Ir = 0.5 V.

Q19 · Unknown e.m.f. from terminal p.d.SLIDE 19

As printed. "A cell of unknown e.m.f E, and internal resistance 2 [Ω] is connected to a 5 [Ω] resistor. If the terminal p.d. is 1 volt, calculate the value of “E” if a current of “I” flows through the cell."

Break it down. Given internal r = 2 Ω, external R = 5 Ω, terminal p.d. V = 1 V. Find the current I first, then the e.m.f. E. The terminal p.d. is the voltage across the external resistor.

  1. The terminal p.d. sits across the external resistor, so I = V/R = 1/5 = 0.2 A.
  2. The e.m.f. is the terminal p.d. plus the voltage lost inside the cell: E = V + Ir.
  3. Substitute: E = 1 + (0.2)(2) = 1 + 0.4.
I = 0.2 A E = 1.4 V

Shortcut. Terminal p.d. over external resistance gives the current (1/5 = 0.2 A); then add back the internal drop Ir = 0.4 V to the terminal 1 V for E = 1.4 V. The lost-volts idea, E − V = Ir, is the whole of internal-resistance questions.

Tutorial · Magnetism and induction

Moving charges, wires, coils and transformers

Six slides here show a worked solution with the question sentence missing; each is reconstructed from its own Given block and flagged. Constants: µ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.

Q15 · Radius of an electron's pathSLIDE 15

As printed. The slide shows only the worked solution; the question sentence is not in the deck. From its Given block: an electron (m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) enters a field B = 0.03 T perpendicularly at v = 4 × 10⁶ m/s. Reconstructed ask: find the radius of its circular path.

Break it down. Given m, q, B, v as above, entering perpendicular to B so the path is a circle. Find r.

  1. The magnetic force supplies the centripetal force: qvB = mv²/r.
  2. Cancel one v and rearrange for r: r = mv/(qB).
  3. Substitute: r = (9.1 × 10⁻³¹)(4 × 10⁶)/[(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)(0.03)].
  4. Numerator: 9.1 × 4 = 36.4, exponent −25, so 3.64 × 10⁻²⁴. Denominator: 1.6 × 0.03 = 0.048, exponent −19, so 4.8 × 10⁻²¹.
r = 7.58 × 10⁻⁴ m ≈ 0.76 mm

Shortcut. Memorise r = mv/(qB) as "momentum over (charge times field)". Divide the fronts (36.4/4.8 = 7.58) and subtract exponents (−24 − (−21) = −3): 7.58 × 10⁻⁴ m. A sub-millimetre curl, as an electron's tiny mass demands.

Q17 · Current in a long straight wireSLIDE 17

As printed. The slide shows only the worked solution (with the standard field-around-a-wire illustration); the question sentence is not in the deck. From its Given block: the field is B = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T at r = 5 cm from a long straight wire. Reconstructed ask: find the current in the wire.

Break it down. Given B = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T, r = 5 cm = 0.05 m, µ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A. Find I.

  1. Field around a long straight wire: B = µ₀I/(2πr). Make I the subject: I = B(2πr)/µ₀.
  2. Substitute: I = (4.0 × 10⁻⁵)(2π × 0.05)/(4π × 10⁻⁷).
  3. The π cancels top and bottom: I = (4.0 × 10⁻⁵)(0.1)/(4 × 10⁻⁷) = (4.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(4 × 10⁻⁷).
I = 10 A

Shortcut. Write I = 2πrB/µ₀. The 2π on top and the 4π in µ₀ reduce to a half, leaving I = rB/(2 × 10⁻⁷). Then rB = 0.05 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶, and dividing by 2 × 10⁻⁷ gives 10 A. The π always cancels in straight-wire problems; never type it.

Q18 · Speed of a proton in a fieldSLIDE 18

As printed. The slide shows only the worked solution; the question sentence is not in the deck. From its Given block: a proton (q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) feels a force F = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ N in a field B = 0.2 T with velocity at θ = 60° to the field. Reconstructed ask: find the proton's speed.

Break it down. Given F, q, B, θ as above. Find v from the magnetic-force law.

  1. Magnetic force on a moving charge: F = qvB sin θ. Make v the subject: v = F/(qB sin θ).
  2. sin 60° = 0.866. Denominator: (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)(0.2)(0.866) = 2.77 × 10⁻²⁰.
  3. Substitute: v = (3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴)/(2.77 × 10⁻²⁰).
v = 1.15 × 10⁶ m/s

Shortcut. v = F/(qB sin θ). Build the denominator in one pass (qB = 3.2 × 10⁻²⁰, times sin 60° ≈ 2.77 × 10⁻²⁰), then 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ over that is ≈ 1.15 × 10⁶ m/s. Notice qB = 3.2 × 10⁻²⁰ mirrors the force's 3.2, keeping the front arithmetic clean.

Q20 · Current in a solenoidSLIDE 20

As printed. The slide shows only the worked solution; the question sentence is not in the deck. From its Given block: a solenoid l = 0.4 m long with N = 800 turns has an internal field B = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ T. Reconstructed ask: find the current.

Break it down. Given l = 0.4 m, N = 800, B = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ T, µ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷. Find I.

  1. Field inside a long solenoid: B = µ₀nI, where n = N/l is turns per metre.
  2. Turns per metre: n = 800/0.4 = 2000 /m.
  3. Rearrange: I = B/(µ₀n) = (2.5 × 10⁻⁴)/[(4π × 10⁻⁷)(2000)].
  4. Denominator: (4π × 10⁻⁷)(2000) = 2.513 × 10⁻³.
I = 0.0995 A ≈ 0.10 A

Shortcut. Find n = N/l first (2000), then I = B/(µ₀n). Since µ₀n = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 2000 ≈ 2.51 × 10⁻³, dividing 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ by it gives ≈ 0.10 A. The field depends on turns-per-metre, not the raw turn count, so l must go in.

Q21 · Secondary voltage of a transformerSLIDE 21

As printed. The slide shows only the worked solution; the question sentence is not in the deck. From its Given block: Np = 100 turns, Ns = 1000 turns, primary voltage Ep = 240 V. Reconstructed ask: find the secondary voltage (and name the transformer type).

Break it down. Given the turn counts and Ep. Since Ns > Np, it is a step-up transformer. Find Es.

  1. Voltage ratio equals turns ratio: Es/Ep = Ns/Np.
  2. So Es = Ep(Ns/Np) = 240 × (1000/100).
  3. The turns ratio is 10, so the voltage is multiplied by 10.
E_s = 2400 V (step-up, since N_s > N_p)

Shortcut. The turns ratio is the voltage ratio. Ns/Np = 10, so just multiply 240 by 10: 2400 V. More turns on the secondary means step-up; the voltage follows the turns.

Q22 · Magnetic dipole moment of a coilSLIDE 22

As printed. "A current of 3 A flows in a circular coil of radius 5 cm with 20 turns. Calculate the magnetic dipole moment."

Break it down. Given I = 3 A, r = 5 cm = 0.05 m, N = 20 turns. Find the magnetic dipole moment m.

  1. Dipole moment of a coil: m = NIA, with A the area of one turn.
  2. Area of the circle: A = πr² = π(0.05)² = π(2.5 × 10⁻³) = 7.85 × 10⁻³ m².
  3. Substitute: m = (20)(3)(7.85 × 10⁻³) = 60 × 7.85 × 10⁻³.
m = 0.471 A·m²

Shortcut. m = NIA. Do NI first (20 × 3 = 60), then times the area πr² = 7.85 × 10⁻³, giving 0.471 A·m². Do not forget to square the radius; 5 cm is 0.05 m, and (0.05)² = 2.5 × 10⁻³.

Q23 · Transformer efficiencySLIDE 23

As printed. "A 240 V step-down transformer is used to light up eight 12 V, 24 W lamps and draws a current of 1 A in the primary. Calculate its efficiency."

Break it down. Given primary Vp = 240 V, primary current Ip = 1 A, and 8 lamps each rated 24 W. Find efficiency η. The output is the total lamp power; the input is the primary power.

  1. Output power (the eight lamps): Pout = 8 × 24 = 192 W. (The 12 V rating is not needed once you have the lamp wattage.)
  2. Input power to the primary: Pin = VpIp = 240 × 1 = 240 W.
  3. Efficiency: η = (Pout/Pin) × 100% = (192/240) × 100%.
η = 80%

Shortcut. Total the load wattage (8 × 24 = 192 W) over the primary VA (240 × 1 = 240 W): 192/240 = 0.8 = 80%. The 12 V lamp rating is a distractor; power out is all you need on top.

Q24 · Dipole moment from a torqueSLIDE 24

As printed. The slide shows only the worked solution; the question sentence is not in the deck. From its Given block: a current loop in a field B = 0.6 T feels a torque τ = 4.8 × 10⁻³ N·m when its moment is at θ = 30° to the field. Reconstructed ask: find the magnetic dipole moment.

Break it down. Given τ, B, θ. Find m from the torque law.

  1. Torque on a magnetic dipole: τ = mB sin θ. Make m the subject: m = τ/(B sin θ).
  2. sin 30° = 0.5, so B sin θ = 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.3.
  3. Substitute: m = (4.8 × 10⁻³)/0.3.
m = 0.016 A·m²

Shortcut. m = τ/(B sin θ). sin 30° = ½ makes B sin θ = 0.3 instantly, and 4.8 × 10⁻³ over 0.3 = 1.6 × 10⁻². The two "nice" angles worth memorising: sin 30° = ½ and sin 60° = 0.866.

Q25 · Energy stored in an inductorSLIDE 25

As printed. "A 2 H inductor carries 5 A of current. What is the stored energy?"

Break it down. Given L = 2 H, I = 5 A. Find the stored energy W.

  1. Energy in an inductor: W = ½LI². (It mirrors ½CV² for a capacitor: energy goes as the square of what flows or sits.)
  2. Square the current: I² = 25.
  3. Substitute: W = ½(2)(25).
W = 25 J

Shortcut. ½LI²: the ½ and the L = 2 cancel, so it is just I² = 25 J. Whenever L = 2 H the energy in joules equals the current squared.

Q26 · Energy stored in a solenoidSLIDE 26

As printed. "A solenoid 0.5 m long has 1000 turns and carries 3 A current. Find the energy stored in it (area = 0.001 m²)."

Break it down. Given l = 0.5 m, N = 1000, I = 3 A, A = 0.001 m², µ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷. Find the stored energy. First get the inductance L, then W = ½LI².

  1. Inductance of an air-core solenoid: L = µ₀N²A/l.
  2. Substitute: L = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(1000)²(0.001)/0.5. With (1000)² = 10⁶: L = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(10⁶)(10⁻³)/0.5 = (4π × 10⁻⁴)/0.5 = 8π × 10⁻⁴.
  3. So L ≈ 2.513 × 10⁻³ H.
  4. Energy: W = ½LI² = ½(2.513 × 10⁻³)(3²) = ½(2.513 × 10⁻³)(9).
L = 2.51 × 10⁻³ H W = 1.13 × 10⁻² J ≈ 11.3 mJ

Shortcut. Keep L as 8π × 10⁻⁴ H and go straight to W = ½LI² = ½ × 8π × 10⁻⁴ × 9 = 36π × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 1.13 × 10⁻² J. Carrying the π symbolically to the last line beats rounding it twice.

Q27 · Secondary current of a transformerSLIDE 27

As printed. "A step-up transformer increases the voltage from 120 V to 600 V. If the primary current is 5 A, what is the secondary current assuming 100% efficiency?"

Break it down. Given Vp = 120 V, Vs = 600 V, Ip = 5 A, and 100% efficiency (ideal). Find Is. In an ideal transformer, power in equals power out.

  1. Ideal transformer: VpIp = VsIs. Make the secondary current the subject: Is = VpIp/Vs.
  2. Substitute: Is = (120 × 5)/600 = 600/600.
I_s = 1 A

Shortcut. Step up the voltage, step down the current by the same ratio. Voltage rises 120 → 600, a factor of 5, so current falls 5 → 1 A. Power (600 W) is unchanged; that is what "100% efficient" buys you.
