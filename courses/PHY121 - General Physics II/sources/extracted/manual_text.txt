FOUNDATIONS F.1
Powers of ten, prefixes and calculator discipline
Almost every quantity in this course is either enormous or minuscule. Handling that cleanly
is a skill, and it is worth more marks than any single formula.
TEACH · WHY POWERS OF TEN EXIST
The charge on an electron is 0.000000000000000000160 coulombs. Nobody can read
that, let alone multiply by it. So we write it as 1.60 × 10−19 C.
The rule is simply: 10n means move the decimal point n places. Positive n moves it
right and makes the number bigger. Negative n moves it left and makes the number smal‐
ler. That is all a power of ten is: a shorthand for where the decimal point sits.
● MUST-MEMORISE · THE THREE INDEX LAWS
10a × 10b = 10a+b     10a ÷ 10b = 10a−b     (10a)b = 10ab
MEMORY HOOK: multiply then add, divide then subtract, power then multiply. Every single
exponent error in this course comes from breaking one of these three.
TEACH · THE PREFIXES YOU WILL MEET
A prefix is just a power of ten wearing a costume. When a question says 5 nC, it is handing
you 5 × 10−9 C and hoping you notice.

FOUNDATIONS F.3
Scalars, vectors and components
Force, electric field and magnetic field are all vectors. Charge, potential and energy are not.
Knowing which is which prevents the most expensive class of error in this course.
TEACH · THE DISTINCTION
A  scalar has size only. Charge, mass, potential, energy, capacitance and resistance are
scalars. To combine them you simply add the numbers.
A vector has size and direction. Force, electric field, and magnetic field are vectors. To
combine them you cannot just add the numbers, because direction decides whether they
reinforce or cancel.
Charge is a scalar even though it carries a plus or minus sign. The sign says what kind of
charge it is, not which way it points. This confuses people every year. 
● MUST-MEMORISE · ADDING TWO VECTORS
If two vectors act along the same line, add or subtract the sizes directly.
If they act at right angles, use Pythagoras for the size and the tangent for the angle:
R = √(A2 + B2)      tan θ = B / A
MEMORY HOOK: square, add, root. It is Pythagoras, because a right-angled triangle is exactly
what two perpendicular vectors make.
TEACH · RESOLVING A VECTOR INTO COMPONENTS
A vector at an angle can always be replaced by two vectors at right angles, one horizontal
and one vertical, that together do exactly the same job. For a vector of size A at angle θ to
the horizontal:
Ax = A cos θ      Ay = A sin θ
Cosine goes with the side adjacent to the angle, sine with the side opposite. This is used directly in
Module 4, where a velocity at an angle to the magnetic field is split into a part along the field and a
part across it.

FOUNDATIONS F.5
Simultaneous equations
Kirchhoff's laws hand you two or three equations in two or three unknowns. This is the
machinery for solving them, and it is the whole difficulty of that topic.
TEACH · SUBSTITUTION, THE ONLY METHOD YOU NEED
If you have as many equations as unknowns, you can solve. The reliable method is substi‐
tution:
Take the simplest equation and make one unknown the subject.
Substitute that expression into the other equation. You now have one equation in one
unknown, which you can solve.
Put that value back into the first equation to get the other unknown.
Check both original equations with your two answers. If either fails, you have slipped.

FOUNDATIONS F.6
Reciprocal arithmetic
Parallel resistors and series capacitors both need this. It is easy, and it is also where a
startling number of marks quietly disappear.
TEACH · THE STEP EVERYONE FORGETS
When you compute something of the form
1 / Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3
the arithmetic gives you 1/Req, not Req. You must flip it at the end. Stopping one step
early is the single most common mistake in circuit questions.
● MUST-MEMORISE · THE TWO-ITEM SHORTCUT
For exactly two items, you can skip the reciprocals entirely:
Req = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2)
MEMORY HOOK: product over sum. It only works for two. With three or more, go back to the
reciprocals. This shortcut also works for two capacitors in series, since they follow the same
reciprocal rule.
WORKED EXAMPLE · BOTH ROUTES, SAME ANSWER
Two resistors, 250 Ω and 150 Ω, in parallel.
Req = 93.75 Ω
Sanity check that always works: a parallel combination is always smaller than the smallest resistor
present. Here 93.75 < 150. If your parallel answer ever comes out bigger than the smallest resistor, you
have made an error. This check is free and it never fails.
Product over sum: (250 × 150) / (250 + 150) = 37500 / 400 = 93.75 Ω. 
Two items, so the shortcut is legal.

FOUNDATIONS F.7
Proportional reasoning
Many exam questions never give you a number. They ask what happens to one quantity
when another doubles. These are free marks if you can read a formula properly.
TEACH · READING A FORMULA AS A STATEMENT ABOUT CHANGE
Look at where a symbol sits and what power it is raised to.
So in F = kq1q2 / r2, tripling the separation r does not cut the force to a third. It cuts it to
a ninth, because r is squared.
RECALL AND CHECK
In a parallel plate capacitor, C = ε0A / d. If you double the plate separation d and
also double the area A, what happens to C?
ANSWER
A is on top, so doubling it doubles C. d is on the bottom, so doubling it halves C. The two effects
cancel exactly: C is unchanged.
On the top: if it doubles, the answer doubles. Directly proportional.▸
On the bottom: if it doubles, the answer halves. Inversely proportional.▸
Squared on the bottom: if it doubles, the answer drops to a quarter. This is the
inverse square law, and it governs Coulomb's law.

FOUNDATIONS F.8
The small amount of calculus you need
Two places in this course use calculus. You do not need to be fluent, but you should not be
startled by the notation.
TEACH · WHAT THE NOTATION IS TELLING YOU
A derivative, dΦ/dt, is a rate of change. It means: how fast is Φ changing per second.
That is all. When Faraday's law says the induced emf equals −N dΦ/dt, it is saying that a
faster change of flux induces a bigger voltage. A flux that sits still induces nothing,
however large it is.
An integral is a total, accumulated in small pieces. When Module 2 charges a capa‐
citor, each extra scrap of charge dq is harder to add than the last, because the capacitor is
already charged and pushes back. The integral adds up all those unequal scraps of work.
That is where the factor of ½ in the energy formula comes from, and it is why the energy
is not simply QV .

UNIT 1.1
Forces in nature and the idea of charge
Learning outcomes: identify the four fundamental forces; explain electric charge and its
quantisation; describe charging by friction, conduction and induction.
TEACH · THE FOUR FUNDAMENTAL FORCES
Every interaction in the universe, from a balloon sticking to a wall to a star holding itself
together, is produced by just four forces.
FORCE RELATIVE
STRENGTH WHAT IT DOES
Strong nuclearStrongest Holds the nucleus together against the protons
repelling each other.
ElectromagneticVery strong Electricity, magnetism, light, chemistry, and
everything in this course.
Weak nuclear Weak Governs radioactive decay.
Gravity Weakest by far Attracts all mass. Only noticeable because it never
cancels out.
The  electromagnetic force is the one we meet most in daily life. It is responsible for
electricity, magnetism and light, and it is what PHY121 studies.
Gravity is the weakest of the four, which sounds absurd when it holds you to the Earth.
The reason it feels dominant is that it never cancels: mass only attracts. Electric forces are
vastly stronger but positive and negative charge almost perfectly balance in ordinary
matter, so their effects mostly cancel and we barely notice them. 
TEACH · WHAT ELECTROSTATICS IS
Electrostatics is the study of electric charges at rest, and of the forces and fields
they produce. The charges are not moving, yet they still push and pull on each other
across empty space. That interaction is the electric force.
At the heart of it is electric charge, a basic property of matter that comes in two kinds,
positive and negative.

The six properties of charge
TEACH · PROPERTIES 1 AND 2: ADDITIVITY AND CONSERVATION
1. Additivity. Total charge is just the algebraic sum of the individual charges, signs in‐
cluded:
Qtotal = q1 + q2 + q3 + …
So +5 C combined with −3 C gives +2 C. You add them like ordinary signed numbers,
because charge is a scalar.
2. Conservation. Charge cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one body
to another. The total charge of an isolated system never changes.
When you rub a balloon on your hair, you do not manufacture charge. Electrons move from your
hair to the balloon. The balloon gains exactly as much negative charge as the hair loses. The books
always balance.

Unit: the coulomb, C▸
Positive charge is carried by protons.▸
Negative charge is carried by electrons.▸

● MUST-MEMORISE · PROPERTY 3: QUANTISATION OF CHARGE
Charge  does  not  come  in  arbitrary  amounts.  It  comes  in  whole  multiples  of  one
fundamental lump:
q = n e   where   e = 1.602 × 10−19 C
n is an integer (…, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …) and e is the elementary charge, the magnitude of the charge
on one electron or one proton. 
MEMORY HOOK: charge comes in coins, not in liquid. You can have 3 coins or 4 coins, never 3.7
coins. A charge of 1.5e simply cannot exist.
TEACH · PROPERTIES 4, 5 AND 6
4. Invariance. The magnitude of a charge does not depend on its speed, on the frame you
observe it from, or on temperature and pressure. A charge is the same whether it is mov‐
ing or at rest. (Mass, by contrast, does change with speed at high velocities. Charge does
not.)
5. Charge is a scalar. It has magnitude only, and no direction. The plus or minus sign
indicates the type of charge, not a direction in space.
6. Transferability. Charge can be moved between bodies by friction, by conduction, or by
induction. These are the three methods of charging, covered next.
⚠ TRAP · THE SIGN ON A CHARGE IS NOT A DIRECTION
Because charge is a scalar, you add charges as signed numbers. But force is a vector, so
you cannot add forces as signed numbers unless they lie on the same line. Students
routinely add up the forces from several charges as if they were numbers. They are not.
See Unit 1.2 on superposition.

The three ways to charge a body
TEACH · FRICTION, CONDUCTION AND INDUCTION
(a) Charging by friction. Rub two different materials together and electrons transfer
from one to the other. One becomes positive, the other equally negative. This is the
balloon-and-hair case.
(b) Charging by conduction. Touch a charged object to a neutral one. Charge flows
across the contact, and both objects end up with the same sign of charge, sharing what
was there.
(c) Charging by induction. Bring a charged object near a neutral one, without touching.
The charges inside the neutral object rearrange: the opposite kind is pulled towards the
near side, the same kind is pushed to the far side. If you now earth the far side and
remove it, the object is left with a net charge opposite to the one you brought near, and
you never touched it.

● MUST-MEMORISE · COULOMB'S LAW
The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and
inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them:
F = k · q1q2 / r2
F is the force in newtons (N)
q1, q2 are the two charges in coulombs (C)
r is the separation in metres (m)

ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F m−1, the permittivity of free space 
MEMORY HOOK: product of charges on top, distance squared underneath. It has exactly the same
shape as Newton's law of gravitation, with charge in place of mass.

The electric field
TEACH · WHY WE NEED A FIELD AT ALL
Coulomb's law says two charges push on each other across empty space. But how does
one charge know the other is there? The field is the answer, and it is a genuinely useful
change of viewpoint.
We say a charge alters the space around it, creating an electric field. Any other charge
placed in that space then feels a force from the field at its own location. The field is the
middleman, and it exists whether or not there is a second charge there to feel it.
● MUST-MEMORISE · ELECTRIC FIELD: DEFINITION AND THE FIELD OF A POINT
CHARGE
The electric field is the force per unit positive charge:
E = F / q    so    F = qE
For a single point charge Q, the field at distance r is:
E = kQ / r2 = Q / (4πε0r2)
E is the electric field, in newtons per coulomb (N/C) or volts per metre (V/m). These two units are
identical. 
MEMORY HOOK: the field is Coulomb's law with one of the charges removed. Take F = kq1q2/r2,
divide out one charge, and you have the field the other one makes.

So the field points away from an isolated positive charge and towards an isolated negative

Electric potential and energy
Learning outcomes: compute the electric potential due to point charges; relate the electric

TEACH · THE GRAVITATIONAL ANALOGY, WHICH IS EXACT ENOUGH TO TRUST
Lift a book onto a shelf and you do work against gravity. The book now stores gravitation‐
al potential energy: let it go and the energy comes back as motion.
Electric  potential  energy  works  identically.  Push  a  positive  charge  towards  another
positive charge and you do work against the repulsion. The charge now stores electric
potential energy: let it go and it flies away, converting that energy into kinetic energy.
The height of the shelf is the analogue of potential. The book on the shelf is the analogue
of potential energy. Note the difference carefully: the shelf has a height whether or not a
book sits on it.
● MUST-MEMORISE · POTENTIAL ENERGY, POTENTIAL, AND THE DIFFERENCE
BETWEEN THEM
Electric potential energy is the work done in bringing a charge from infinity to a point
in the field:
U = kQq / r   (joules, J)
Electric potential is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing it from infinity to
that point:
V = kQ / r   (volts, V)
and therefore
U = qV    and    W = qV
MEMORY HOOK: potential is per unit charge. It belongs to the point in space, and exists even with
no charge there. Potential energy needs an actual charge to be sitting at that point. Shelf height
versus book on shelf.

⚠ TRAP · POTENTIAL HAS R, NOT R SQUARED
Compare carefully:
E = kQ / r2    but    V = kQ / r
The  field has r squared. The  potential has plain r. Mixing these up is one of the most
common errors in this module. Also remember that E is a vector but V is a scalar: to
combine potentials from several charges you simply add the numbers, signs included,
with no vector work at all. That makes potential much easier to handle than field, which is
precisely why it is worth having.
● MUST-MEMORISE · POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE AND WORK
The potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving a
charge from one to the other. The work done in moving a charge q through a potential
difference V is:
W = qV
MEMORY HOOK: work equals charge times voltage. This one formula reappears in Module 3 as
the work done by a battery, and in Module 4 for emf. It is the same idea each time.
TEACH · THE LINK BETWEEN FIELD AND POTENTIAL
The electric field is the negative gradient of the potential:
E = − dV / dr
In words: the field points in the direction in which the potential falls most steeply, and its
strength is the steepness of that fall. Water flows downhill fastest where the hill is steep‐
est. A positive charge is pushed "downhill" in potential in exactly the same way.
The minus sign encodes "downhill". For a uniform field between parallel plates separated
by distance d this simplifies to the very useful:
E = V / d
This is why the electric field can be measured in volts per metre.

TEACH · ELECTRIC FLUX, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE
Electric flux is a measure of how many field lines pass through a surface. Think of the
field as rain falling and the surface as a hoop: the flux is how much rain goes through the
hoop.
ΦE = E A cos θ
Hold the hoop face-on to the rain and you catch the most (θ = 0, cos = 1). Turn it edge-on
and you catch none (θ = 90°, cos = 0). The angle θ is measured between the field and the
normal to the surface, that is, the line sticking straight out of it.
Unit: N m2/C.
● MUST-MEMORISE · GAUSS'S LAW
The total electric flux through any  closed surface is proportional to the total charge
enclosed within that surface:
ΦE = Qenclosed / ε0
ΦE is the total flux out through the closed surface
Qenclosed is the net charge inside the surface only
ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m 
MEMORY HOOK: flux out equals charge in, divided by epsilon nought. Charges outside the surface
contribute nothing to the net flux: whatever field lines enter one side must leave the other.

This is the whole content of the law, and it is why it is such a powerful shortcut: you can
ignore everything outside your chosen surface.
Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges.▸
The number of lines crossing a closed surface depends only on the charge enclosed by
it.

A charge outside the surface sends lines in one side and straight out the other. They

TEACH · WHY MATERIALS MATTER
Electrostatics deals with charges at rest and the fields they produce. But fields exist in‐
side matter too, and how a material responds to an applied field determines everything
about it: whether it stores energy, whether it conducts, whether it can be used to build a
device.
Understanding  this  response  explains  energy  storage  in  capacitors,  the  behaviour  of
insulating materials, and the operation of electronic devices.
Conductors
● MUST-MEMORISE · THE FOUR PROPERTIES OF A CONDUCTOR IN EQUILIBRIUM
Free charge movement. Electrons respond immediately to an applied field, and
charges redistribute until equilibrium is reached.
Electrostatic equilibrium. Once settled, there is no net motion of charge, and the 
internal electric field becomes zero.
Charge distribution. All excess charge resides on the surface, with a higher
concentration at sharp points.
Field direction. At the surface, the electric field is perpendicular to that surface.
MEMORY HOOK: inside a conductor, E = 0. If it were not zero, the free charges would still be
moving, and by definition we would not be at equilibrium yet. The field being zero is what
equilibrium means.
TEACH · WHY CHARGE SITS ON THE SURFACE, AND CROWDS AT SHARP POINTS
Put excess charge on a conductor and every bit of it repels every other bit. They push
each other as far apart as they can get, and the furthest apart they can get is the outer
surface. So that is where they end up. Nothing is left in the interior, which is also why the
internal field is zero, exactly as Gauss's law told us in Unit 1.4.
At a sharp point, a charge has fewer neighbours crowding it from the sides, so charge
packs more densely there. This produces very strong fields at points and edges, and it is
the entire principle of the lightning conductor.

TEACH · OHM'S LAW, AND WHERE IT FAILS
Conduction of charge in a conductor is governed by  Ohm's law: the current flowing
through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided
temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.
V ∝ I    so, introducing a constant    V = IR
V = potential difference, in volts (V)
I = current, in amperes (A)
R = resistance, in ohms (Ω) 
From V = IR it follows that increasing the voltage increases the current, provided R is
constant, and that R measures the opposition to the flow of current.
The law does not apply to everything. It works for ohmic materials such as metals. It
fails for semiconductors with a negative temperature coefficient, where resistance
decreases as  temperature  increases,  so  conductivity  increases.  That  proviso  about
constant temperature is doing real work.

Capacitance and dielectrics
A capacitor stores energy in an electric field. This unit builds it from the definition, through
the geometry, to the energy, and then to combinations.
TEACH · WHAT A CAPACITOR IS
A capacitor is a device for storing electrical energy by allowing charge to accumulate on
metal plates. Apply a voltage across it and one plate becomes positively charged while the
other becomes equally negatively charged. The electric field between the plates stores
electrostatic energy.
That energy is recovered when the charges are allowed to flow back off the plates into the
circuit. Generally, any device that can store charge is a capacitor.
● MUST-MEMORISE · THE DEFINITION OF CAPACITANCE
The capacitance C of a capacitor is the ratio of the magnitude of the charge Q stored on
either conductor to the potential difference V between them:
C = Q / V    (so Q = CV)
C = capacitance, in farads (F)
1 farad = 1 coulomb per volt. Common submultiples: microfarad (µF = 10−6 F) and picofarad (pF
= 10−12 F). 
MEMORY HOOK: capacitance is charge per volt. It measures how much charge the device will hold
for each volt you push across it. A big capacitance holds a lot of charge cheaply.
⚠ TRAP · CAPACITANCE DOES NOT DEPEND ON Q OR V
C = Q/V looks as though capacitance depends on charge and voltage. It does not. Capa‐
citance is fixed by the geometry: the size and shape of the conductors and the material
between them. Double the voltage and you simply double the charge stored, and the ratio
C stays exactly the same. The slides state this explicitly: potential is geometrically de‐
pendent, so in determining capacitance, the geometry of the surface storing the charge is
what matters.

TEACH · WHERE THE FACTOR OF ONE HALF COMES FROM
Charge a capacitor from zero up to a final charge Q. At an intermediate moment it already
carries charge q, and the potential difference across it is v = q/C.
To add one more infinitesimal scrap of charge dq, you must do work against that existing
potential:
dW = v dq = (q/C) dq
The point is that each scrap is harder to add than the last, because the capacitor is
already partly charged and pushes back. So the total work is not simply QV . Adding up all
those unequal scraps, using the one integral from Foundations F .8:
W = ∫0Q (q/C) dq = (1/C) · [q2/2]0Q = Q2 / 2C
That is where the one half is born: from the fact that the voltage rose steadily from zero to
V as you charged it, so the average voltage you worked against was only V/2.

All electric circuits have one thing in common: they depend on the flow of electric charge.

● MUST-MEMORISE · DIODE AND TRANSISTOR
Diode. The simplest semiconductor device: a p-type joined to an n-type. Its basic property
is that it allows current to flow in one direction only.
Transistor. A sandwich of three layers. The common type has n-type on either side of a
thin p-type middle: an npn transistor. Reverse the sequence and you have a pnp tran‐

The basic function of a transistor is to act as an electronic switch that controls the flow
of current in a circuit.
MEMORY HOOK: the diode is a one-way valve; the transistor is a switch. Those two devices, made

Series, parallel and combination circuits
Nearly every circuit question reduces to this: replace groups of resistors with a single
equivalent resistor until only one remains, then apply Ohm's law.
● MUST-MEMORISE · RESISTORS IN SERIES
Resistors connected end to end form a series circuit. The equivalent resistance is simply
the sum:
Req = R1 + R2 + R3 + …
The defining features of a series circuit:
MEMORY HOOK: series adds. More resistors in a line means a longer, harder path, so more
resistance. Two identical resistors R in series give 2R.
● MUST-MEMORISE · RESISTORS IN PARALLEL
Resistors connected across the same potential difference form a parallel circuit. The
reciprocals add:
1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + …
The defining features of a parallel circuit:
MEMORY HOOK: parallel lanes on a motorway. Adding another lane makes it easier for traffic to
get through, so resistance falls. Two identical resistors R in parallel give R/2; three give R/3.
⚠ TRAP · DO NOT FORGET TO FLIP THE RECIPROCAL
The parallel formula gives you 1/Req, not Req. You must invert at the end. Stopping one
step early is the most common error in the entire course.
Free check, every time: a parallel answer must be smaller than the smallest resistor
in the group. If it is not, you forgot to flip.

Voltage dividers and current dividers
Two shortcuts that let you find a voltage or a current directly, without computing the whole
circuit first. Worth knowing well: they turn several lines of work into one.
● MUST-MEMORISE · THE VOLTAGE DIVIDER RULE
The voltage drop across any given resistor in a series circuit equals the ratio of that res‐
istor to the total resistance, multiplied by the source voltage.
V1 = [ R1 / (R1 + R2) ] × V
and correspondingly V2 = [ R2 / (R1 + R2) ] × V. In general, replace the denominator with the total
series resistance RT. 
MEMORY HOOK: the resistor you want goes on top; the total goes underneath. The bigger resistor
takes the bigger share of the voltage, which is exactly what you would expect.

UNIT 3.4
Kirchhoff's laws
When a circuit cannot be reduced by series and parallel rules alone, Kirchhoff's two laws
will always solve it. They are the most powerful tool in this module and the most heavily ex‐
amined.
● MUST-MEMORISE · THE THREE DEFINITIONS YOU MUST HAVE FIRST
MEMORY HOOK: junctions are where wires meet; loops are closed paths you can walk round and
return to the start. Kirchhoff's first law is about junctions, the second is about loops.
● MUST-MEMORISE · KIRCHHOFF'S CURRENT LAW (KCL)
The algebraic sum of the currents entering any junction equals the sum of the currents
leaving it.
∑I = 0    or    ∑Iin = ∑Iout
MEMORY HOOK: KCL is a statement of the conservation of charge. Charge cannot pile up at a
junction or vanish there. Whatever flows in must flow out. Think of water pipes meeting at a T-
joint.
● MUST-MEMORISE · KIRCHHOFF'S VOLTAGE LAW (KVL)
The algebraic sum of the potential differences across all the elements around any closed
loop must be zero.
∑V = 0    or    ∑(voltage drops) = ∑(voltage rises)
MEMORY HOOK: KVL is a statement of the conservation of energy. Walk all the way round a
loop and return to where you started, and you must be back at the same potential. Like walking
round a hill and returning to your front door: your net change in height is zero, however much you
climbed on the way.
Junction (also called a node or branch point): a point in a circuit where two or more
conductors meet.
▸
Branch: a single element in the circuit, such as a resistor, a current source or a
voltage source.
▸
Loop: a collection of branches forming a closed conducting path.▸

The sign conventions, which are where marks are lost
● MUST-MEMORISE · THE FOUR SIGN RULES FOR TRAVERSING A LOOP
First choose a travel direction around the loop. It may be clockwise or anticlockwise; it
genuinely does not matter, as long as you are consistent. Then:
WHAT YOU PASS THROUGH SIGN OF THE TERM
A source, travelling from − to + emf is positive (+ε)
A source, travelling from + to − emf is negative (−ε)
A resistor, travelling with the assumed current the IR term is negative (−IR)
A resistor, travelling against the assumed current the IR term is positive (+IR)
MEMORY HOOK: going with the current through a resistor is going downhill in potential, so the
term is negative. Going from minus to plus through a battery is climbing uphill, so it is positive.
Voltage is like height, and you are walking a landscape.
⚠ TRAP · A NEGATIVE CURRENT IS NOT A MISTAKE
You must assume a current direction before you can write the equations, and you have no
way  of  knowing  in  advance  whether  you  guessed  right.  If  your  assumption  was
backwards, the algebra returns a negative value.
This is not an error, and you should not go back and redo it. It means: the magnitude is
correct, but the real current flows in the direction opposite to your arrow. Simply state
that. Losing your nerve and re-deriving everything is the actual mistake here.

TEACH · WHAT A MAGNETIC FIELD IS
A magnetic field is a vector field describing the magnetic influence on moving electric
charges, electric currents, and magnetic materials. Its symbol is B.
The crucial sentence, which contains the whole unit: a moving charge in a magnetic
field experiences a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field.
Unit: the tesla (T), where 1 T = 1 N s / (C m). 
● MUST-MEMORISE · PROPERTIES OF MAGNETIC FIELD LINES
MEMORY HOOK: the key difference from electric field lines. Electric lines begin on positive
charges and end on negative ones. Magnetic lines neither begin nor end: they always close on
themselves. This is because there is no such thing as an isolated magnetic pole.

field lines are perpendicular to the magnetic force on a moving charge. So the field
does not tell you the force direction directly, which is why we need a hand rule.
Electric field lines begin and end on charges; magnetic field lines never do.
Sources of magnetic fields: moving charges (that is, currents), permanent magnets,
and changing electric fields.

Motion of charged particles in a magnetic field
● MUST-MEMORISE · A MAGNETIC FIELD DOES NO WORK
The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the motion. Work is force times displace‐
ment times cos θ, and here θ = 90°, so:
W = F d cos 90° = 0
Therefore, in a magnetic field:
MEMORY HOOK: a magnetic field steers but never speeds up. It cannot change the speed or the
kinetic energy of a particle, only bend its path. An electric field, by contrast, can accelerate a
charge and change its energy.
TEACH · THE THREE CASES OF MOTION
1. Motion parallel to the field (θ = 0). sin 0 = 0, so F = 0. The particle carries straight
on, undeflected.
2. Motion perpendicular to the field (θ = 90°). The force is maximum and always at
right angles to the velocity, so it acts as a  centripetal force. The particle moves in a
circle.
3. Motion at an angle θ. Split the velocity into components (Foundations F .3):
vparallel = v cos θ      vperpendicular = v sin θ
The perpendicular component produces circular motion; the parallel component carries
the particle steadily forward. Combine a circle with a forward drift and you get a helix,
that is, a spiral. The forward distance covered in one full revolution is called the pitch.
Kinetic energy remains constant.▸
Speed remains constant.▸
Only the direction changes.▸

Currents make magnetic fields. Two laws compute them: Biot-Savart for any shape,

TEACH · MAGNETIC FORCE ON A CURRENT-CARRYING CONDUCTOR
A wire carrying a current, placed in a magnetic field, experiences a force. This is not a

Add up the effect on all of them and you get a net force on the whole wire.
This  is  the  operating  principle  of  electric  motors,  loudspeakers,  galvanometers,
moving-coil meters and rail guns.

The Biot-Savart law
TEACH · WHERE THE LAW CAME FROM
In 1820 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Oersted noticed that a compass needle de‐
flected when placed near a current-carrying wire. That was the discovery that electricity
and magnetism are connected: a current produces a magnetic field.
In the same year the French physicists Jean-Baptiste Biot and Felix Savart worked out the
quantitative relationship. It lets us calculate the magnetic field produced by any current
distribution, which makes it fundamental to electromagnetism.
FORMULA · THE BIOT-SAVART LAW
The field dB produced by a small current element I dl is directly proportional to the cur‐
rent and the length of the element, proportional to the sine of the angle between the
element and the line joining it to the point, and inversely proportional to the square of
the distance:
dB = (µ0 / 4π) · (I dl × r̂) / r2
µ0 = permeability of free space = 4π × 10−7 H/m
I = current (A), dl = length element in the direction of the current
r = distance from the element to the point of interest 
Note this is directly analogous to Coulomb's law for the electric field of a point charge: an inverse
square law, with a constant out the front. The direction of dB is perpendicular to both r and dl.

UNIT 4.3
Electromagnetic induction
Oersted showed that a current makes a magnetic field. Faraday asked the reverse question:
can a magnetic field make a current? The answer, found in 1831, is the basis of the entire
electrical power industry.
TEACH · WHAT INDUCTION IS
Electromagnetic induction is the production of an emf, and in a closed circuit an
electric current, whenever the magnetic flux linking a conductor changes.
Michael Faraday discovered in 1831 that a changing magnetic field could induce a current
in a nearby conductor with no direct electrical contact at all.
A current is induced only if something changes:
It is the underlying principle of electric generators, transformers, induction motors,
wireless charging, microphones, metal detectors and induction cookers.
● MUST-MEMORISE · MAGNETIC FLUX
Magnetic flux measures the amount of magnetic field passing through a surface. It is the
number of field lines through a given area:
Φ = BA cos θ
Φ = magnetic flux, in webers (Wb), where 1 Wb = 1 T × 1 m2
B = magnetic flux density (T)
A = area (m2)
θ = angle between the field and the normal to the surface 
If the coil has N turns, the flux linkage is NΦ.
MEMORY HOOK: at θ = 0 the field is straight through the loop and cos 0 = 1, so the flux is 
maximum at Φ = BA. At θ = 90° the field skims along the surface and cos 90° = 0, so the flux is 
zero. Same hoop-and-rain picture as the electric flux in Module 1.
The magnetic field changes.▸
The conductor moves relative to the field.▸
The area of the conducting loop changes.▸
The orientation of the conductor changes.▸

● MUST-MEMORISE · FARADAY'S LAWS OF INDUCTION
Faraday's first law. Whenever the magnetic flux linking a conductor changes, an emf is
induced in the conductor. If the circuit is closed, an induced current flows.
Faraday's second law. The magnitude of the induced emf is directly proportional to the
rate of change of magnetic flux:
ε = −N · dΦ/dt
N = number of turns in the coil
dΦ/dt = rate of change of flux, in Wb/s
The minus sign is Lenz's law, explained next. 
MEMORY HOOK: the word to underline is CHANGE. A huge steady flux induces nothing. A small
but rapidly changing flux induces a large emf. It is the rate that matters, not the size.
● MUST-MEMORISE · LENZ'S LAW AND THE MINUS SIGN
The direction of the induced current is such that it opposes the change in magnetic
flux that produced it.
MEMORY HOOK: Lenz's law is a consequence of the conservation of energy. If the induced
current helped the change instead of opposing it, you would get a runaway: a tiny push would
produce ever-growing current and energy from nothing. Nature forbids that. The induced effect
always fights back, which is why you must do work to turn a generator.
FORMULA · MOTIONAL EMF AND INDUCED CURRENT
When a conductor of length L moves with velocity v through a field B:
ε = BLv

Eddy currents. When a solid conductor experiences a changing flux, closed loops of cur‐

UNIT 4.4
emf, internal resistance and terminal voltage
A real battery is not ideal. It has resistance of its own, and that single fact explains why your
headlights dim when you start the engine.
TEACH · WHAT EMF REALLY IS
Electromotive force is the energy per unit electric charge imparted by an energy source
such as a generator or a battery. Energy is converted from one form to another inside the
device as it does work on the charge passing through it, leaving one terminal positive and
the other negative.
The work done per unit charge is the emf. Its unit is the  volt: driving one coulomb
through a potential difference of one volt requires one joule of work. Despite the name,
emf is not a force. It is an energy per unit charge.
● MUST-MEMORISE · EMF VERSUS TERMINAL VOLTAGE
emf (E) is the potential difference of a source when no current is flowing. It is what the
battery would give you if nothing were connected.
Terminal voltage (V) is the voltage actually measured across the terminals while current
flows. It is always less than the emf.
E = I(R + r)    so    I = E / (R + r)
E = emf of the cell (V)
R = external resistance (Ω)
r = internal resistance of the cell (Ω)
I = current (A) 
MEMORY HOOK: the total resistance is R + r. The battery has to push current through itself as
well as through the circuit, and that wastes some voltage internally. Terminal voltage V = E − Ir:
the emf, minus what is lost inside.

TEACH · WHAT AN INDUCTOR IS
An  inductor is usually a coil of wire. From Unit 4.3, when the current through a coil
changes, the changing flux induces an emf in that same coil which opposes the change.
That is self-induction.
The constant relating the induced emf to the rate of change of current is the inductance
L, measured in henries (H):
ε = −L · dI/dt
An inductor therefore  resists changes in current, in the same way that mass resists
changes in velocity. It is electrical inertia.
● MUST-MEMORISE · ENERGY STORED IN AN INDUCTOR
W = ½L I2
W = energy stored, in joules (J)
L = inductance, in henries (H)
I = current through the inductor, in amperes (A) 
MEMORY HOOK: this is the exact magnetic twin of the capacitor's W = ½CV2 from Unit 2.2. Swap
capacitance for inductance, and voltage for current. A capacitor stores energy in an electric field
and cares about voltage; an inductor stores energy in a magnetic field and cares about current.
Learn them as a pair and you get both for the price of one.

moment
Examined in CBT Test 2, Q7, and again absent from every slide deck. It is, however, a near-

TEACH · A CURRENT LOOP IS A MAGNET
From Unit 4.2, a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force F = BIL sin θ. Now
bend that wire into a closed rectangular loop and put it in a uniform field.
Two opposite sides of the loop carry current in opposite directions, so they feel forces in
opposite directions. The forces cancel, so the loop does not fly away. But because they act
on different sides of the loop, they produce a twist: a torque that rotates the loop until it
lines up with the field.
This is exactly what an electric dipole does in an electric field, and it is the operating
principle of the electric motor and the moving-coil galvanometer.
● MUST-MEMORISE · MAGNETIC DIPOLE MOMENT AND TORQUE
The magnetic dipole moment of a loop of N turns, each of area A, carrying current I, is:
m = NIA
m is measured in ampere square metres (A m2).
The torque on that loop in a uniform field B is:
τ = m B sin θ    (and so τ = NIAB sin θ)
θ = angle between the loop's magnetic moment and the field. 
MEMORY HOOK: compare with the electric dipole from Unit 2.1, which had Τ = PE SIN Θ. It is the
identical formula with p replaced by m and E replaced by B. Torque uses sine. Spot the analogy
and this becomes a question you can answer instantly.

Q2 · ELECTRON AT 45 DEGREES: WHY THE PATH IS A HELIX
This question asks you to show something, so the reasoning matters more than the arith‐
metic.
r = m v sin θ / (qB)   ·   pitch = v cos θ × (2πm / qB) 
The idea to remember:sin for the radius, cos for the pitch. The perpendicular part curls the
particle round; the parallel part pushes it along. Every helix question in this course is these two formulas.
Resolve the velocity into two components (Foundations F .3): vparallel = v cos 45° and
vperp = v sin 45°. 
cos 45° = sin 45° = 0.707, so at exactly 45° the two components are equal.
1
The perpendicular component feels a force F = qvperpB, always at right angles to
the motion. That produces circular motion in the plane perpendicular to B. 
A constant force at right angles to velocity is precisely centripetal force.
2
The parallel component feels no force at all, because sin 0 = 0. So it continues 
unchanged and constant, carrying the particle steadily forward along the field. 
This is the crucial insight. The magnetic field cannot touch the parallel component.
3
Combine them: a circle in one plane, plus a constant drift at right angles to that
plane. A circle plus a steady forward drift is a helix. This is what was to be shown. 
Like the thread of a screw, or a stretched spring.
4
Radius: only the perpendicular component produces the circle, so r = mvperp / (qB)
= m(v sin 45°) / (qB). 
Use v sin θ, not v. Using the full v here is the classic error.
5
Pitch: the pitch is the forward distance travelled during one complete revolution. So
it is the parallel speed multiplied by the period: p = vparallel × T = (v cos 45°)(2πm /
qB).

A general problem-solving method
● MUST-MEMORISE · THE SEVEN STEPS
Read the question twice. Underline what is asked for. Many marks are lost by
answering a question that was not set.
List what you know, with symbols and units. Write the unknown with a question
mark.
Convert everything to SI immediately. Centimetres to metres, milliamps to amps,
microfarads to farads. Do this before anything else.
Choose the formula that contains what you know and what you want. If none does,
you need an intermediate step.
Rearrange in symbols first, and substitute numbers only at the end.
Compute, keeping track of powers of ten separately from the front numbers.
Check. Is the size believable? Does it have the right unit? Does the sanity check apply
(parallel less than smallest, voltages summing to the source)?
MEMORY HOOK: read, list, convert, choose, rearrange, compute, check. Step 3 and step 7 are the
two that save the most marks, and they are the two that are most often skipped.

TERM MEANING
Ampere (A) Unit of current. One coulomb per second.
Amperian loop An imaginary closed path chosen to make Ampere's law easy.
Branch A single element in a circuit, such as a resistor or a source.
Capacitance Charge stored per volt applied. Unit: farad.
Conductor A material in which charge moves freely.
Coulomb (C) Unit of electric charge.
Cyclotron
frequency
Revolutions per second of a charge in a magnetic field. Independent of
speed.
Dielectric An insulator between capacitor plates. Increases capacitance.
Dipole Two equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance.
Doping Adding impurities to a semiconductor to make it conduct.
Eddy current Loops of current induced inside a solid conductor by changing flux.
emf Energy per unit charge given by a source. Measured in volts, despite the
name.
Equipotential A surface on which the potential is the same everywhere.
Farad (F) Unit of capacitance. One coulomb per volt.
Gaussian surfaceAn imaginary closed surface chosen to make Gauss's law easy.
Insulator A material in which charge is bound and cannot move freely.
Internal resistanceThe resistance of a source itself. Causes terminal voltage to fall below emf.
Junction (node) A point where two or more conductors meet.
Loop A closed conducting path in a circuit.
Ohm (Ω) Unit of resistance. One volt per amp.
Ohmic Obeying Ohm's law, with resistance constant at fixed temperature.
Permittivity How readily a material permits an electric field. Symbol ε.
Permeability How readily a material permits a magnetic field. Symbol µ.
Polarisation Creation of induced dipoles in an insulator by an applied field.
Quantisation Charge exists only in whole multiples of e.
Resistivity A material's intrinsic opposition to current. Symbol ρ. Unit Ω m.
