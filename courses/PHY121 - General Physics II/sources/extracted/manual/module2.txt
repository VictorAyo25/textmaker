UNIT 2.1

## Conductors, insulators and the electric dipole

The slides set out the logic of this unit explicitly: material response (conductors and insulators), then microscopic behaviour (dipoles), then devices (capacitors), then energy storage. Follow that chain and the unit is straightforward.

TEACH · WHY MATERIALS MAT TER

Electrostatics deals with charges at rest and the fields they produce. But fields exist inside matter too, and how a material responds to an applied field determines everything about it: whether it stores energy, whether it conducts, whether it can be used to build a device. Understanding this response explains energy storage in capacitors, the behaviour of insulating materials, and the operation of electronic devices.

### Conductors

MUST-MEMORISE · THE FOUR PROPERTIES OF A CONDUCTOR IN EQUILIBRIUM

 - Free charge movement. Electrons respond immediately to an applied field, and charges redistribute until equilibrium is reached.
 - Electrostatic equilibrium. Once settled, there is no net motion of charge, and the internal electric field becomes zero.
 - Charge distribution. All excess charge resides on the surface, with a higher concentration at sharp points.
 - Field direction. At the surface, the electric field is perpendicular to that surface.

MEMORY HOOK: inside a conductor, E = 0. If it were not zero, the free charges would still be

moving, and by definition we would not be at equilibrium yet. The field being zero is what equilibrium means.

TEACH · WHY CHARGE SITS ON THE SURFACE, AND CROWDS AT SHARP POINTS

Put excess charge on a conductor and every bit of it repels every other bit. They push each other as far apart as they can get, and the furthest apart they can get is the outer surface. So that is where they end up. Nothing is left in the interior, which is also why the internal field is zero, exactly as Gauss's law told us in Unit 1.4. At a sharp point, a charge has fewer neighbours crowding it from the sides, so charge packs more densely there. This produces very strong fields at points and edges, and it is the entire principle of the lightning conductor.

TEACH · OHM'S LAW, AND WHERE IT FAILS

Conduction of charge in a conductor is governed by Ohm's law: the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.

V ∝ I    so, introducing a constant    V = IR

V = potential difference, in volts (V) I = current, in amperes (A) R = resistance, in ohms (Ω) From V = IR it follows that increasing the voltage increases the current, provided R is constant, and that R measures the opposition to the flow of current. The law does not apply to everything. It works for ohmic materials such as metals. It fails for semiconductors with a negative temperature coefficient, where resistance decreases as temperature increases, so conductivity increases. That proviso about constant temperature is doing real work.

WORKED EXAMPLE · THE OHM'S LAW TEMPERATURE TESTSLIDE

From the slides: a material carries 2 mA at 2 V. At 350 K, the same 2 V drives 5 mA. Does the material obey Ohm's law?

 - First condition: V = 2 V, I = 2 mA = 2 × 10−3 A. Convert milliamps to amps immediately, or the resistance will be out by a factor of 1000.
 - R = V/I = 2 / (2 × 10−3) = 1000 Ω. Dividing by 10−3 is the same as multiplying by 1000.
 - Second condition at 350 K: V = 2 V, I = 5 mA = 5 × 10−3 A.
 - R = V/I = 2 / (5 × 10−3) = 400 Ω.
 - Compare. The resistance fell from 1000 Ω to 400 Ω when the temperature rose. R drops from 1000 Ω to 400 Ω. Resistance decreases as temperature increases, so the material does not obey Ohm's law. It behaves as a semiconductor.For an ohmic material R must stay constant. It did not.

Now redo it: the whole question turns on the phrase "provided temperature remains constant". A

material that changes its resistance when heated is not ohmic, and this is the signature of a

semiconductor with a negative temperature coefficient.

### Insulators and polarisation

TEACH · WHAT AN INSULATOR DOES INSTEAD

An insulator is a material in which there is no free charge movement. Common examples are glass, rubber and plastic. But an insulator is not inert. Apply a field to it and, although no charge can travel through it, something still happens:

 - The charges shift slightly within each atom. The electron cloud is pulled one way, the nucleus the other.
 - An induced dipole is created.

Polarisation is the process whereby an induced dipole is created in an insulator by an applied electric field.

MUST-MEMORISE · CONDUCTOR VERSUS INSULATOR

MEMORY HOOK: conductors move charge, insulators stretch it. That single distinction generates

every row of this table, and it is a very likely exam question.

### The electric dipole

TEACH · WHAT A DIPOLE IS

An atom in a field has its electron cloud centre pulled away from its nucleus. Those two displaced centres of charge, equal in size and opposite in sign, form an electric dipole. Formally: a system of two equal and opposite charges (+q and −q) separated by a small distance d constitutes an electric dipole. Polarisation in an insulator is simply what you get when a vast number of these dipoles all line up together.

MUST-MEMORISE · DIPOLE MOMENT

p = q d

q = magnitude of one of the charges, in coulombs (C) d = separation distance, in metres (m) p = dipole moment, measured in coulomb metres (C m)

MEMORY HOOK: the unit follows straight from the formula. Charge times distance gives coulomb

metres. The slides pose exactly this as a class drill, and the unit is the answer.

FORMULA · A DIPOLE IN A UNIFORM ELECTRIC FIELD

Place a dipole in a uniform field E. The positive end is pushed one way and the negative end the other, so the field twists it. The forces cancel, so the dipole does not move off, but it rotates, tending to align with the field.

τ = pE sin θ

and the potential energy stored is

U = −pE cos θ

τ = torque (the twisting effect), θ = angle between the dipole and the field. 

TEACH · READING THE ENERGY EQUATION

The slides state that the dipole is aligned with the field at minimum energy, and opposite to the field at maximum energy. Check this against the torque formula: when θ = 0 the dipole is aligned, sin 0 = 0, and the torque vanishes. There is nothing left to twist it, so it stays put. That is exactly what an equilibrium position means. When the dipole is opposite to the field, it is unstable: the slightest nudge and it flips round.

A compass needle behaves in precisely this way in a magnetic field. It swings until it lines up, then stops.

RECALL AND CHECK

Two charges of +2 nC and −2 nC are separated by 3 mm. What is the dipole moment?

Answer

p = qd = (2 × 10−9 C)(3 × 10−3 m) = 6 × 10−12 C m. Use the magnitude of one charge, not the sum of both, and remember that 3 mm is 3 × 10−3 m.

## Capacitance and dielectrics

A capacitor stores energy in an electric field. This unit builds it from the definition, through the geometry, to the energy, and then to combinations.

TEACH · WHAT A CAPACITOR IS

A capacitor is a device for storing electrical energy by allowing charge to accumulate on metal plates. Apply a voltage across it and one plate becomes positively charged while the other becomes equally negatively charged. The electric field between the plates stores electrostatic energy. That energy is recovered when the charges are allowed to flow back off the plates into the circuit. Generally, any device that can store charge is a capacitor.

MUST-MEMORISE · THE DEFINITION OF CAPACITANCE

The capacitance C of a capacitor is the ratio of the magnitude of the charge Q stored on either conductor to the potential difference V between them:

C = Q / V    (so Q = CV)

C = capacitance, in farads (F) 1 farad = 1 coulomb per volt. Common submultiples: microfarad (µF = 10−6 F) and picofarad (pF = 10−12 F). 

MEMORY HOOK: capacitance is charge per volt. It measures how much charge the device will hold

for each volt you push across it. A big capacitance holds a lot of charge cheaply.

TRAP · CAPACITANCE DOES NOT DEPEND ON Q OR V

C = Q/V looks as though capacitance depends on charge and voltage. It does not. Capacitance is fixed by the geometry: the size and shape of the conductors and the material between them. Double the voltage and you simply double the charge stored, and the ratio C stays exactly the same. The slides state this explicitly: potential is geometrically dependent, so in determining capacitance, the geometry of the surface storing the charge is what matters.

### Capacitance depends on geometry

WORKED EXAMPLE 1 · CAPACITANCE OF AN ISOLATED CONDUCTING SPHERESLIDE

An insulated conducting sphere of radius R carries charge Q. Find (1) the potential of its outer surface, and (2) its capacitance.

 - All the charge on a conductor sits on its surface, and for external points the sphere behaves exactly like a point charge at its centre. This is the Gauss's law result from Unit 1.4, being put to work.
 - So the potential at the surface (r = R) is V = Q / (4πε0R). Potential of a point charge, with plain R on the bottom, not R squared. This is constant over the surface, which is why it is an equipotential surface.
 - Now apply the definition of capacitance, C = Q/V, and substitute that V: C = Q ÷ [Q / (4πε0R)]. Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal.
 - The Q cancels top and bottom. And this is the whole point: the charge disappears, leaving capacitance as a function of geometry alone, exactly as promised.
V = Q / (4πε0R)   and   C = 4πε0R
Read the result: capacitance depends only on R. A larger sphere has a larger capacitance. Zero

charge is mentioned nowhere in the answer. Note also that the potential goes to zero as R tends to

infinity, which is why we take earth (ground) as the zero of potential.

WORKED EXAMPLE · CAPACITANCE OF A SHELL OF RADIUS 100 CMCLASSWORK

Find the capacitance if R = 100 cm. Take ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m.

 - Convert first: R = 100 cm = 1.0 m. The single most likely place to lose this mark.
 - C = 4πε0R = 4π(8.85 × 10−12)(1.0).
 - 4π = 12.566, and 12.566 × 8.85 = 111.2. So C = 111.2 × 10−12 F.
 - Write in standard form: 111.2 × 10−12 = 1.11 × 10−10 F.
C ≈ 1.1 × 10−10 F  (about 111 pF)
Now redo it: I recomputed this independently as 1.1121 × 10−10 F, so the slide's 1.1 × 10−10 F is

correct. Note how small a farad is as a unit: a sphere a metre across has a capacitance of only about 100

pF. Real farad-sized capacitors are an engineering achievement.

### The parallel plate capacitor

MUST-MEMORISE · THE PARALLEL PLATE CAPACITOR

This is the most commonly used capacitor: two metal plates held close together with an insulator between them.

C = ε0A / d

A = area of one plate, in m2 d = separation between the plates, in m ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m 

MEMORY HOOK: area on top, distance underneath. Bigger plates hold more charge, so

capacitance rises with A. Push the plates closer and the attraction between the opposite charges is stronger, so more charge is held for the same voltage, so capacitance rises as d falls.

TEACH · THE THREE FACTORS THAT SET THE CAPACITANCE

Reading C = ε0A/d straight off, the factors affecting a parallel plate capacitor are:

 - The area of the plates.
 - The separation between the plates.
 - The dielectric material insulating the plates.

The third does not appear in the formula above, because that formula assumes a vacuum. Putting a real insulator in changes things, and that is the next box.

MUST-MEMORISE · DIELECTRICS

A dielectric is an insulating material placed between the plates. It increases the capacitance by a factor called the dielectric constant (or relative permittivity) εr:

C = εr C0    and so    C = εrε0A / d

C0 = the capacitance in vacuum, that is, with no dielectric present. εr has no units. It is a pure multiplying factor, always greater than 1. 

MEMORY HOOK: the dielectric helps. It always multiplies the capacitance up, never down. The

reason is polarisation from Unit 2.1: the dielectric's dipoles line up and partly cancel the field between the plates, so the voltage falls for the same charge, and since C = Q/V a smaller V means a larger C.

 - Apply C = εrC0 = 3 × 8.85 × 10−11 F. A single multiplication. The geometry has not changed, only the material.

C ≈ 2.7 × 10−10 F

### Combining capacitors

MUST-MEMORISE · CAPACITORS IN PARALLEL AND IN SERIES

MEMORY HOOK: capacitors behave backwards compared with resistors. Capacitors add in

parallel; resistors add in series. If you remember one, you get the other by flipping it.

TEACH · WHY THE RULES ARE THAT WAY ROUND

Parallel: putting capacitors side by side is effectively making one big capacitor with more plate area. Since C is proportional to A, capacitance simply adds. Series: connecting them end to end is like increasing the total separation d. Since C is proportional to 1/d, capacitance falls. That is why the series result is always smaller than the smallest capacitor in the chain, which is a free sanity check on every series answer you produce. For exactly two capacitors in series you may use the product-over-sum shortcut from Foundations F.6: Ceq = C1C2 / (C1 + C2).

### Energy stored in a capacitor

TEACH · WHERE THE FACTOR OF ONE HALF COMES FROM

Charge a capacitor from zero up to a final charge Q. At an intermediate moment it already carries charge q, and the potential difference across it is v = q/C. To add one more infinitesimal scrap of charge dq, you must do work against that existing potential:

dW = v dq = (q/C) dq

The point is that each scrap is harder to add than the last, because the capacitor is already partly charged and pushes back. So the total work is not simply QV. Adding up all those unequal scraps, using the one integral from Foundations F.8:

W = ∫0Q (q/C) dq = (1/C) · [q2/2]0Q = Q2 / 2C

That is where the one half is born: from the fact that the voltage rose steadily from zero to V as you charged it, so the average voltage you worked against was only V/2.

MUST-MEMORISE · THE THREE FORMS OF THE ENERGY STORED

Starting from W = Q2/2C and using Q = CV, all three of these are the same equation wearing different clothes:

W = Q2 / 2C   =   ½CV2   =   ½QV

MEMORY HOOK: memorise one, derive the other two by substituting Q = CV. Use whichever form

matches the quantities you were given. If you know C and V, use ½CV2. If you know Q and C, use Q2/2C.

WORKED EXAMPLE 3 · A PARALLEL AND SERIES COMBINATIONSLIDE

A circuit has C1 = 2.0 µF and C2 = 3.0 µF in parallel. That combination is in series with C3 = 4.0 µF. The whole thing is connected to a 12 V battery. Find (a) the equivalent capacitance, (b) the total charge, (c) the total energy stored.
 - Deal with the parallel pair first, because it is self-contained. Capacitors in parallel simply add: Cp = C1 + C2 = 2 + 3 = 5 µF. Always collapse the innermost group first, then treat it as a single component.
 - Now Cp = 5 µF is in series with C3 = 4 µF. Two items, so use product over sum: Ceq = (5 × 4) / (5 + 4) = 20/9. Series means reciprocals, and product over sum is the two-item shortcut.
 - Ceq = 20/9 = 2.22 µF. Sanity check: smaller than the smallest in the series pair (4 µF). Correct.
 - Total charge: Q = CeqV = (20/9 × 10−6)(12) = 2.67 × 10−5 C. Convert microfarads to farads before multiplying, or the answer will be out by 106. Keep Ceq exact as 20/9 here: the rounded 2.22 gives 2.66 × 10−5 C, wrong in the third figure. That is this manual's own rounding trap, in the wild.
 - Total energy: W = ½CV2 = ½(2.22 × 10−6)(12)2 = ½(2.22 × 10−6)(144). Square the voltage before multiplying. 122 = 144.
(a) Ceq ≈ 2.22 µF  ·  (b) Q ≈ 26.7 µC  ·  (c) W ≈ 1.60 × 10−4 J
Now redo it: I recomputed each independently: Ceq = 2.2222 µF, Q = 2.6667 × 10−5 C, W = 1.600 ×

10−4 J. All three slide values are correct. The method to carry away is collapse the innermost group first.

It works for every network you will meet.

RECALL AND CHECK

Three capacitors, 2 µF, 4 µF and 8 µF, are connected in series. Without calculating, what can you immediately say about the answer?

Answer

It must be less than 2 µF, the smallest one present, because a series combination is always smaller than its smallest member. (For the record, the actual value is 1/C = 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 = 0.875, so C = 1.14 µF, which is indeed under 2 µF.) Use this check on every series answer.
