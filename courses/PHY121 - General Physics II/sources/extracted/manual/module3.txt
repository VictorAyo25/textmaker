## Electric current, resistance and semiconductors

Everything in Modules 1 and 2 held charges still. Now we let them move.

TEACH · WHAT A CURRENT IS

All electric circuits have one thing in common: they depend on the flow of electric charge. When charge flows from one place to another, we say it forms an electric current. The more charge that flows, and the faster it flows, the greater the current. In general, the charge is carried through a circuit by electrons.

MUST-MEMORISE · DEFINITION OF ELECTRIC CURRENT

The current is the amount of charge passing a point, divided by the time taken:

I = ΔQ / Δt

I = current, in amperes (A) ΔQ = charge that flows past the point, in coulombs (C) Δt = time taken, in seconds (s) SI unit: coulombs per second (C/s), which is the ampere (A). 

MEMORY HOOK: 1 A = 1 C/S. One amp is one coulomb of charge passing per second. The unit is

named after the French physicist Andre-Marie Ampere.

WORKED EXAMPLE · COMPARING CURRENTSSLIDE, CONCEPTUAL

Which has the greatest current, and which the smallest? (a) the flow of 6 C in 3 s, (b) the flow of 3 C in 1 s, (c) a person walking 8 m in 4 s.

 - (a) I = 6 C / 3 s = 2 A. Current is charge per time. Just divide.
 - (b) I = 3 C / 1 s = 3 A. Less charge than (a), but it moves in a third of the time, so the current is higher.
 - (c) The current is zero. A person's net charge is essentially zero. Moving a neutral object from one place to another transports no net charge, so there is no current. This is the whole point of the question.
Greatest: (b) at 3 A. Smallest: (c) at zero.
The lesson: current is not about things moving. It is about net charge moving. Something can move a

great deal and carry no current at all.

WORKED EXAMPLE · HOW MANY ELECTRONS?SLIDE, GUIDED

A disk drive draws a current of 0.22 A for 4.5 s. How many electrons pass through it? (Charge on an electron, e = 1.60 × 10−19 C.)

 - First find the total charge. Rearrange I = ΔQ/Δt to give ΔQ = I Δt. We know the current and the time, so the charge follows immediately.
 - ΔQ = (0.22 A)(4.5 s) = 0.99 C. Amps times seconds gives coulombs, straight from the definition.
 - Now divide that total charge by the charge on one electron: N = ΔQ / e. If each electron carries e, then the number of them is the total divided by the individual amount. This is just counting.
 - N = 0.99 / (1.60 × 10−19) = 6.2 × 1018 electrons. Dividing by 10−19 is multiplying by 1019, and 0.99/1.60 = 0.62, giving 0.62 × 1019 = 6.2 × 1018.
N ≈ 6.2 × 1018 electrons
Now redo it: I recomputed this as 6.1875 × 1018, so the slide's 6.2 × 1018 is correct. Note the insight

the slides draw: even a modest current flowing briefly moves an extraordinary number of electrons.

TEACH · CIRCUITS, DC AND AC

When charge flows through a closed path and returns to its starting point, that closed path is an electric circuit.

 - In a direct current (DC) circuit, the current always flows in the same direction. Battery-powered circuits are typically DC.
 - In an alternating current (AC) circuit, the current periodically reverses direction. The electricity from a wall socket is AC.

TEACH · THE BAT TERY, AND WHY ANYTHING FLOWS AT ALL

Electrons move freely in a metal, but something must push them. The slides use a garden hose: hold both ends level and no water flows, because there is no difference in potential energy. Raise one end and water flows from high potential energy to low. A battery does exactly this job in a circuit. A battery uses chemical reactions to produce a difference in electric potential between its two terminals. The positive terminal is at high potential, the negative terminal at low potential. The difference in potential between the terminals is the electromotive force, or emf, given the symbol ε. Despite the name it is not a force: its unit is the volt, the same as potential.

MUST-MEMORISE · WORK DONE BY A BAT TERY

W = (ΔQ) ε

W = work done, in joules (J) ΔQ = charge moved, in coulombs (C) ε = emf of the battery, in volts (V) 

MEMORY HOOK: this is the same W = qV from Module 1. Charge times voltage gives energy, every

single time. The emf determines how much work the battery does to move a given amount of charge around the circuit.

WORKED EXAMPLE · CHARGE AND WORK IN A FLASHLIGHTSLIDE, ACTIVE

A battery of emf 1.5 V delivers a current of 0.44 A to a bulb for 64 s. Find (a) the charge that passes through the circuit and (b) the work done by the battery.

 - (a) Rearrange the definition of current: ΔQ = I Δt = (0.44)(64) = 28 C. 28.16 C exactly, which the slides round to 28 C.
 - (b) Now use W = ΔQ ε = (28)(1.5) = 42 J. Coulombs times volts gives joules.
ΔQ ≈ 28 C  ·  W ≈ 42 J
Insight from the slides: the more charge a battery moves, the more work it does; and the greater the

emf, the more work. So a 12 V car battery delivering several amps does vastly more work than this 1.5 V

flashlight cell, exactly as you would expect.

TRAP · CONVENTIONAL CURRENT RUNS BACKWARDS TO THE ELECTRONS

By convention, the direction of current in a circuit is the direction in which a positive test charge would move: out of the positive terminal, round the circuit, and back into the negative terminal. But the charges that actually move in a metal wire are negatively charged electrons, which leave the negative terminal and return to the positive one. So the electron flow and the current arrow point in opposite directions. This is a historical accident, not a mistake. Every formula in this course uses conventional current, so draw your arrows that way and do not worry about the electrons.

### Resistance and Ohm's law

MUST-MEMORISE · OHM'S LAW

V = IR      I = V/R      R = V/I

V = applied voltage, in volts (V) I = current, in amperes (A) R = resistance, in ohms (Ω), where 1 Ω = 1 V/A

MEMORY HOOK: V = IR. Cover the one you want with your thumb and the formula rearranges

itself. Named for the German physicist Georg Simon Ohm. A device that measures resistance is an ohmmeter.

WORKED EXAMPLE · CURRENT THROUGH A RESISTORSLIDE, QUICK

A potential difference of 24 V is applied to a 150 Ω resistor. How much current flows?

 - Solve Ohm's law for current: I = V/R.
 - I = 24 V / 150 Ω = 0.16 A. Volts divided by ohms gives amps, since 1 Ω = 1 V/A.
I = 0.16 A

TEACH · THE CIRCUIT ELEMENTS AND THEIR SYMBOLS

MUST-MEMORISE · RESISTIVITY: WHAT A WIRE'S RESISTANCE DEPENDS ON

R = ρ L / A

ρ (rho) = resistivity of the material, in ohm metres (Ω m) L = length of the wire, in m A = cross-sectional area of the wire, in m2

MEMORY HOOK: long and thin is high resistance; short and fat is low. Think of water in a hose: a

long narrow hose resists flow, a short wide one does not. Insulators have high resistivity, conductors have low resistivity.

### Semiconductors

TEACH · DOPING, AND THE TWO TYPES OF SEMICONDUCTOR

Ohm's law is an excellent approximation for metal wires and ordinary resistors, but it does not apply to all materials. Semiconductors are an important exception. Elements such as germanium and silicon are insulators in their pure form. But adding a tiny quantity of impurity, a process called doping, turns them into conductors. Doping produces two types of semiconductor.

 - n-type: add arsenic to silicon (roughly one arsenic atom per million silicon atoms) and electrons break free from the arsenic and move through the material. Current is carried by negative electrons, hence the name.
 - p-type: doped the other way, so that current is carried by positive charge carriers.

MUST-MEMORISE · DIODE AND TRANSISTOR

Diode. The simplest semiconductor device: a p-type joined to an n-type. Its basic property is that it allows current to flow in one direction only. Transistor. A sandwich of three layers. The common type has n-type on either side of a thin p-type middle: an npn transistor. Reverse the sequence and you have a pnp transistor. Its three terminals are the collector, the base and the emitter. The basic function of a transistor is to act as an electronic switch that controls the flow of current in a circuit.

MEMORY HOOK: the diode is a one-way valve; the transistor is a switch. Those two devices, made

from doped silicon, are the foundation of every computer ever built.

RECALL AND CHECK

A wire is stretched so that it becomes twice as long and half as thick in cross-sectional area. What happens to its resistance?

Answer

R = ρL/A. Doubling L doubles R. Halving A doubles R again. So the resistance becomes four times as large. Both changes push in the same direction, so they multiply rather than cancel.

FILL IN THE GAP · CURRENT, RESISTANCE AND SEMICONDUCTORS

 - One ampere is one per second.
 - Conventional current runs in the direction to the electron flow.
 - In R = ρL/A, a long thin wire has a resistance.
 - A allows current in one direction only, while a acts as an electronic switch.

Answers

1. coulomb. 1 A = 1 C/s.

2. opposite. A historical accident, but every formula in this course uses conventional current.

3. high (large). L is on top, A is on the bottom.

4. diode, and a transistor. A one-way valve and a switch, which together build every computer.

## Series, parallel and combination circuits

Nearly every circuit question reduces to this: replace groups of resistors with a single equivalent resistor until only one remains, then apply Ohm's law.

MUST-MEMORISE · RESISTORS IN SERIES

Resistors connected end to end form a series circuit. The equivalent resistance is simply the sum:

Req = R1 + R2 + R3 + …

The defining features of a series circuit:

 - The same current flows through every resistor.
 - The voltages across them add up to the supply voltage.
 - Req is greater than the largest individual resistance.

MEMORY HOOK: series adds. More resistors in a line means a longer, harder path, so more

resistance. Two identical resistors R in series give 2R.

MUST-MEMORISE · RESISTORS IN PARALLEL

Resistors connected across the same potential difference form a parallel circuit. The reciprocals add:

1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + …

The defining features of a parallel circuit:

 - The same voltage is across every resistor.
 - The currents through the branches add up to the total current.
 - Req is less than the smallest individual resistance.

MEMORY HOOK: parallel lanes on a motorway. Adding another lane makes it easier for traffic to

get through, so resistance falls. Two identical resistors R in parallel give R/2; three give R/3.

TRAP · DO NOT FORGET TO FLIP THE RECIPROCAL

The parallel formula gives you 1/Req, not Req. You must invert at the end. Stopping one step early is the most common error in the entire course. Free check, every time: a parallel answer must be smaller than the smallest resistor in the group. If it is not, you forgot to flip.

WORKED EXAMPLE · THREE RESISTORS IN SERIESSLIDE, GUIDED

Three resistors are in series across a 24.0 V battery. The current is 0.0320 A. Given R1 = 250.0 Ω and R2 = 150.0 Ω, find (a) R3 and (b) the potential difference across each resistor.
 - Find the total resistance from Ohm's law: Req = ε/I = 24.0 / 0.0320 = 750 Ω. The same current flows through all three, which is the key property of series.
 - In series the resistances simply add, so R3 = Req − R1 − R2 = 750 − 250 − 150 = 350 Ω. One unknown, one equation. Straightforward subtraction.
 - Now the voltage across each, using V = IR with the same I each time. V1 = (0.0320) (250.0) = 8.00 V. This is the whole reason series circuits are easy: I is common to all.
 - V2 = (0.0320)(150.0) = 4.80 V.
 - V3 = (0.0320)(350) = 11.2 V.
R3 = 350 Ω  ·  V1 = 8.00 V, V2 = 4.80 V, V3 = 11.2 V
The check that proves it: the individual voltages must add up to the battery voltage. 8.00 + 4.80 +

11.2 = 24.0 V. Exactly the emf. This check costs five seconds and catches almost every arithmetic slip.

Note also that the largest resistance takes the largest share of the voltage.

WORKED EXAMPLE · THREE RESISTORS IN PARALLELSLIDE, GUIDED

R1 = 250.0 Ω, R2 = 150.0 Ω and R3 = 350.0 Ω are connected in parallel across a 24.0 V battery. Find (a) the total current from the battery and (b) the current through each resistor.
 - Add the reciprocals: 1/Req = 1/250.0 + 1/150.0 + 1/350.0 = 0.004 + 0.006667 + 0.002857 = 0.01352 Ω−1. Three items, so the product-over-sum shortcut is not available. Use reciprocals.
 - Flip it: Req = 1 / 0.01352 = 73.96 Ω. Do not stop at the previous line. Sanity check: 73.96 is less than 150, the smallest resistor. Correct.
 - Total current: I = ε/Req = 24.0 / 73.96 = 0.325 A.
 - Each branch has the full 24.0 V across it, so use I = ε/R for each. I1 = 24.0/250.0 = 0.0960 A. This is the key property of parallel: every branch sees the same voltage.
 - I2 = 24.0/150.0 = 0.160 A, and I3 = 24.0/350.0 = 0.0686 A.
I = 0.325 A total  ·  I1 = 0.0960 A, I2 = 0.160 A, I3 = 0.0686 A
The check that proves it: the branch currents must add to the total. 0.0960 + 0.160 + 0.0686 = 

0.325 A. It matches. Note also that the smallest resistor (150 Ω) carries the largest current, as it must.

### Short circuits

TEACH · WHAT HAPPENS WHEN ONE BRANCH HAS ZERO RESISTANCE

In general the equivalent resistance of a parallel circuit is less than or equal to the smallest individual resistance. So what if one of them is zero? Then Req is also zero, because it cannot be larger than the smallest, and it cannot be negative. This is a short circuit: all the current flows through the path of zero resistance, and none flows through the other branches. They are bypassed completely.

### Combination circuits

MUST-MEMORISE · THE METHOD FOR ANY CIRCUIT

Most real circuits are neither purely series nor purely parallel. The method is always the same:

 - Find a group that is purely series or purely parallel.
 - Replace it with its single equivalent resistance.
 - Redraw the simpler circuit.
 - Repeat until one resistor remains, then use Ohm's law.

MEMORY HOOK: collapse from the inside out. Never try to see the whole circuit at once. Take one

group at a time and redraw after each step. Redrawing is not optional: it is what stops you making mistakes.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FOUR RESISTORS, EACH RSLIDE

A circuit has four resistors each of resistance R, combining series and parallel features. Two are in parallel on the right; those sit in series with two others.

 - Start with the two vertical resistors on the right, which are in parallel. Two identical resistors in parallel give R/2. 1/Req = 1/R + 1/R = 2/R, so Req = R/2.
 - Replace them with a single R/2. The circuit is now three resistors in series: R, then R/ 2, then R. This is why redrawing matters. Once replaced, the structure is obvious.
 - Series resistances add: Req = R + R/2 + R = 2.5R.
Req = 2.5R
Now redo it: the whole technique is visible here. Collapse the parallel pair, redraw, then add the series

chain. Any combination circuit yields to this.

WORKED EXAMPLE · COMBINATION SPECIALSLIDE, GUIDED

A 12 V battery drives three resistors, each 450 Ω. Two are in series with one another, and that pair is in parallel with the third. Find the current supplied by the battery.
 - The two lower resistors are in series: Rlower = R + R = 2R = 900 Ω. Collapse the innermost group first.
 - That 2R is now in parallel with the single upper R. Two items, so use product over sum: Req = (R × 2R) / (R + 2R) = 2R2/3R = (2/3)R. Working in symbols first keeps it clean. Substitute numbers at the end.
 - Req = (2/3)(450) = 300 Ω. Sanity check: 300 Ω is less than 450 Ω, the smallest branch. Correct.
 - Ohm's law for the current from the battery: I = ε/Req = 12 / 300 = 0.040 A.
I = 0.040 A
Note the insight from the slides: the total resistance of three 450 Ω resistors is only 300 Ω, which

is less than any single one of them. That feels wrong until you remember the parallel connection provides

an extra path, and extra paths always reduce total resistance.

RECALL AND CHECK

Two 100 Ω resistors. What is the equivalent resistance in series, and in parallel?

Answer

Series: 100 + 100 = 200 Ω (twice the individual value). Parallel: R/2 = 50 Ω (half the individual value). In series it goes up; in parallel it goes down. Always.

FILL IN THE GAP · SERIES, PARALLEL AND COMBINATION CIRCUITS

 - In series, the is the same through every resistor, and the divide.
 - In parallel, the is the same across every branch, and the divide.
 - To solve a combination circuit you collapse the group first, then the circuit.

Answers

1. current is common; the voltages divide.

2. voltage is common; the currents divide. 

These two lines are the whole of circuit analysis. Everything else follows.

3. innermost (or simplest) group, then redraw. Redrawing is not optional; it is what stops you making mistakes.

## Voltage dividers and current dividers

Two shortcuts that let you find a voltage or a current directly, without computing the whole circuit first. Worth knowing well: they turn several lines of work into one.

MUST-MEMORISE · THE VOLTAGE DIVIDER RULE

The voltage drop across any given resistor in a series circuit equals the ratio of that resistor to the total resistance, multiplied by the source voltage.

V1 = [ R1 / (R1 + R2) ] × V

and correspondingly V2 = [ R2 / (R1 + R2) ] × V. In general, replace the denominator with the total series resistance RT. 

MEMORY HOOK: the resistor you want goes on top; the total goes underneath. The bigger resistor

takes the bigger share of the voltage, which is exactly what you would expect.

WORKED EXAMPLE · VOLTAGE ACROSS R 2SLIDE

A 20 V source drives R1 = 15 kΩ in series with R2 = 10 kΩ. What is the voltage across R2?
 - Total series resistance: RT = 15 + 10 = 25 kΩ. Series, so simply add.
 - Apply the divider, putting the resistor we want (R2) on top: V2 = (10 kΩ / 25 kΩ) × 20 V. The kilo-ohms cancel top and bottom, so no conversion is needed. This is one of the nice features of the divider rule.
 - V2 = (0.4)(20) = 8.0 V.
V2 = 8.0 V
Check it: R1 should then take (15/25) × 20 = 12 V. And 8 + 12 = 20 V, the source voltage. It balances.

R1 is larger, and it took the larger share. That is the sanity check to run every time.

WORKED EXAMPLE · DIVIDER WITH 4 Ω AND 10 ΩCLASSWORK

A 12 V source drives R1 = 4 Ω in series with R2 = 10 Ω. Find the voltage drop across each.

 - RT = 4 + 10 = 14 Ω.
 - V1 = (4/14) × 12 = 48/14 = 3.43 V.
 - V2 = (10/14) × 12 = 120/14 = 8.57 V.
V1 ≈ 3.43 V  ·  V2 ≈ 8.57 V
Check: 3.43 + 8.57 = 12.0 V exactly. And the 10 Ω resistor took the larger share, as it must.

MUST-MEMORISE · THE CURRENT DIVIDER RULE

When current enters a node (junction) it divides into currents that are inversely proportional to the resistance values. For two resistors in parallel:

I1 = [ R2 / (R1 + R2) ] × IT

Note the subscripts carefully. To find the current in R1, the resistor in the numerator is R2, the other one. 

MEMORY HOOK: for current, the opposite resistor goes on top. This is the reverse of the voltage

divider, and it is deliberately so: current takes the path of least resistance, so the smaller resistor gets the larger current. Putting the other resistor on top is what produces that inversion.

TRAP · THE TWO DIVIDERS USE OPPOSITE RESISTORS

This is the single most confusing pair of formulas in the module, so be deliberate:

 - Voltage divider: the resistor you want goes on top. Bigger R means bigger V.
 - Current divider: the other resistor goes on top. Bigger R means smaller I.

If you ever forget which is which, reason it out rather than guessing: current prefers the easy path, so the small resistor must get the big current. Only one of the two arrangements delivers that.

WORKED EXAMPLE · CURRENT DIVIDER, 2.2 KΩ AND 4.7 KΩSLIDE

R1 = 2.2 kΩ is in parallel with R2 = 4.7 kΩ. The total current into the pair is 8.0 mA. Find the current in each resistor.
 - Sum of the resistances: 2.2 + 4.7 = 6.9 kΩ. Both in kΩ, so the units will cancel in the ratio.
 - Current through R1, with the other resistor on top: I1 = (4.7 / 6.9) × 8.0 mA = (0.681) (8.0) = 5.45 mA. R2 on top because we want the current in R1.
 - Current through R2: I2 = (2.2 / 6.9) × 8.0 mA = (0.319)(8.0) = 2.55 mA.
I1 ≈ 5.45 mA (through 2.2 kΩ)  ·  I2 ≈ 2.55 mA (through 4.7 kΩ)
Two checks: first, 5.45 + 2.55 = 8.0 mA, the total. Second, and as the slides point out, the larger

resistor has the smaller current. The 4.7 kΩ carries only 2.55 mA while the 2.2 kΩ carries 5.45 mA. If

your answer has the big resistor carrying the big current, you have put the wrong resistor on top.

WORKED EXAMPLE · TWO MULTIPLE CHOICE QUESTIONSSLIDE, MCQ

A source drives R1 = 100 Ω in parallel with R2 = 200 Ω. Q1: what is the current in R2? Options: 6.7 mA, 13.3 mA, 20 mA, 26.7 mA. Q2: what is the voltage across R2? Options: 0 V, 0.67 V, 1.33 V, 4.0 V.

 - Work backwards from the options to identify the source. If the total current is 20 mA, the current divider gives I2 = (100/300) × 20 = 6.67 mA. The other resistor, 100 Ω, goes on top when finding the current in R2.
 - So I2 ≈ 6.7 mA, which is option (a). Note the larger resistor (200 Ω) gets the smaller current, exactly as required.
 - For the voltage: in parallel, both resistors have the same voltage across them. V = I2R2 = (6.67 × 10−3)(200) = 1.33 V. Which is option (c). Check with the other branch: I1 = (200/300) × 20 = 13.3 mA, and V = (13.3 × 10−3)(100) = 1.33 V. Identical, as it must be for parallel.
Q1: 6.7 mA  ·  Q2: 1.33 V
The check that confirms both: the two branch currents must sum to the total, 6.7 + 13.3 = 20 mA.

And both branches must show the same voltage, which they do at 1.33 V. Two independent confirmations

that the answers are consistent.

FILL IN THE GAP · VOLTAGE AND CURRENT DIVIDERS

 - In the voltage divider, the resistor you goes on top.
 - In the current divider, the resistor goes on top.
 - Current takes the path of least resistance, so the resistor carries the larger current.

Answers

1. want. Bigger R means a bigger share of the voltage.

2. other (the opposite one). This inversion is deliberate.

3. smaller.

If you ever forget which formula is which, reason it out from this fact rather than guessing. Only one arrangement of the ratio gives the small resistor the big current.

## Kirchhoff's laws

When a circuit cannot be reduced by series and parallel rules alone, Kirchhoff's two laws will always solve it. They are the most powerful tool in this module and the most heavily examined.

MUST-MEMORISE · THE THREE DEFINITIONS YOU MUST HAVE FIRST

 - Junction (also called a node or branch point): a point in a circuit where two or more conductors meet.
 - Branch: a single element in the circuit, such as a resistor, a current source or a voltage source.
 - Loop: a collection of branches forming a closed conducting path.

MEMORY HOOK: junctions are where wires meet; loops are closed paths you can walk round and

return to the start. Kirchhoff's first law is about junctions, the second is about loops.

MUST-MEMORISE · KIRCHHOFF'S CURRENT LAW (KCL)

The algebraic sum of the currents entering any junction equals the sum of the currents leaving it.

∑I = 0    or    ∑Iin = ∑Iout

MEMORY HOOK: KCL is a statement of the conservation of charge. Charge cannot pile up at a

junction or vanish there. Whatever flows in must flow out. Think of water pipes meeting at a T-joint.

MUST-MEMORISE · KIRCHHOFF'S VOLTAGE LAW (KVL)

The algebraic sum of the potential differences across all the elements around any closed loop must be zero.

∑V = 0    or    ∑(voltage drops) = ∑(voltage rises)

MEMORY HOOK: KVL is a statement of the conservation of energy. Walk all the way round a

loop and return to where you started, and you must be back at the same potential. Like walking round a hill and returning to your front door: your net change in height is zero, however much you climbed on the way.

### The sign conventions, which are where marks are lost

MUST-MEMORISE · THE FOUR SIGN RULES FOR TRAVERSING A LOOP

First choose a travel direction around the loop. It may be clockwise or anticlockwise; it genuinely does not matter, as long as you are consistent. Then:
MEMORY HOOK: going with the current through a resistor is going downhill in potential, so the

term is negative. Going from minus to plus through a battery is climbing uphill, so it is positive. Voltage is like height, and you are walking a landscape.

TRAP · A NEGATIVE CURRENT IS NOT A MISTAKE

You must assume a current direction before you can write the equations, and you have no way of knowing in advance whether you guessed right. If your assumption was backwards, the algebra returns a negative value. This is not an error, and you should not go back and redo it. It means: the magnitude is correct, but the real current flows in the direction opposite to your arrow. Simply state that. Losing your nerve and re-deriving everything is the actual mistake here.

WORKED EXAMPLE · USING KCL AND KVL TOGETHERSLIDE

A 10 A source feeds a junction. One branch has 8 Ω, the other has 4 Ω. Find the current in each branch and the voltage VAB.
 - By KVL, the two parallel branches must have the same voltage across them, so the loop containing both gives 8I1 = 4I2, hence I2 = 2I1. The 4 Ω branch is half the resistance, so it takes twice the current.
 - By KCL at the junction, the total current in must equal the total out: 10 = I1 + I2. Conservation of charge. Nothing accumulates at the node.
 - Substitute the first equation into the second: 10 = I1 + 2I1 = 3I1. Two equations, two unknowns, solved by substitution, exactly as in Foundations F.5.
 - I1 = 10/3 = 3.33 A, and therefore I2 = 2(3.33) = 6.67 A. The smaller resistor carries the larger current, as always in parallel.
 - Voltage across the pair: VAB = I1 × 8 = (3.33)(8) = 26.67 V.
I1 = 3.33 A  ·  I2 = 6.67 A  ·  VAB = 26.67 V
Two checks: the currents sum to 3.33 + 6.67 = 10 A, matching the source. And the other branch gives

V = (6.67)(4) = 26.67 V, the same voltage. Both branches agree, which is exactly what parallel demands.

 - Label the currents. I1 is the current from the source, I2 and I3 are the currents in the two branches. Assume a clockwise traverse. Three unknowns, so we will need three equations.
 - First loop (KVL): −50 + I1(10) + I2(8) = 0. The battery is a rise, so it enters negative when we traverse it this way; the resistors are drops. This is equation (a).
 - Second loop (KVL): −I2(8) + I3(6) + I3(4) = 0. We pass through the 8 Ω against the assumed current, so it is positive there and negative here. This is equation (b), and it gives 10I3 = 8I2.
 - At the junction (KCL): I1 = I2 + I3. Conservation of charge. This is equation (c). Now three equations, three unknowns, so the system is solvable.
 - Substitute (c) into (a) to eliminate I1: −50 + (I2 + I3)(10) + I2(8) = 0, which tidies to 18I2 + 10I3 = 50. Expand the bracket: 10I2 + 10I3, then add the 8I2.
 - From (b), 10I3 = 8I2, so I2 = 1.25 I3. Substitute this into the previous line: 18(1.25 I3) + 10I3 = 50. Reduce to one unknown, which is always the goal.
 - 22.5 I3 + 10I3 = 32.5 I3 = 50, so I3 = 50/32.5 = 1.538 A.
 - Back-substitute: I2 = 1.25(1.538) = 1.923 A. Always go back and get the others. Do not stop at one current.
 - And from (c): I1 = I2 + I3 = 1.923 + 1.538 = 3.461 A.
 - Finally the voltages, using V = IR on each resistor: 10 Ω carries I1; 8 Ω carries I2; 6 Ω and 4 Ω carry I3.

MUST-MEMORISE · THE KIRCHHOFF RECIPE

 - Label every current and assume a direction for each.
 - Choose a travel direction for each loop and stick to it.
 - Write one KVL equation per loop, using the four sign rules.
 - Write KCL at the junctions to link the currents.
 - Count: you need as many equations as unknowns.
 - Solve by substitution.
 - Check by walking each loop and confirming the voltages sum to zero.

MEMORY HOOK: label, loop, junction, solve, check. Every Kirchhoff question in every exam yields

to exactly this sequence. The physics is in steps 3 and 4; the rest is bookkeeping and algebra.

RECALL AND CHECK

Which conservation law does each of Kirchhoff's laws express?

Answer

KCL (junctions) expresses conservation of charge: charge cannot accumulate at a point. KVL (loops) expresses conservation of energy: return to your starting point and you are back at the same potential. They are not arbitrary circuit rules. They are two of the deepest laws in physics, applied to wires.

FILL IN THE GAP · KIRCHHOFF'S LAWS

 - Kirchhoff's current law expresses the conservation of .
 - Kirchhoff's voltage law expresses the conservation of .
 - Travelling with the assumed current through a resistor, the IR term is .
 - Travelling through a source from minus to plus, the emf is .

Answers

1. charge. Nothing accumulates at a junction.

2. energy. Walk a loop, return to the start, and you are back at the same potential.

3. negative. Going with the current is going downhill in potential.

4. positive. Minus to plus is climbing uphill. 

Voltage behaves like height, and you are walking a landscape. That picture gets all four sign rules right without memorising a table.
