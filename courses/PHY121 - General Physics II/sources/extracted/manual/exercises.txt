Q11 · FIELD INSIDE A UNIFORMLY CHARGED SOLID SPHERE

A solid sphere of radius 0.10 m has a uniform charge density ρ = 2 × 10−6 C/m3. Find the field at r = 0.05 m from the centre.

 - Note carefully: r = 0.05 m is less than R = 0.10 m, so the point is inside the sphere. This changes everything. This is the whole point of the question. A Gaussian surface at r = 0.05 m encloses only part of the total charge, not all of it.
 - Find the charge enclosed by a Gaussian sphere of radius r. Charge density times the volume of that inner sphere: Qenc = ρ × (4/3)πr3. Only the charge inside the Gaussian surface counts. The charge in the outer shell contributes nothing, by Gauss's law.
 - Qenc = (2 × 10−6)(4/3)π(0.05)3 = (2 × 10−6)(5.236 × 10−4) = 1.047 × 10−9 C. (0.05)3 = 1.25 × 10−4, and (4/3)π = 4.189, so the volume is 5.236 × 10−4 m3.
 - Apply Gauss's law: E(4πr2) = Qenc/ε0. The area is 4π(0.05)2 = 3.142 × 10−2 m2.
 - E = Qenc / (ε0 × 4πr2) = (1.047 × 10−9) / [(8.85 × 10−12)(3.142 × 10−2)] Denominator: 2.781 × 10−13.
 - E = 3.77 × 103 N/C.
E ≈ 3.8 × 103 N/C, directed radially outward
The physics to take away: if you simply substitute the total charge into E = kQ/r2 you get a badly

wrong answer. Inside a uniformly charged solid sphere the field grows linearly with r (it is proportional

to r, since r3/r2 = r), reaching a maximum at the surface and only then falling off as 1/r2. At the exact

centre, the field is zero.

Q12 · FIELD OUTSIDE A CHARGED CONDUCTING SPHERE

A conducting sphere of radius 0.25 m carries a total charge of 6 × 10−6 C. Find the field at 0.50 m from the centre.

 - Here r = 0.50 m is greater than R = 0.25 m, so the point is outside the sphere. Compare with Q11, where the point was inside. Always check this first.
 - Outside a charged sphere, the whole charge behaves exactly as though concentrated at the centre, so use the point charge result: E = kQ/r2. This is the Gauss's law result from Unit 1.4. The Gaussian sphere encloses the entire charge.
 - E = (8.99 × 109)(6 × 10−6) / (0.50)2.
 - Numerator: 5.394 × 104. Denominator: (0.50)2 = 0.25. Not 0.5. Squaring a half gives a quarter.
 - E = 5.394 × 104 / 0.25 = 2.16 × 105 N/C.
E ≈ 2.16 × 105 N/C, radially outward
Contrast Q11 and Q12 side by side. They look almost identical, but one is inside and one is

outside, and the method is completely different. Always compare r with R before choosing your approach.

Note too that if this question had asked for the field inside the conducting sphere, the answer would be

exactly zero.

Q1 · THE CAPACITOR NETWORK

Q1. Determine the total capacitance of the capacitors shown, where C₁ = 1 µF and C₂ = 5 µF are stacked in one branch, and C₃ = 8 µF is in the other branch.
 - Read the circuit. C1 and C2 are stacked one above the other in the same branch, so they are in series with each other. That branch is then in parallel with C3. Getting the topology right is the whole question. Everything else is arithmetic.
 - Series pair first, using product over sum since there are exactly two: C12 = (1 × 5)/(1 + 5) = 5/6 = 0.833 µF. Sanity check: smaller than the smallest (1 µF), as a series combination must be.
 - Now add C3 in parallel. Capacitors in parallel simply add: Ctotal = 0.833 + 8 = 8.83 µF.
Ctotal ≈ 8.83 µF
Note the trap: if you had wrongly treated C1 and C2 as parallel you would get 1 + 5 = 6, then a series

with 8 giving 3.43 µF, which is quite different. Read the circuit carefully before you compute anything.

Q2 · CAPACITANCE FROM STORED ENERGY

Q2. What is the capacitance of a capacitor having a stored energy of 2.25 mJ after being charged with a 30 volt source?

 - We know energy and voltage, and want C. Choose the energy form that contains exactly those: W = ½CV2. Three forms exist. Pick the one matching your known quantities.
 - Rearrange: C = 2W / V2. Multiply by 2 first, then divide by V squared. See Foundations F.2.
 - Convert: W = 2.25 mJ = 2.25 × 10−3 J. Milli means 10−3.
 - C = 2(2.25 × 10−3) / (30)2 = (4.5 × 10−3) / 900. 302 = 900, not 60. Square it, do not double it.
 - C = 5 × 10−6 F.
C = 5 × 10−6 F = 5 µF
Check by working forwards: W = ½(5 × 10−6)(900) = 2.25 × 10−3 J. That is 2.25 mJ, as given.

Confirmed.

Q3 · PARALLEL PLATE CAPACITOR WITH A DIELECTRIC

Q3. The area of each plate of a parallel plate capacitor is 4 × 10−2 m2. The dielectric thickness between the plates is 10−3 m and the relative permittivity is 7. What is the capacitance?

 - With a dielectric present, use C = εrε0A / d. The relative permittivity εr = 7 multiplies the vacuum result.
 - Numerator: εrε0A = (7)(8.85 × 10−12)(4 × 10−2). 7 × 8.85 = 61.95, so 61.95 × 10−12. Times 4 × 10−2 gives 247.8 × 10−14 = 2.478 × 10−12.
 - Divide by d = 10−3 m: C = (2.478 × 10−12) / (10−3). Dividing by 10−3 means adding 3 to the exponent.
 - C = 2.478 × 10−9 F.
C ≈ 2.48 × 10−9 F  (about 2.5 nF)
Now redo it: the dielectric multiplied the capacitance by 7. Without it, C would be only 3.54 × 10−10 F.

Dielectrics always increase capacitance, never decrease it.

Q4 · CURRENT THROUGH A SERIES RESISTOR

Q4. R₁, R₂ and R₃ are in series with a 48 V source, where R₁ = 7 Ω, R₂ = 6 Ω and R₃ = 3 Ω. Calculate the current through R₃.

 - All three resistors are in series, so add them: Rtotal = 7 + 6 + 3 = 16 Ω. Series simply adds.
 - Ohm's law for the whole circuit: I = V / Rtotal = 48 / 16 = 3 A.
 - The question asks for the current through R3 specifically. In a series circuit, the same current flows through every element. This is the trick in the question. There is nothing extra to calculate. The current through R3 is the circuit current.
I = 3 A through R3 (and through R1 and R2 as well)
Check: the voltage drops are (3)(7) = 21 V, (3)(6) = 18 V and (3)(3) = 9 V. Sum: 21 + 18 + 9 = 48 V, the

source voltage. It balances.

Q5 · CAPACITANCE OF A CHARGED SPHERE

Q5. A metal sphere on an insulating rod carries a charge of 4.5 nC when its potential is 180 V higher than its surroundings. What is the capacitance of the capacitor formed by the sphere and its surroundings?

 - Use the definition of capacitance directly: C = Q/V. We are given a charge and a potential, so no geometry is needed at all.
 - Convert: Q = 4.5 nC = 4.5 × 10−9 C. Nano means 10−9.
 - C = (4.5 × 10−9) / 180. The potential is 180 V above its surroundings, which is the potential difference we need.
 - C = 2.5 × 10−11 F.
C = 2.5 × 10−11 F = 25 pF
Now redo it: notice that the sphere and its surroundings form a capacitor, even though it looks nothing

like two parallel plates. Any two conductors at different potentials form a capacitor. That is the definition.

Q6 · VOLTAGE ACROSS R 2 WHEN R 2 = 2R 1

This is a proportional reasoning question. No numbers are given for the resistors, and none are needed.

 - Let R1 = R. Then R2 = 2R. Introducing a symbol lets us work without knowing the actual value.
 - They are in series, so the total is RT = R + 2R = 3R.
 - Voltage divider, with the resistor we want on top: V2 = [2R / 3R] × Vs. The R cancels completely, which is why no numerical value was needed.
 - V2 = (2/3)Vs.
V2 = two thirds of the source voltage
Check: R1 then takes (1/3)Vs, and 2/3 + 1/3 = 1, the whole source voltage. The larger resistor takes the

larger share, exactly as the divider rule requires. If Vs were 12 V, then V2 = 8 V.

Q7 · THE KIRCHHOFF TWO-LOOP PROBLEM

The full worked solution, with all three currents and all four voltages, is given in Unit 3.4. Rather than repeat it, here is the checklist to apply to any Kirchhoff question of this type. Work the problem in Unit 3.4 from scratch on blank paper, then compare.

 - Label every current with an arrow. Guess the directions; the algebra will correct you if you are wrong.
 - Write one KVL equation per loop, using the four sign rules from Unit 3.4.
 - Write KCL at a junction to link the currents together.
 - Count equations against unknowns. They must be equal, or the system cannot be solved.
 - Solve by substitution, reducing to one unknown at a time.
 - Check by walking each loop and confirming the voltages sum to zero.
Answers (from Unit 3.4): I1 = 3.461 A, I2 = 1.923 A, I3 = 1.538 A. Voltages: 34.61 V, 15.38 V, 9.23 V and 6.15 V.
Remember: a negative current is not an error. It means the true direction is opposite to the arrow you

drew. Report the magnitude and say so.

Q1 · PROTON ENTERING PERPENDICULAR TO THE FIELD

Q1. A proton enters a magnetic field of 0.5 T perpendicular to the field at 2 × 106 m/s. Calculate (a) the magnetic force, (b) the radius of its path, and (c) the period.

 - (a) The force. Perpendicular entry means θ = 90° and sin 90° = 1, so F = qvB. This is the maximum possible force.
 - F = (1.6 × 10−19)(2 × 106)(0.5). Multiply the first two: 3.2 × 10−13. Then times 0.5. Fronts: 1.6 × 2 = 3.2. Exponents: −19 + 6 = −13.
 - F = 1.6 × 10−13 N.
 - (b) The radius. Use r = mv/(qB). Numerator: (1.67 × 10−27)(2 × 106) = 3.34 × 10−21. The proton mass, not the electron mass. Check which particle you are given.
 - Denominator: qB = (1.6 × 10−19)(0.5) = 8.0 × 10−20.
 - r = (3.34 × 10−21) / (8.0 × 10−20) = 4.18 × 10−2 m (about 4.2 cm). 3.34/8.0 = 0.4175, and −21 − (−20) = −1, so 0.4175 × 10−1 = 4.18 × 10−2.
 - (c) The period. The period is the time for one full circle: T = 2πm/(qB), or equivalently T = 2πr/v. Use the second form, since we already have r.
 - T = 2π(4.18 × 10−2) / (2 × 106) = (0.2626) / (2 × 106) = 1.31 × 10−7 s.
(a) F = 1.6 × 10−13 N  ·  (b) r ≈ 4.18 × 10−2 m  ·  (c) T ≈ 1.31 × 10−7 s
Cross-check the period the other way: T = 2πm/(qB) = 2π(1.67 × 10−27)/(8.0 × 10−20) = 1.31 ×

10−7 s. Identical. Note the period does not depend on the speed, which is the cyclotron principle from

Unit 4.1.

Q2 · ELECTRON AT 45 DEGREES: WHY THE PATH IS A HELIX

This question asks you to show something, so the reasoning matters more than the arithmetic.

 - Resolve the velocity into two components (Foundations F.3): vparallel = v cos 45° and vperp = v sin 45°. cos 45° = sin 45° = 0.707, so at exactly 45° the two components are equal.
 - The perpendicular component feels a force F = qvperpB, always at right angles to the motion. That produces circular motion in the plane perpendicular to B. A constant force at right angles to velocity is precisely centripetal force.
 - The parallel component feels no force at all, because sin 0 = 0. So it continues unchanged and constant, carrying the particle steadily forward along the field. This is the crucial insight. The magnetic field cannot touch the parallel component.
 - Combine them: a circle in one plane, plus a constant drift at right angles to that plane. A circle plus a steady forward drift is a helix. This is what was to be shown. Like the thread of a screw, or a stretched spring.
 - Radius: only the perpendicular component produces the circle, so r = mvperp / (qB) = m(v sin 45°) / (qB). Use v sin θ, not v. Using the full v here is the classic error.
 - Pitch: the pitch is the forward distance travelled during one complete revolution. So it is the parallel speed multiplied by the period: p = vparallel × T = (v cos 45°)(2πm / qB).
r = m v sin θ / (qB)   ·   pitch = v cos θ × (2πm / qB) 
The idea to remember: sin for the radius, cos for the pitch. The perpendicular part curls the

particle round; the parallel part pushes it along. Every helix question in this course is these two formulas.

Q3 · PROTON AT 60 DEGREES: FORCE, RADIUS AND PITCH

v = 3.5 × 106 m/s, B = 0.25 T, θ = 60°, m = 1.67 × 10−27 kg, q = 1.6 × 10−19 C.

 - (a) Force. F = qvB sin θ, with sin 60° = 0.866. One of the four values from Foundations F.4.
 - qvB = (1.6 × 10−19)(3.5 × 106)(0.25). First two: 5.6 × 10−13. Times 0.25: 1.4 × 10−13.
 - F = (1.4 × 10−13)(0.866) = 1.21 × 10−13 N.
 - (b) Radius, using only the perpendicular component: vperp = v sin 60° = (3.5 × 106) (0.866) = 3.031 × 106 m/s. Sin for the radius. This is the component that curls.
 - r = m vperp / (qB) = (1.67 × 10−27)(3.031 × 106) / [(1.6 × 10−19)(0.25)] Numerator: 5.062 × 10−21. Denominator: 4.0 × 10−20.
 - r = 0.127 m (about 12.7 cm).
 - (c) Pitch. First the period: T = 2πm/(qB) = 2π(1.67 × 10−27) / (4.0 × 10−20) = 2.623 × 10−7 s.
 - Parallel speed: vpar = v cos 60° = (3.5 × 106)(0.5) = 1.75 × 106 m/s. Cos for the pitch, and cos 60° = 0.5 exactly.
 - Pitch = vpar × T = (1.75 × 106)(2.623 × 10−7) = 0.459 m.
(a) F ≈ 1.21 × 10−13 N  ·  (b) r ≈ 0.127 m  ·  (c) pitch ≈ 0.459 m
Now redo it: the single most common mistake here is using the full speed v in the radius formula

instead of v sin θ. That would give r = 0.146 m, which is wrong. Only the perpendicular component makes

the circle.

Q4 · CLASS DRILL: FIND THE INTERNAL RESISTANCE

Q4. A wire of 4 Ω is connected to a battery of emf 6 V and internal resistance r. The current flowing is 1 A. Calculate r.

 - Use E = I(R + r), the emf equation from Unit 4.4.
 - Substitute: 6 = 1 × (4 + r). E = 6 V, I = 1 A, R = 4 Ω.
 - So 4 + r = 6, giving r = 6 − 4 = 2 Ω.
r = 2 Ω
Check: the terminal voltage is IR = (1)(4) = 4 V, and the voltage lost inside the cell is Ir = (1)(2) = 2 V.

Together, 4 + 2 = 6 V, the emf. It balances.

What is the absolute potential at r = 5 cm and r = 20 cm from a charge of +4 µC in air?

Q5 · ELECTRIC FLUXANSWER: C

A uniform field E = 200 N/C passes through a surface of area 2 m2 at 0°. Find the flux.

a. 450 · b. 300 · c. 400 · d. 120 · e. 100  (all N m2/C)

 - Φ = EA cos θ. The flux formula from §1.4.
 - θ = 0°, so cos 0° = 1. The field passes straight through the surface, giving maximum flux.
 - Φ = (200)(2)(1) = 400.
Φ = 400 N m2/C  (option c)   Method: §1.4

Q8 · FIELD AT THE MIDPOINT BETWEEN OPPOSITE CHARGESANSWER: C

Q₁ = +3.0×10−6 C and Q₂ = −3.0×10−6 C are 0.10 m apart. Find the net field at the midpoint.

a. 1.99×107 · b. 1.08×107 · c. 2.16×107 · d. 3.0×107 · e. 1.07×107  (all N/C)

 - The midpoint is 0.05 m from each charge. Half of 0.10 m.
 - The key insight. The field from the positive charge points away from it, that is, toward the negative charge. The field from the negative charge points toward it, which is the same direction. So the two fields add. Sketch the two arrows. Three seconds of drawing prevents this error forever.
 - Each contributes E = kQ/r2 = (8.99 × 109)(3 × 10−6) / (0.05)2. (0.05)2 = 2.5 × 10−3.
 - E = (2.697 × 104) / (2.5 × 10−3) = 1.08 × 107 N/C from each. Note this is exactly option (b), which is the distractor placed there for people who forget to add the two contributions.
 - Add them: Enet = 1.08 × 107 + 1.08 × 107 = 2.16 × 107 N/C. Enet = 2.16 × 107 N/C, directed from the positive toward the negative charge  (option c)   Method: §1.2

TRAP · OPPOSITE CHARGES ADD AT THE MIDPOINT; LIKE CHARGES CANCEL

This catches people constantly, and option (b) was placed there deliberately to catch you.

 - Two opposite charges (+ and −): at the midpoint both fields point the same way, from + toward −, so they ADD. The answer is twice one field.
 - Two like charges (both +): at the midpoint the fields point in opposite directions and CANCEL exactly. The answer is zero, a null point.

Always sketch the two arrows before adding.

Q5 · AMPERE'S LAW IS MOST USEFUL FOR CONDUCTORS WITH HIGH ______ FILL IN THE BLANK

Q5. Ampere's law is most useful for conductors with high ________. (Fill in the blank.)

 - Ampere's law is ∮B · dl = µ0Ienc. The integral is only tractable if B is constant along the loop you choose. From §4.2: the whole trick is choosing an Amperian loop on which B does not vary.
 - That is only possible when the current distribution has a high degree of symmetry: a straight wire, a solenoid, a toroid. The slides say it directly: Ampere formulated the law to simplify calculations for systems with high symmetry.
The answer is SYMMETRY.   Method: §4.2
Compare Gauss's law, which works the same way in electrostatics: choose a surface matching the

symmetry and the integral collapses to a simple product. Same idea, two different laws.

Q6 · TRANSFORMER EFFICIENCYANSWER: A

a. 80% · b. 60% · c. 40% · d. 20% · e. 0%

 - Power in is primary voltage times primary current: Pin = 240 × 1 = 240 W. P = VI on the primary side.
 - Efficiency = (Pout / Pin) × 100% = (192 / 240) × 100%.

Q11 · A CHANGING MAGNETIC ______ INDUCES AN EMFFILL IN THE BLANK
