## Forces in nature and the idea of charge

Learning outcomes: identify the four fundamental forces; explain electric charge and its quantisation; describe charging by friction, conduction and induction.

TEACH · THE FOUR FUNDAMENTAL FORCES

Every interaction in the universe, from a balloon sticking to a wall to a star holding itself together, is produced by just four forces.
The electromagnetic force is the one we meet most in daily life. It is responsible for electricity, magnetism and light, and it is what PHY121 studies. Gravity is the weakest of the four, which sounds absurd when it holds you to the Earth. The reason it feels dominant is that it never cancels: mass only attracts. Electric forces are vastly stronger but positive and negative charge almost perfectly balance in ordinary matter, so their effects mostly cancel and we barely notice them. 

TEACH · WHAT ELECTROSTATICS IS

Electrostatics is the study of electric charges at rest, and of the forces and fields they produce. The charges are not moving, yet they still push and pull on each other across empty space. That interaction is the electric force. At the heart of it is electric charge, a basic property of matter that comes in two kinds, positive and negative.

MUST-MEMORISE · THE FIRST LAW OF CHARGE

Like charges repel. Unlike charges attract.

MEMORY HOOK: opposites attract. Everything electrostatic follows from this one sentence plus an

inverse square law.

TEACH · CHARGE, SYMBOL AND UNIT

Electric charge is the property of matter that causes it to experience a force in an electric field.

 - Symbol: Q or q
 - Unit: the coulomb, C
 - Positive charge is carried by protons.
 - Negative charge is carried by electrons.

### The six properties of charge

TEACH · PROPERTIES 1 AND 2: ADDITIVITY AND CONSERVATION

1. Additivity. Total charge is just the algebraic sum of the individual charges, signs included:

Qtotal = q1 + q2 + q3 + …

So +5 C combined with −3 C gives +2 C. You add them like ordinary signed numbers, because charge is a scalar.

2. Conservation. Charge cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one body to another. The total charge of an isolated system never changes.

When you rub a balloon on your hair, you do not manufacture charge. Electrons move from your hair to the balloon. The balloon gains exactly as much negative charge as the hair loses. The books always balance.

MUST-MEMORISE · PROPERTY 3: QUANTISATION OF CHARGE

Charge does not come in arbitrary amounts. It comes in whole multiples of one fundamental lump:

q = n e   where   e = 1.602 × 10−19 C

n is an integer (…, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …) and e is the elementary charge, the magnitude of the charge on one electron or one proton. 

MEMORY HOOK: charge comes in coins, not in liquid. You can have 3 coins or 4 coins, never 3.7

coins. A charge of 1.5e simply cannot exist.

TEACH · PROPERTIES 4, 5 AND 6

4. Invariance. The magnitude of a charge does not depend on its speed, on the frame you observe it from, or on temperature and pressure. A charge is the same whether it is moving or at rest. (Mass, by contrast, does change with speed at high velocities. Charge does not.)

5. Charge is a scalar. It has magnitude only, and no direction. The plus or minus sign indicates the type of charge, not a direction in space.

6. Transferability. Charge can be moved between bodies by friction, by conduction, or by induction. These are the three methods of charging, covered next.

TRAP · THE SIGN ON A CHARGE IS NOT A DIRECTION

Because charge is a scalar, you add charges as signed numbers. But force is a vector, so you cannot add forces as signed numbers unless they lie on the same line. Students routinely add up the forces from several charges as if they were numbers. They are not. See Unit 1.2 on superposition.

### The three ways to charge a body

TEACH · FRICTION, CONDUCTION AND INDUCTION

(a) Charging by friction. Rub two different materials together and electrons transfer from one to the other. One becomes positive, the other equally negative. This is the balloon-and-hair case. (b) Charging by conduction. Touch a charged object to a neutral one. Charge flows across the contact, and both objects end up with the same sign of charge, sharing what was there. (c) Charging by induction. Bring a charged object near a neutral one, without touching. The charges inside the neutral object rearrange: the opposite kind is pulled towards the near side, the same kind is pushed to the far side. If you now earth the far side and remove it, the object is left with a net charge opposite to the one you brought near, and you never touched it.

RECALL AND CHECK

A body carries a charge of 4.8 × 10−19 C. How many elementary charges is that, and is such a charge possible?

Answer

n = q/e = (4.8 × 10−19) / (1.602 × 10−19) = 3.0. That is a whole number, so yes: this is 3 elementary charges. A charge of, say, 2.4 e would be impossible, because charge is quantised.

## Coulomb's law and the electric field

Learning outcomes: state and apply Coulomb's law; calculate forces between multiple charges using superposition; define and interpret electric field lines.

MUST-MEMORISE · COULOMB'S LAW

The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them:

F = k · q1q2 / r2

F is the force in newtons (N) q1, q2 are the two charges in coulombs (C) r is the separation in metres (m) k = 8.99 × 109 N m2 C−2, the Coulomb constant, where k = 1 / (4πε0) ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F m−1, the permittivity of free space 

MEMORY HOOK: product of charges on top, distance squared underneath. It has exactly the same

shape as Newton's law of gravitation, with charge in place of mass.

TEACH · READING THE INVERSE SQUARE

Because r is squared on the bottom, distance matters enormously:

 - Double the separation, and the force falls to a quarter.
 - Triple the separation, and the force falls to a ninth.
 - Halve the separation, and the force becomes four times larger.

The sign of the answer tells you the nature of the force: a positive product q1q2 means repulsion, a negative product means attraction.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FORCE BETWEEN TWO PROTONS 5 M APARTSLIDE

The question. Two protons are 5.0 m apart. Calculate the electrostatic force between them, and state whether it is attractive or repulsive.

 - Write down what you know. Each proton carries q = 1.6 × 10−19 C, and r = 5.0 m. Both charges are identical, so q1q2 = (1.6 × 10−19)2.
 - Substitute into Coulomb's law: F = (8.99 × 109)(1.6 × 10−19)2 / (5.0)2Units are already SI: metres and coulombs. No conversion needed.
 - Square the charge: (1.6 × 10−19)2 = 2.56 × 10−38. Square the distance: 5.02 = 25. Squaring doubles the exponent: −19 becomes −38.
 - F = (8.99 × 109)(2.56 × 10−38) / 25 Now it is just arithmetic.
F ≈ 9.2 × 10−30 N  (repulsive, since both are positive)
Now redo it: I recomputed this independently and obtained 9.206 × 10−30 N, so 9.2 × 10−30 N is

correct to two significant figures. Note how tiny it is: this is why the electric force between individual

particles is irrelevant at everyday distances, yet decisive inside an atom.

TEACH · SUPERPOSITION: MORE THAN TWO CHARGES

Coulomb's law handles exactly two charges. For three or more, use the principle of superposition: Work out the force from each other charge separately, one at a time, as if the others were not there. Then add those forces as vectors. That is the entire method. The only difficulty is the vector addition, which is exactly why Foundations F.3 exists.

MUST-MEMORISE · THE PRINCIPLE OF SUPERPOSITION

Fnet = F1 + F2 + F3 + …   (added as vectors)

MEMORY HOOK: one at a time, then add. Superposition is the most reusable idea in this course. It

works for forces, for electric fields, and for potentials. The only difference is that potentials are scalars, so for those you add plain numbers.

### The electric field

TEACH · WHY WE NEED A FIELD AT ALL

Coulomb's law says two charges push on each other across empty space. But how does one charge know the other is there? The field is the answer, and it is a genuinely useful change of viewpoint. We say a charge alters the space around it, creating an electric field. Any other charge placed in that space then feels a force from the field at its own location. The field is the middleman, and it exists whether or not there is a second charge there to feel it.

MUST-MEMORISE · ELECTRIC FIELD: DEFINITION AND THE FIELD OF A POINT CHARGE

The electric field is the force per unit positive charge:

E = F / q    so    F = qE

For a single point charge Q, the field at distance r is:

E = kQ / r2 = Q / (4πε0r2)

E is the electric field, in newtons per coulomb (N/C) or volts per metre (V/m). These two units are identical. 

MEMORY HOOK: the field is Coulomb's law with one of the charges removed. Take F = kq1q2/r2,

divide out one charge, and you have the field the other one makes.

TEACH · ELECTRIC FIELD LINES AND HOW TO READ THEM

Field lines are a picture of the field. They obey strict rules:

 - They point in the direction of the force on a positive test charge.
 - They begin on positive charges and end on negative charges. Positive charges are sources, negative charges are sinks.
 - Where the lines are close together the field is strong; where they spread out it is weak.
 - They never cross. If they did, the field would have two directions at one point, which is meaningless.

So the field points away from an isolated positive charge and towards an isolated negative charge.

Question (slides): What is the direction of the field 1 m away from a 1 C positive charge, in or out? What force would a 1.4 × 10−5 C negative charge experience at 1 m from it, and is that force towards or away?

 - Field strength at r = 1 m: E = kQ/r2 = (8.99 × 109)(1) / 12 = 8.99 × 109 N/C. A colossal field. A charge of one full coulomb is enormous.
 - Force on the test charge: F = qE = (1.4 × 10−5)(8.99 × 109) Use the magnitude of the charge for the size, then reason separately about direction.
 - F = 1.259 × 105 N. Multiply the fronts (1.4 × 8.99 = 12.59), then add exponents (−5 + 9 = 4), giving 12.59 × 104 = 1.259 × 105.
 - Direction of the force: the test charge is negative and the source is positive, so they attract. The force is towards the 1 C charge. Note this is opposite to the field direction, exactly as expected for a negative charge: F = qE with q negative flips the direction.
Field: outward. Force ≈ 1.26 × 105 N, directed toward the 1 C charge.
Now redo it: the key idea to carry away is that the field points outward, but the force on a negative

WORKED EXAMPLE · TWO POINT CHARGES, 1.0 CM APARTCLASSWORK

Question (slides): Point charge A has charge −1.0 nC and point charge B has charge 4.0 nC. They are separated by 1.0 cm. Find the magnitude and direction of the electric force on each.

 - Substitute magnitudes into Coulomb's law: F = (8.99 × 109)(1.0 × 10−9)(4.0 × 10−9) / (0.01)2Use magnitudes for the size. Decide the direction afterwards from the signs.
 - Numerator: (8.99 × 109)(4.0 × 10−18) = 3.596 × 10−8. (1.0 × 10−9)(4.0 × 10−9) = 4.0 × 10−18: multiply fronts, add exponents.
 - Denominator: (0.01)2 = 1 × 10−4. Squaring a small number makes it much smaller, which makes the force much larger.
 - F = (3.596 × 10−8) / (1 × 10−4) = 3.6 × 10−4 N. Dividing, so subtract exponents: −8 − (−4) = −4.
 - Direction: one charge is negative and the other positive, so the force is attractive. Each charge is pulled towards the other.
F = 3.6 × 10−4 N on each charge, attractive.
Now redo it, and notice the physics: the forces on A and B are equal in magnitude and opposite in

direction, even though B carries four times the charge of A. That is Newton's third law, and it holds no

matter how unequal the charges are. Students very often expect the bigger charge to feel the bigger

force. It does not.

TRAP · THE BIGGER CHARGE DOES NOT FEEL A BIGGER FORCE

In Coulomb's law the force depends on the product q1q2, which is the same number for both charges. So both charges always feel exactly the same size of force, in opposite directions, regardless of how lopsided the charges are. Only the accelerations differ, because the masses differ.

## Electric potential and energy

Learning outcomes: compute the electric potential due to point charges; relate the electric field to the potential gradient; calculate work done in moving charges.

TEACH · THE GRAVITATIONAL ANALOGY, WHICH IS EXACT ENOUGH TO TRUST

Lift a book onto a shelf and you do work against gravity. The book now stores gravitational potential energy: let it go and the energy comes back as motion. Electric potential energy works identically. Push a positive charge towards another positive charge and you do work against the repulsion. The charge now stores electric potential energy: let it go and it flies away, converting that energy into kinetic energy. The height of the shelf is the analogue of potential. The book on the shelf is the analogue of potential energy. Note the difference carefully: the shelf has a height whether or not a book sits on it.

MUST-MEMORISE · POTENTIAL ENERGY, POTENTIAL, AND THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THEM

Electric potential energy is the work done in bringing a charge from infinity to a point in the field:

U = kQq / r   (joules, J)

Electric potential is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing it from infinity to that point:

V = kQ / r   (volts, V)

and therefore

U = qV    and    W = qV

MEMORY HOOK: potential is per unit charge. It belongs to the point in space, and exists even with

no charge there. Potential energy needs an actual charge to be sitting at that point. Shelf height versus book on shelf.

TRAP · POTENTIAL HAS R, NOT R SQUARED

Compare carefully:

E = kQ / r2    but    V = kQ / r

The field has r squared. The potential has plain r. Mixing these up is one of the most common errors in this module. Also remember that E is a vector but V is a scalar: to combine potentials from several charges you simply add the numbers, signs included, with no vector work at all. That makes potential much easier to handle than field, which is precisely why it is worth having.

MUST-MEMORISE · POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE AND WORK

The potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving a charge from one to the other. The work done in moving a charge q through a potential difference V is:

W = qV

MEMORY HOOK: work equals charge times voltage. This one formula reappears in Module 3 as

the work done by a battery, and in Module 4 for emf. It is the same idea each time.

TEACH · THE LINK BETWEEN FIELD AND POTENTIAL

The electric field is the negative gradient of the potential:

E = − dV / dr

In words: the field points in the direction in which the potential falls most steeply, and its strength is the steepness of that fall. Water flows downhill fastest where the hill is steepest. A positive charge is pushed "downhill" in potential in exactly the same way. The minus sign encodes "downhill". For a uniform field between parallel plates separated by distance d this simplifies to the very useful:

E = V / d

This is why the electric field can be measured in volts per metre.

WORKED EXAMPLE · POTENTIAL 2 M FROM A 5 × 10 −6 C CHARGECLASSWORK

The question. Calculate the electric potential at a point 2 m from a charge of 5 × 10−6 C.

 - Use the potential formula, which has plain r on the bottom: V = kQ / r. Not r squared. That would be the field.
 - V = (8.99 × 109)(5 × 10−6) / 2 Everything is already in SI.
 - Numerator: (8.99 × 109)(5 × 10−6) = 4.495 × 104. Multiply fronts: 8.99 × 5 = 44.95. Add exponents: 9 + (−6) = 3. So 44.95 × 103 = 4.495 × 104.
 - Divide by 2: V = 2.2475 × 104 V.
V ≈ 2.25 × 104 V  (about 22.5 kV)
Now redo it: then ask what the field would be at the same point. E = kQ/r2 = 4.495 × 104 / 4 = 1.12 ×

104 V/m. Note that V and E are numerically different because of that extra factor of r. Confirm you can

produce both without hesitating.

WORKED EXAMPLE · WORK DONE MOVING 3 C THROUGH 12 VCLASSWORK

The question. A charge of 3 C moves through a potential difference of 12 V. Calculate the work done.

 - Use W = qV. The definition of potential difference is work per unit charge, so the work is charge times potential difference. No need for distances or fields. The potential difference already contains all of that.
 - W = (3)(12) = 36 J. Coulombs times volts gives joules directly.
W = 36 J
Now redo it: this is why the volt is defined as a joule per coulomb. Driving one coulomb through one

### Equipotential surfaces

MUST-MEMORISE · THE THREE PROPERTIES OF AN EQUIPOTENTIAL SURFACE

An equipotential surface is a surface on which every point has the same electric potential.

 - No work is done when a charge moves along an equipotential surface.
 - Electric field lines are always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.
 - Equipotential surfaces never intersect one another.

MEMORY HOOK: contour lines on a map. Walking along a contour costs no climbing, and the

steepest slope is always at right angles to the contour.

RECALL AND CHECK

Explain, in one line, why no work is done when a charge moves along an equipotential surface.

Answer

Because W = qV, and along an equipotential surface the potential difference V between start and finish is zero. So W = q × 0 = 0. Equivalently: the force is perpendicular to the motion, and a force at right angles to the displacement does no work.

## Gauss's law

Gauss's law is a shortcut. Where the situation is symmetric, it produces in three lines a field that would otherwise take an unpleasant integral.

TEACH · ELECTRIC FLUX, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE

Electric flux is a measure of how many field lines pass through a surface. Think of the field as rain falling and the surface as a hoop: the flux is how much rain goes through the hoop.

ΦE = E A cos θ

Hold the hoop face-on to the rain and you catch the most (θ = 0, cos = 1). Turn it edge-on and you catch none (θ = 90°, cos = 0). The angle θ is measured between the field and the normal to the surface, that is, the line sticking straight out of it.

Unit: N m2/C.

MUST-MEMORISE · GAUSS'S LAW

The total electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the total charge enclosed within that surface:

ΦE = Qenclosed / ε0

ΦE is the total flux out through the closed surface Qenclosed is the net charge inside the surface only ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m 

MEMORY HOOK: flux out equals charge in, divided by epsilon nought. Charges outside the surface

contribute nothing to the net flux: whatever field lines enter one side must leave the other.

TEACH · THE PHYSICAL PICTURE, AND WHY EXTERNAL CHARGES DO NOT MAT TER

 - Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges.
 - The number of lines crossing a closed surface depends only on the charge enclosed by it.
 - A charge outside the surface sends lines in one side and straight out the other. They cancel exactly, contributing zero net flux.

This is the whole content of the law, and it is why it is such a powerful shortcut: you can ignore everything outside your chosen surface.

TEACH · WHAT A GAUSSIAN SURFACE IS, AND HOW TO CHOOSE IT

A Gaussian surface is an imaginary closed surface that you invent, purely to make the calculation easy. It has no physical existence. The art is choosing it so that E is constant over it and perpendicular to it, because then the flux integral collapses to the simple product E × A.

### The standard results, derived

CASE 1 · FIELD OF A POINT CHARGESLIDE

The task. Use Gauss's law to find the electric field at a distance r from a point charge Q.

 - Draw a sphere of radius r centred on the charge Q. By symmetry, E has the same magnitude everywhere on this sphere and points straight out.
 - The flux is then simply E multiplied by the sphere's area: Φ = E(4πr2). This uses the surface area of a sphere from Foundations F.9. The cos θ is 1 because E is perpendicular to the surface everywhere.
 - Set that equal to Q/ε0 by Gauss's law: E(4πr2) = Q / ε0.
 - Rearrange for E.
E = Q / (4πε0r2) = kQ / r2
Look at what just happened: this is Coulomb's law, recovered in four lines from Gauss's law. The

two are not separate facts. They are the same physics stated two ways, and the 4π in Coulomb's law is

precisely the 4π from the surface of a sphere.

CASE 2 · INFINITE LINE CHARGE (LONG STRAIGHT WIRE)

A wire carrying a uniform linear charge density λ (charge per metre, C/m). Wrap a cylinder of radius r and length L around it. The charge enclosed is λL, and the curved area is 2πrL. So E(2πrL) = λL/ε0, and the L cancels:

E = λ / (2πε0r)

Note the field falls off as 1/r, not 1/r2. A line of charge spreads its influence more slowly than a point does.

CASE 3 · INFINITE PLANE SHEET

A sheet carrying a uniform surface charge density σ (charge per square metre, C/m2). Push a cylinder through the sheet so it sticks out both sides. Flux escapes only through the two flat ends, giving 2A of area:

E = σ / (2ε0)

Remarkably, r does not appear. The field of an infinite sheet is the same at every distance. Move away from it and it does not weaken.

MUST-MEMORISE · CASE 4: THE FIELD INSIDE A HOLLOW CONDUCTOR IS ZERO

E = 0 everywhere inside a hollow conducting shell

Draw a Gaussian surface inside the cavity. It encloses no charge, because all the charge on a conductor sits on its outer surface. So the flux through it is zero, and therefore E = 0. Outside the shell (r > R), the Gaussian sphere encloses the whole charge Q, and the shell behaves exactly like a point charge at its centre: E = kQ/r2.

MEMORY HOOK: no charge inside means no field inside. This is the principle of the Faraday cage,

and it is why you are safe inside a car during a lightning strike and why sensitive electronics are shielded in metal boxes.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FIELD AT THE SURFACE OF A GAUSSIAN SPHERECLASSWORK

Question (slides): A point charge of 5 × 10−6 C sits at the centre of a spherical Gaussian surface of radius 0.20 m. Find the electric field at the surface.

 - E = 4.495 × 104 / 0.04 = 1.124 × 106 N/C.
E ≈ 1.12 × 106 N/C, directed radially outward
Now redo it: and note that we never actually needed Gauss's law to compute this, only the point charge

WORKED EXAMPLE · TOTAL FLUX THROUGH A SURFACE ENCLOSING 3 µCCLASSWORK

The question. A closed spherical surface encloses a charge of 3 µC. Calculate the total electric flux through the surface.

Φ ≈ 3.39 × 105 N m2/C
Now redo it: notice you were given no radius and no shape, and you did not need them. If a question

WORKED EXAMPLE · FIELD FROM A LONG CHARGED WIRECLASSWORK

Question (slides): An infinitely long straight wire carries a uniform linear charge density of 4 × 10−6 C/m. Using a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius 0.05 m, find the field at that distance.

 - Use the line charge result: E = λ / (2πε0r). Falls off as 1/r, since this is a line and not a point.
 - Denominator: 2π(8.85 × 10−12)(0.05) = 2.781 × 10−12. 2π × 8.85 = 55.6, times 0.05 gives 2.78, keeping the 10−12.

E ≈ 1.44 × 106 N/C, pointing radially away from the wire
Now redo it: the length L of the cylinder never appeared in the answer, because it cancelled. That

cancellation is the signature of a correct Gauss's law calculation for a line charge. If L survives into your

final answer, you have gone wrong.

RECALL AND CHECK

A closed surface encloses no charge, but there is a large charge sitting just outside it. What is the net flux through the surface?

Answer

Zero. Gauss's law depends only on the enclosed charge. The external charge sends field lines in through one side and out through the other, and they cancel exactly. Note that the field on the surface is not zero, but the net flux is.
