Circuits

Current, Ohm's Law, Resistivity and Materials

Before any network can be solved, four words have to be exact: current, resistance, resistivity and ohmic. The exam asks for the definitions directly, and then uses them in every calculation.

About 14 minutes. Work every step yourself before reading on. Nothing here is a summary to skim: each box is one small piece of the skill, in order.

Current is a rate, not an amount

Water through a pipereason before rule

Start with a tap. Ask two different questions. How much water came out? That is litres. How fast is it flowing? That is litres per second.

Charge and current are that same pair.

Charge QCurrent I
Water wordLitresLitres per second
It is aTotal amountRate
Unitcoulomb, Campere, A

I = ΔQ / Δt (I the current in amperes, ΔQ the charge that passed in coulombs, Δt the time it took in seconds)

Key point, and it is the unit written out

One ampere is one coulomb per second.
So the ampere is not a new idea at all. It is the coulomb, per second, exactly as the formula says.

The battery's side of the story is the energy it hands each coulomb:

W = ΔQ × ε (W the work done in joules, ΔQ the charge pushed round in coulombs, ε the emf in volts)

Which is W = qV wearing a battery's clothes. A volt is a joule per coulomb, so coulombs times volts is joules, every time.

Your turn. 60 C passes a point in 20 seconds. What is the current? And how much work did a 12 V battery do pushing it?

Current
I = ΔQ/Δt
I = 60/20
I = 3 A

Work
W = ΔQ × ε
W = 60 x 12
W = 720 J

Notice the current did not enter the second one. The work depends on how much charge went round, not how quickly.

Ohm's law and the ohmmust memorise

V = IR

SymbolMeaningUnit
VVoltage, or potential difference, ACROSS the componentvolt, V
ICurrent THROUGH the componentampere, A
RResistance of the componentohm, Ω
The rearrangement trick

Write V on top, with I and R side by side underneath.
Cover the one you want and the formula reads itself:
cover V, and you see I times R
cover I, and you see V over R
cover R, and you see V over I

You never have to do algebra on V = IR again.

The ohm, defined: one ohm is one volt per amp. Read straight off R = V/I, exactly as the ampere read off I = Q/t.

And the word ohmic: a component is ohmic if it obeys Ohm's law, meaning its resistance stays constant as the voltage changes, at a fixed temperature. Double the voltage, double the current, and R does not budge. A filament lamp is NOT ohmic, because it heats up and its resistance climbs.

Resistivity: long and thin resiststhe shape of the wire

Resistance is not just a property of a material. It depends on the SHAPE of the piece you cut.

The hook, four words

Long and thin resists. Short and fat does not.
L is on top, so LONGER means more resistance.
A is on the bottom, so THINNER means more resistance.

The everyday case: a garden hose. A long narrow hose gives you a trickle. A short wide one gives you a torrent. Same water pressure, same tap, different pipe.

Your turn. You replace a wire with one of the same material, twice as long and twice the cross-sectional area. What happens to its resistance?

Nothing. It is unchanged.

R = ρL/A
L doubles: R would double
A doubles: R would halve
2 x ½ = 1

The two changes cancel exactly. That is why you must read BOTH dimensions before answering.

Three words the exam defines directlyeasy marks if you have the words

Three definitions that appear as fill-in-the-gap and true-or-false questions, where knowing the exact wording is the entire mark.

WordDefinitionThe detail that is marked
SemiconductorA material between a conductor and an insulatorIts resistance FALLS as it heats, which is backwards from a metal
DopingAdding impurities to a semiconductor to make it conductDeliberate contamination, not a defect
Short circuitA path of zero resistanceIt carries all the current, so everything in parallel with it is bypassed
Why a semiconductor is backwards

Heat a metal and its atoms vibrate harder, getting in the way of the moving charge, so resistance rises.
Heat a semiconductor and the extra energy frees more charge carriers than it obstructs, so resistance falls.

Same heat, opposite result. That contrast is exactly what the exam tests.

The everyday case for a short circuit: a bare wire falls across the two terminals of a battery. Current takes the zero-resistance path in preference to everything else, rises enormously since I = V/R with R near zero, and the wire gets hot enough to burn. That is what a fuse exists to interrupt.

Your turn. A 10 Ω lamp has a short circuit placed directly across it. How much current now flows through the lamp?

Essentially none.

The short is 0 Ω, the lamp is 10 Ω
they are in parallel
current takes the path of least resistance
so it all goes through the short

The lamp goes out although it is undamaged and still connected. Examiners like this one because nothing about the lamp changed.

Your turnfive definitions and a calculation

(1) Define the ampere.

(2) Define the ohm.

(3) What does ohmic mean?

(4) A wire is stretched to three times its length, the material and volume unchanged so its area falls to a third. What happens to R?

(5) 240 V across a 60 Ω heater. Find the current.

Answer

(1) One ampere is one coulomb per second.

(2) One ohm is one volt per amp.

(3) Obeying Ohm's law: the resistance stays constant as the voltage changes, at a fixed temperature.

(4) R = ρL/A. L triples, so R triples. A falls to a third, so R triples again. Total: nine times the original resistance. Stretching a wire raises its resistance far faster than people expect.

(5) I = V/R = 240/60 = 4 A.
