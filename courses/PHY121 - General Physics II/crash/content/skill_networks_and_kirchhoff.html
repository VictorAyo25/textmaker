Resistor Networks, Dividers and Kirchhoff's Laws

Two laws, two network rules and two dividers. Between them they solve every circuit the paper can set, and each one has a free check built into it.

About 16 minutes. Work every step yourself before reading on. Nothing here is a summary to skim: each box is one small piece of the skill, in order.

Series and parallel, with their sanity checks

One road or tworeason before rule

Start with traffic. Two roadworks one after the other on the same road: you are held up twice, so the total delay adds. Now open a second road alongside: traffic splits and everyone gets through faster than on either road alone.

Resistors do exactly that.

ArrangementFormulaSanity check
Series, one after the otherR = R1 + R2Bigger than either. Same current through both
Parallel, side by side1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2Less than the smallest. Same voltage across both
Two in parallel onlyR = R1R2/(R1+R2)Product over sum. Faster for exactly two
The free check, worth using every time

A parallel total is ALWAYS less than the smallest resistor in it.
If your answer is bigger, you almost certainly forgot to flip 1/R back into R at the end. That one check catches the most common circuit slip there is.

And remember the flip from capacitors. Resistors add in SERIES. Capacitors add in PARALLEL. Learn one properly, flip it for the other.

Your turn. 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel. Use product over sum, then check it.

R = 18/9

2 is less than 3, the smallest
so it passes

Had you added them to 9 Ω, the check would have failed instantly.

The two dividersone has the OTHER resistor on top

When two resistors share a voltage or a current, you can get either share in one line, without solving the whole circuit.

DividerFormulaWhat is on top
Voltage divider, resistors in seriesV1 = (R1/Rtotal) x VThe resistor you WANT the voltage across
Current divider, resistors in parallelI1 = (R2/(R1+R2)) x ItotalThe OTHER resistor
Why one of them is swapped, and this is the whole trick

Voltage goes where the resistance is. A bigger resistor takes a bigger share of the voltage, so its own R sits on top.
Current avoids resistance. A bigger resistor takes a SMALLER share of the current, so the OTHER resistor sits on top.

Say it as: voltage prefers the big one, current prefers the small one.

Your turn. 4 Ω and 6 Ω in series across 20 V. What is the voltage across the 6 Ω?

Formula
V6 = (R6/Rtotal) x V
Substitute
V6 = (6/10) x 20
Answer
V6 = 12 V

Check
the 4 Ω must take the other 8 V
12 + 8 = 20, the full supply

And the bigger resistor took the bigger share, exactly as voltage should.

Now the current divider, with the same two resistors in PARALLEL across a 5 A supply. How much goes through the 6 Ω?

Formula, OTHER resistor on top
I6 = (R4/(R4+R6)) x I
Substitute
I6 = (4/10) x 5
Answer
I6 = 2 A

Check
the 4 Ω takes the other 3 A
2 + 3 = 5, the full current

And the SMALLER resistor took the bigger current, exactly as it should.

Kirchhoff's two lawscharge at junctions, energy round loops

Two laws carry every circuit that is too tangled for series and parallel alone. Both are conservation laws in disguise, and knowing WHICH one each is makes them impossible to mix up.

First, two words the laws are stated in:

  • A junction, also called a node, is a point where two or more conductors meet.
  • A loop is a closed conducting path, any route round the circuit that ends where it began.
LawStatementIt is really
Current law, at a junctionSum of currents IN equals sum of currents OUTConservation of CHARGE
Voltage law, round a loopThe sum of the voltages around any closed loop is zeroConservation of ENERGY
The hook, and it is what the exam asks for

Junctions conserve charge. Loops conserve energy.
Nothing piles up at a junction, because charge cannot be created or destroyed there.
Nothing is gained going all the way round, because you end at the same potential you started at, so the energy each coulomb gained must equal the energy it spent.

The everyday case for the current law: a river reaching a fork. Whatever flows in must flow out along the two branches. Water does not pile up at the split, and neither does charge.

The everyday case for the voltage law: a walk round a hill that returns to your front door. However much you climbed, you descended exactly the same amount, because you are back where you started.

Your turn. Three wires meet at a junction. 7 A flows in along one, 3 A flows out along another. What flows in the third, and which way?

Current law

7 = 3 + I
I = 4 A
Direction
OUT, since the ins had to balance

Nothing accumulates at the point, so the arithmetic has no choice.

Which resistance does the question mean?Test 1 question 12

Test 1 question 12 asks for "the current that flows through R3" in a series circuit of 7, 6 and 3 ohms across 48 V.

Naming R3 invites you to compute 48/3 = 16 A. But in series the same current flows through everything, so the answer for R3 is the answer for the whole loop.

R = 7 + 6 + 3 = 16 Ω, and I = 48/16 = 3 A.

The trap works because the question sounds like it is asking about one component. It is not. In series there is only one current in the entire circuit, and naming a resistor cannot change that.

A mixed network, worked end to endseries first, or parallel first?

A 12 V supply feeds a 4 Ω resistor in series with a parallel pair of 6 Ω and 12 Ω. Find the total resistance, the current from the supply, and the voltage across the parallel pair.

Step 1: always collapse the PARALLEL group first

You cannot add the 4 Ω to anything until the parallel pair is a single number.

Rp = (6 x 12)/(6 + 12) = 72/18 = 4 Ω

Check: 4 is less than 6, the smaller of the pair. It passes.

Step 2: now it is a simple series circuit

Rtotal = 4 + 4 = 8 Ω

Step 3: the supply current, from Ohm's law on the whole circuit

I = V/Rtotal = 12/8 = 1.5 A

Step 4: the voltage across the parallel pair

Two routes, and they must agree.

  • Ohm's law on the group: V = I Rp = 1.5 x 4 = 6 V
  • Voltage divider: V = (Rp/Rtotal) x 12 = (4/8) x 12 = 6 V

Step 5: check with Kirchhoff's voltage law

Round the loop: 6 V across the 4 Ω resistor, plus 6 V across the pair, is 12 V, the supply. The sum round the loop is zero, as the law requires.

Answer R = 8 Ω, I = 1.5 A, and 6 V across the parallel pair

Redo it. Redo it asking how the 1.5 A SPLITS between the 6 Ω and the 12 Ω. Use the current divider with the OTHER resistor on top: I through the 6 Ω = (12/18) x 1.5 = 1.0 A, and I through the 12 Ω = (6/18) x 1.5 = 0.5 A. They add back to 1.5 A, as the current law demands, and the smaller resistor took the larger current.
