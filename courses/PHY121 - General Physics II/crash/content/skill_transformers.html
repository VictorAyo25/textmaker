Transformers and Why They Need AC

A transformer trades voltage for current, and deciding step-up or step-down before any arithmetic eliminates most of the options.

About 10 minutes. Work every step yourself before reading on. Nothing here is a summary to skim: each box is one small piece of the skill, in order.

Two coils that never touch

How a transformer works, and why DC kills itreason before rule

A transformer is two coils coupled by mutual induction. They are not connected electrically at all. The only thing passing between them is a changing magnetic flux, usually guided by an iron core.

  1. An alternating current in the primary makes a constantly changing flux.
  2. That changing flux threads the secondary.
  3. By Faraday's law, a changing flux through the secondary induces an emf in it.
  4. More turns on the secondary means more flux linkages, so more voltage.

Vs/Vp = Ns/Np

SymbolMeaningUnit
Vp, VsVoltage across the primary and the secondaryvolt, V
Np, NsNumber of turns on the primary and the secondarynone, they are counts
Key point, and it follows from step 1

A transformer works on AC only.
A steady direct current makes a steady flux, and a steady flux induces nothing at all. Connect a transformer to a battery and the secondary gives you one brief pulse as you connect, one as you disconnect, and nothing in between.

The turns ratio is the voltage ratio. Decide the DIRECTION before you calculate anything:

  • More turns on the secondary than the primary, so Ns greater than Np: it steps UP.
  • Fewer turns on the secondary: it steps DOWN.

That single decision usually kills three of the five options on the paper before any arithmetic.

The everyday case: the national grid steps voltage UP to hundreds of kilovolts to carry power across the country, because high voltage means low current and low current means little heat wasted in the cables. Then it steps it back DOWN outside your street. The whole grid runs on AC for exactly this reason, and that is not an accident of history.

Test 2 question 6, the efficiency oneoutput over input

A 240 V transformer lights eight 12 V, 24 W lamps and draws 1 A in the primary. Find the efficiency.

Step 1: what is the OUTPUT power?

Each lamp is rated 24 W, and there are eight of them.

Pout = 8 x 24 = 192 W

The 12 V rating is not needed. The wattage already tells you the power each lamp uses; quoting the voltage as well is the examiner giving you a spare number to see if you use it wrongly.

Step 2: what is the INPUT power?

Pin = Vp x Ip = 240 x 1 = 240 W

Step 3: efficiency is always output over input

Efficiency = Pout/Pin = 192/240 = 0.8 = 80 per cent

Step 4: check

An efficiency must be below 100 per cent. If you had divided the other way you would have got 125 per cent, which would mean the transformer created energy. That check alone identifies the slip.

Answer 80 per cent

Redo it. Redo it with the primary drawing 1.2 A instead. Input becomes 288 W, so the efficiency falls to 192/288 = 66.7 per cent. More current drawn for the same useful output means more of it is being wasted as heat in the core and the windings.

Your turnthree quick ones

(1) A transformer has 500 primary turns and 100 secondary turns, with 240 V in. Step up or down, and what comes out?

(2) What IS a transformer, in one sentence?

(3) Why can it not run on DC?

Answer

(1) Fewer secondary turns, so it steps DOWN and the answer must be below 240 V. Vs = 240 x 100/500 = 240/5 = 48 V.

(2) Two coils coupled by mutual induction, which changes voltage and works on AC only.

(3) Because it needs a CHANGING flux to induce anything in the secondary. Direct current gives a steady flux, and a steady flux induces nothing. It is Faraday's law again, and the whole of the last lesson in one sentence.
