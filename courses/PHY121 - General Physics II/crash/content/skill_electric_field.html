Electrostatics

The Electric Field: One Charge, Not Two

The single most common error on this paper is putting the wrong charge into the formula. This lesson makes the choice automatic, and gives you a picture that decides direction without any algebra.

About 16 minutes. Work every step yourself before reading on. Nothing here is a summary to skim: each box is one small piece of the skill, in order.

The idea before the formulas

What a field actually isreason before rule

Start with something you have felt. Hold two magnets near each other and you feel a push before they touch. Nothing crosses the gap that you can see, yet the force is real.

Electric charges do the same thing, and the explanation is worth getting exactly right.

  1. A charge does not reach out and touch another charge.
  2. It changes the space around it.
  3. A second charge, placed in that space, responds to the space it finds itself in.

That change in the space is what we call the electric field.

Key point

Because the field belongs to the space, there are always TWO questions, never one. What field is there? And what force does a charge feel in it?

QuestionDepends onFormula
What field is there?Only the charge producing itE = k|Q|/r2 (E the field, Q the charge producing it, r your distance from it in metres, k = 9 x 109)
What force does a charge feel?The field, and the charge sitting in itF = qE (F the force in newtons, q the charge sitting in the field, in coulombs)

Your turn. Test 1 question 13 gives two charges and asks for the field produced by the NEGATIVE one, at the location of the positive one. Which charge goes into the formula?

Answer: only the negative one.

The positive charge is where you are standing, not what you are measuring.

Given
Q = 2.0 x 10-8 C (the negative charge, magnitude)
r = 0.40 m
k = 9 x 109
Formula
E = k|Q|/r2
Substitute
E = (9 x 109 x 2.0 x 10-8) / (0.40)2
E = 180 / 0.16
Answer
E = 1125 N/C

Use the other charge instead and you get roughly four times this, which is one of the five options offered.

Say it bluntly

Only the charge PRODUCING the field goes in the formula.

One charge or two, and why the bars are therethis decides which formula

Start with the wind. Ask two different questions about it. How strong is it? That answer is a size: 30 km/h. Which way is it blowing? That answer is a direction: from the east.

You would never answer the first question with "minus 30 km/h". Strength has no sign. The sign belongs to the direction, and you state it separately.

Electric formulas work the same way, and that is what the bars in E = k|Q|/r2 are doing.

  1. The formula gives you the size of the field.
  2. So you feed in the magnitude of Q, dropping any minus sign.
  3. The sign of the charge is not thrown away. It tells you the direction, which you decide separately: a field points away from a positive charge and towards a negative one.

Put a negative Q into the arithmetic and you would get a negative field strength, which means nothing. A field has a direction, not a negative size.

Key point

ONE charge makes a field. TWO charges make a force. E = k|Q|/r2 has a single Q in it, the source. F = kq1q2/r2 has two, because a force needs two objects to act between.

Your turn. Test 1 question 8 puts +3 µC and -3 µC 0.10 m apart and asks for the net field at the midpoint. How many charges go into each use of E = k|Q|/r2, and what do you do with the minus sign?

Say it bluntly

Magnitudes go in the formula. Signs decide the direction, afterwards.

Test 1 question 8, worked in fullsuperposition

Two charges Q1 = +3.0 x 10-6 C and Q2 = -3.0 x 10-6 C are placed 0.10 m apart in air. Find the net electric field at the midpoint between the charges.

Hold this picture in your head first

A positive charge is a FAN. It blows outward, away from itself.
A negative charge is a VACUUM CLEANER. It sucks inward, toward itself.
The electric field is which way the air moves.

Keep that image. It decides this entire question, and you will see why at step 3.

Step 1: Find the distance r from each charge to the midpoint

The charges are 0.10 m apart and you want the field at the midpoint, so you need the distance from each individual charge to that exact middle point. That is half the separation.

r = 0.10 m ÷ 2 = 0.05 m

The same 0.05 m for BOTH charges. Hold on to that, because it is why step 2 only has to be done once.

Step 2: Calculate the SIZE of the field from each charge

The field created by one point charge is:

E = k|Q| / r2

Q1 and Q2 have the same magnitude (3.0 x 10-6 C) and sit at the same distance (0.05 m). So each produces a field of identical size, and one calculation covers both.

  • k = 9 x 109 N m2/C2
  • Q = 3.0 x 10-6 C  (magnitude, so the minus on Q2 is dropped here)
  • r = 0.05 m
E1 = E2 = (9 x 109) x (3.0 x 10-6) ÷ (0.05)2E1 = E2 = 27000 ÷ 0.0025 = 1.08 x 107 N/C

Step 3: Now the direction, and this is what decides the answer

Go back to the picture. On the left is a fan (the positive charge). On the right is a vacuum cleaner (the negative charge). Now stand at the midpoint and ask which way the air moves.

1. The fan on your left blows you to the right.
2. The vacuum on your right sucks you to the right.

Both send you the same way, so the two fields reinforce and add.

Step 4: Add them

Enet = E1 + E2Enet = (1.08 x 107) + (1.08 x 107) = 2.16 x 107 N/C
Carry this into the exam

Fan and vacuum: they cooperate. Two fans: they fight.
A + and a − both push you the same way, so the fields ADD.
Two + charges blow you in opposite directions, so they CANCEL.
Opposite signs add. Same signs cancel. Most people guess that backwards, and the exam offers both answers.

Answer 2.16 x 107 N/C

Redo it. Redo it with Q2 changed to +3.0 x 10-6 C. Now it is TWO FANS, one on each side. The left fan blows you right, the right fan blows you left, and standing in the middle you are pushed equally both ways. The net field is zero. Nothing in the arithmetic changed: both sizes are still 1.08 x 107 N/C. Only the picture did.

Which formula does the question want?cover and slide down

Each frame gives you a question stem. Decide which formula it needs before you slide down.

Read for the QUANTITY asked for and the units offered. Units in N/C or V/m mean a field. Units in volts mean a potential. Units in newtons mean a force. "A charge of +1.5 x 10-9 C is placed in a uniform electric field of magnitude 2500 N/C. What is the magnitude of the force?" Which formula?

F = qE. The field is GIVEN, so no inverse square is needed at all: F = 1.5 x 10-9 x 2500 = 3.75 x 10-6 N. That is Test 1 question 7. When the field is handed to you, Coulomb's law does not come into it. Students lose this mark by reaching for kQ/r2 out of habit. "What is the absolute potential 5 cm from a charge of +4 microcoulombs?" Which formula?

V = kQ/r, with a PLAIN r. V = 9 x 109 x 4 x 10-6 / 0.05 = 36000 / 0.05 = 720000 V = 720 kV. Note that 36000 divided by 0.05 is 720000, not 1800. Dividing by a number less than one makes the answer bigger. "What is the charge of a system that has 6.2 x 1018 electrons?" Which formula?

q = ne = 6.2 x 1018 x 1.602 x 10-19 = 0.99 C. That is Test 1 question 10. Notice the answer sits just under 1 coulomb. That is because 1/e is about 6.24 x 1018, so 6.2 x 1018 electrons is almost exactly one coulomb. A useful check. "Calculate the electric field in which an oil drop of mass 8 mg and charge +9 microcoulombs is held stationary." What is the physical condition?

Stationary means the forces BALANCE: the electric force up equals the weight down. qE = mg, so E = mg/q. Now convert: 8 mg is 8 x 10-6 kg, and 9 microcoulombs is 9 x 10-6 C. E = (8 x 10-6 x 10) / (9 x 10-6) = 8.89 V/m. The examiner is using g = 10. Why did the powers of ten vanish so cleanly there?

Because 10-6 appeared on the top and the bottom and cancelled. Spotting that saves the whole calculation. That is the pattern to look for: when a mass in milligrams meets a charge in microcoulombs, the exponents often cancel.
