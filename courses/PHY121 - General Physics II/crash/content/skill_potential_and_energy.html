Electrostatics

Potential, Energy and the Uniform Field

Potential is the twin of the field, and the exam separates them with one difference: the field is squared and the potential is not. Getting that straight is worth marks on both tests.

About 14 minutes. Work every step yourself before reading on. Nothing here is a summary to skim: each box is one small piece of the skill, in order.

Height, not steepness

The hillreason before rule

Start with a hill. Standing on it, two different things can be asked about where you are.

  1. How high are you? That is a single number. No direction. Just a height.
  2. How steep is it here? That has a direction: the slope points down some particular way.

Electric potential is the height. Electric field is the steepness. They are two descriptions of the same hill, and every question is asking for one or the other.

Key point, and the exam lives here

E = kQ/r2 but V = kQ/r.
The field is squared. The potential is not.
Why? Because the field is a vector spreading over a sphere, and the potential is the total climb, which thins out more slowly.

Your turn. You move from 5 cm to 20 cm away from a charge, four times further out. What happens to the field, and what happens to the potential?

Field: 1 over 4 squared = a SIXTEENTH
E = kQ/r2, so 4 becomes 16

Potential: 1 over 4 = a QUARTER
V = kQ/r, so 4 stays 4

Check it on the real paper
Test 1 question 4 goes 5 cm to 20 cm
720 kV falls to 180 kV
720/180 = 4, a quarter

Had you squared it you would have hunted for a sixteenth and found no such option.

Say it bluntly

Field squared, potential plain. Say it every time you see an r.

Why W = qV is almost a definition

A volt IS a joule per coulomb. So coulombs times volts gives joules, and W = qV is really just the unit written as an equation. Once you see that, you never have to memorise it.

Note the sign in V = kQ/r. Unlike the field formula, there are no magnitude bars here. Potential is a scalar, so a negative charge really does produce a negative potential, and you keep the minus.

The uniform field between platesthe other kind of field question

Everything so far has been the field of a point charge, which weakens as you move away. Between two parallel plates something different happens: the field is the same everywhere in the gap.

E = V/d (E the uniform field in V/m, V the voltage across the plates, d the gap between them in METRES)

Key point

No r, no squaring, no k. A uniform field question is a division, and the only work in it is converting the gap to metres.

Your turn. Two plates 2 mm apart have 60 V across them. What is the field?

Convert first
2 mm = 0.002 m
Formula
E = V/d
Substitute
E = 60 / 0.002
Answer
E = 30000 V/m

Leave the gap in millimetres and you get 30 V/m, wrong by a factor of a thousand.

Look at the size of that answer. A modest 60 V across a gap you can barely see produces thirty thousand volts per metre. That is exactly why capacitors are built with their plates as close together as possible, and also why they eventually break down: push the field high enough and even the insulator between them gives way.

Equipotentials, and why no work is done along thema definition worth two marks

Start back on the hill. Walk around it at a constant height, following a contour line on a map. You go a long way, but you never climb.

An equipotential is exactly that contour: a surface on which the potential is the same everywhere.

Key point

W = qV depends on the CHANGE in potential. Along an equipotential there is no change, so no work is done, no matter how far you travel.

One consequence follows immediately, and examiners like it: field lines always cross an equipotential at right angles. If a field line had any component ALONG the surface, it would push a charge along it and do work, which would mean the potential changed. It did not. So the field can only point straight across.

The everyday case: around a point charge the equipotentials are spheres, one inside another like the layers of an onion. Move around a sphere and you do nothing. Move outward from one sphere to the next and you have climbed.

Your turn. How much work is done carrying a 5 C charge right around a circle centred on a point charge?

Zero.

The circle is an equipotential
so V at the end = V at the start
so the CHANGE in V is 0
W = qV = 5 x 0
W = 0 J

The 5 C was never needed. Any charge, any size of circle, still zero.

Test 1 question 4, worked in fullthe quarter, not the sixteenth

A charge of +4 µC is in air. Find the absolute potential at 5 cm from it, and then at 20 cm from it.

Step 1: Convert both distances before anything else

  • 5 cm = 0.05 m
  • 20 cm = 0.20 m
  • Q = 4 µC = 4 x 10-6 C
  • k = 9 x 109 N m2/C2

Step 2: Choose the formula, and notice the r is plain

V = kQ / r   (not r squared, this is the potential)

Step 3: The first distance

V = (9 x 109 x 4 x 10-6) ÷ 0.05V = 36000 ÷ 0.05 = 720000 V = 720 kV

Dividing by 0.05 MULTIPLIES by 20. If your answer came out smaller than 36000, you divided the wrong way round.

Step 4: The second distance

V = 36000 ÷ 0.20 = 180000 V = 180 kV

Step 5: Check the ratio, which is the real lesson

720 ÷ 180 = 4

The distance went up 4 times and the potential fell to a QUARTER, not a sixteenth. That is V = kQ/r with its plain r, visible in the numbers.

Answer 720 kV at 5 cm, and 180 kV at 20 cm

Redo it. Redo it as a FIELD question, E = k|Q|/r2, at the same two distances. You get 1.44 x 107 N/C and 9.0 x 105 N/C, a ratio of 16. Same charge, same distances, and the ratio is squared. That contrast is the whole of this lesson in two lines.

Your turnthree quick ones

(1) How much work is done moving 3 C through a potential difference of 12 V?

(2) A charge sits at a point where the potential is 500 V. Its charge is 2 µC. What is its potential energy?

(3) Two plates 5 mm apart carry a uniform field of 4000 V/m. What is the voltage across them?

Answer

(1) W = qV = 3 x 12 = 36 J. Coulombs times volts gives joules, because a volt is a joule per coulomb.

(2) U = qV = 2 x 10-6 x 500 = 1 x 10-3 J, that is 1 mJ.

(3) Rearrange E = V/d to V = Ed. Convert first: 5 mm = 0.005 m. V = 4000 x 0.005 = 20 V. Here you MULTIPLY by the small number, so the answer is small. Dividing instead gives 800000, which is the slip to watch for.
