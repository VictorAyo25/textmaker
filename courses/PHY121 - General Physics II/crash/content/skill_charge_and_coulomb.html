Electrostatics

Charge, Conductors and Coulomb's Law

Everything electrical starts with two facts: charge comes in whole coins, and two charges push or pull along a line between them. Get those and the inverse square law stops being something to memorise.

About 13 minutes. Work every step yourself before reading on. Nothing here is a summary to skim: each box is one small piece of the skill, in order.

What charge is, before any formula

Coins, not liquidreason before rule

Start with money. You can hold 3 naira or 4 naira. You cannot hold 3.7 naira in coins, because there is a smallest coin and everything is a whole number of them.

Charge behaves in exactly that way. There is a smallest coin, the charge on one electron, and every charge you will ever meet is a whole number of those.

q = ne (q the total charge in coulombs, n the NUMBER of electrons or protons, always a whole number, e the elementary charge = 1.602 x 10-19 C)

Key point

Charge comes in coins, not in liquid. You never meet half an electron's worth. That is what the word quantised means, and n is a plain count with no unit at all.

The second fact is the one every child discovers with a balloon and their hair.

  • Like repels. Two positives push apart. Two negatives push apart.
  • Unlike attracts. A positive and a negative pull together.

Trivial as it sounds, it decides the sign of every force answer in Part B, and in the field lesson it will decide whether two fields add or cancel.

Your turn. A system carries 6.2 x 1018 electrons. What is its charge?

Given
n = 6.2 x 1018
e = 1.602 x 10-19 C
Formula
q = ne
Substitute
q = 6.2 x 1018 x 1.602 x 10-19
front: 6.2 x 1.602 = 9.93
exponents: 18 plus minus 19 = minus 1
Answer
q = 9.93 x 10-1 = 0.99 C

That is Test 1 question 10.

Notice the answer sits just under 1 coulomb. That is not a coincidence: 1 divided by e is about 6.24 x 1018, so 6.2 x 1018 electrons is very nearly one coulomb exactly. Keep that as a free check.

Say it bluntly

n is a count. It has no unit and it is never a fraction.

Conductors and insulatorsone word decides

Two words that will come back in Gauss's law, in capacitors and in circuits, so learn them once here.

WordWhat it meansEveryday case
ConductorCharge moves freely through itCopper wire, the metal body of a car
InsulatorCharge is bound and cannot move freelyRubber, glass, dry air, plastic
Key point

The whole difference is whether the charge can walk about. In a conductor it can, so any excess charge runs to the outer surface and spreads out. In an insulator it is stuck where you put it.

That one sentence explains three later results in advance:

  1. Charge on a conductor sits on the outside, which is why the field inside a hollow conductor is zero.
  2. An insulator between capacitor plates cannot carry current, which is what lets a capacitor hold a voltage at all.
  3. A conductor's resistance is low precisely because the charge is free to move.

Your turn. Lightning strikes a car with you inside it. Why are you safe?

Because the car body is a conductor.

Charge runs to the outer surface
so no charge is enclosed inside
so there is no field inside
so nothing pushes current through you

It is not the rubber tyres. The metal shell is doing the work, and you will prove it with Gauss's law in Part B.

Coulomb's lawmust memorise

F = k q1 q2 / r2

SymbolMeaningUnit
FForce between the two charges, along the line joining themnewton, N
q1, q2The TWO charges. A force needs two objects to act betweencoulomb, C
rDistance between them, in METRES, and it is squaredmetre, m
kCoulomb's constant8.99 x 109 N m2 C-2
The hook for the inverse square

Triple the distance and the force drops to a NINTH.
Not a third. The r is squared, so 3 becomes 9, 4 becomes 16, 10 becomes 100.
Halve the distance and the force goes up FOUR times.

Why squared, in one picture. Whatever the charge sends out spreads over the surface of a sphere, and a sphere's area is 4πr2. Double the radius and the same amount is spread over four times the area, so what arrives at any one spot is a quarter. Gravity thins out in exactly the same way, for exactly the same reason.

Two charges, always. If a question hands you only one charge, Coulomb's law is not the formula it wants. That is the single most useful thing to notice about it, and the next lesson is built on it.

Superposition: one at a time, then addthe method for every multi-charge question

Start with a tug of war. Three people pull on you at once. How do you work out where you end up? You do not invent a three-person formula. You take each pull separately, then add the pulls as arrows.

Charges work the same way, and the rule has a name.

Superposition

Compute one contribution at a time, then add. It works for forces, for fields and for potentials alike, and it is the only method you need for every multi-charge question on this paper.

There is one difference between the three, and it matters:

QuantityVector or scalarHow you add
Force FVectorAs arrows, direction matters
Field EVectorAs arrows, direction matters
Potential VScalarPlain arithmetic, signs and all

Your turnthree quick ones

(1) A charge of 4.8 x 10-19 C. How many elementary charges is that, and is the answer allowed?

(2) Two charges 2 cm apart feel a force of 36 N. What force do they feel at 6 cm?

(3) Why can Coulomb's law never be used with a single charge?

Answer

(1) n = q/e = 4.8 x 10-19 / 1.602 x 10-19 = 3 electrons. A whole number, so it is allowed. An answer of 3.4 would mean an arithmetic slip, because charge comes in coins.

(2) Three times the distance, so a NINTH of the force. 36/9 = 4 N. Notice you never needed the charges or k: the ratio was the whole question.

(3) Because a force acts BETWEEN two objects. One charge on its own has nothing to push against. What one charge does have is a field, and that is the next lesson.
