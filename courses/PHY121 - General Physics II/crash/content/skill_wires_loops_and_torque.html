Magnetism

Force on a Wire, Magnetic Moment and Torque

A wire is just a lot of moving charges, so the same formula applies with the charges bundled together. Bend the wire into a loop and you have built a motor.

About 12 minutes. Work every step yourself before reading on. Nothing here is a summary to skim: each box is one small piece of the skill, in order.

From one charge to a whole wire

A wire is a bundle of moving chargesreason before rule

You already know a single moving charge feels F = qvB sin θ. A current-carrying wire is nothing more than a great many charges moving together, so it feels the sum of all their forces.

Bundle them up and the formula becomes:

F = BIL sin θ

SymbolMeaningUnit
FForce on the wirenewton, N
BMagnetic flux densitytesla, T
ICurrent in the wire, replacing the moving chargeampere, A
LLength of wire actually sitting IN the fieldmetre, m
θAngle between the WIRE and the fielddegrees
Key point, and it is the same sentence as before

Maximum at 90 degrees, where sin 90 = 1 and the wire lies straight across the field.
Zero at 0 degrees, where the wire lies ALONG the field, exactly as a single charge travelling along the field feels nothing.

Only the component ACROSS the field is deflected. One charge or a wire full of them, the rule does not change.

The everyday case: a loudspeaker. A coil sits in a permanent magnet's field, the music signal drives a changing current through it, and F = BIL pushes the cone in and out. Every sound you have ever heard from a speaker is this formula.

Your turn. A 0.5 m wire carries 3 A across a 0.4 T field at right angles. Find the force. Then say what happens if the wire is turned to lie along the field.

At right angles
F = BIL sin 90
F = 0.4 x 3 x 0.5 x 1
F = 0.6 N

Along the field
θ = 0, sin 0 = 0
F = 0 N

Same wire, same current, same field, and no force at all. The angle is doing all the work.

The loop, its moment, and the motorthe compass needle again

Now bend the wire into a loop and put it in a field. One side of the loop is pushed up, the other side is pushed down, and the loop twists.

Two formulas describe it.

QuantityFormulaNote
Magnetic dipole momentm = NIAHow strong a magnet the loop is
Torque on the loopτ = mB sin θ = NIAB sin θZero when aligned

m does two completely different jobs in this course

In r = mv/(qB) and f = qB/(2πm), m is the particle's MASS in kilograms.
In m = NIA and τ = mB sin θ, m is the MAGNETIC MOMENT in A m2.

Read which quantity the question is about before you decide which m you are looking at. Nothing else distinguishes them.

You have already met this behaviour. The electric dipole obeyed τ = pE sin θ, and a compass needle swings to north and stops. The current loop obeys τ = mB sin θ, and does exactly the same thing: it twists until its moment lines up with the field, and then feels no torque at all.

That is a motor, and also the reason a motor needs a commutator. Left alone, the loop would swing to alignment and stop dead. So a real motor reverses the current every half turn, which flips the loop's moment and keeps it chasing an alignment it never reaches. The twisting never stops, and the shaft goes round.

The torque question, worked in fullrearrange in symbols first

A rectangular loop feels a torque of 4.8 x 10-3 N m in a 0.6 T field, at 30 degrees. Find the magnetic dipole moment. Then, if the loop has 50 turns each enclosing 4 x 10-4 m2, find the current.

Given
τ = 4.8 x 10-3 N m
B = 0.6 T
θ = 30 degrees, so sin 30 = 0.5 exactly

Step 1: rearrange BEFORE substituting

τ = mB sin θ   so   m = τ / (B sin θ)

Step 2: the denominator on its own

B sin θ = 0.6 x 0.5 = 0.3

Step 3: divide

m = 4.8 x 10-3 / 0.3 = 1.6 x 10-2 A m2

A m2, not kilograms. This m is a magnetic moment.

Step 4: now the current, from the loop's construction

m = NIA   so   I = m / (NA)
  • N = 50 turns
  • A = 4 x 10-4 m2
  • NA = 50 x 4 x 10-4 = 2 x 10-2
I = 1.6 x 10-2 / 2 x 10-2 = 0.8 A

Step 5: check

Both answers are ordinary sizes: a small moment and a modest current. Nothing is off by a power of ten, and the units came out right, which is step 7 doing its job.

Answer m = 1.6 x 10-2 A m2, and I = 0.8 A

Redo it. Redo the first part at 90 degrees. sin 90 = 1, so the denominator doubles to 0.6 and the moment halves to 8 x 10-3 A m2. A loop lying square across the field needs only half the moment to feel the same twist, because that is where the field grips hardest.

Your turnthree quick ones

(1) A 2 m wire carries 5 A at 30 degrees to a 0.8 T field. Find the force.

(2) A coil of 100 turns, area 2 x 10-3 m2, carries 0.5 A. Find its magnetic moment.

(3) At what angle does a current loop feel NO torque, and is that position stable?

Answer

(1) F = BIL sin θ = 0.8 x 5 x 2 x sin 30 = 0.8 x 5 x 2 x 0.5 = 4 N.

(2) m = NIA = 100 x 0.5 x 2 x 10-3 = 0.1 A m2. All three simply multiply.

(3) At 0 degrees, with the loop's moment lying ALONG the field, since sin 0 = 0. And yes, that one is stable: it is the position the field has been twisting it towards all along, exactly like a compass needle settling on north.
