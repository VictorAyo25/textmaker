emf, Internal Resistance and Terminal Voltage

One idea carries every remaining circuit question: a battery has resistance of its own, so what you measure at its terminals is always less than what it can supply.

About 13 minutes. Work every step yourself before reading on. Nothing here is a summary to skim: each box is one small piece of the skill, in order.

The one idea behind five questions

Why headlights dimreason before rule

Start with something you have seen. Start a car at night and the headlights dip for a moment, then recover.

The battery has not gone flat. Something else happened.

  1. The starter motor suddenly drew an enormous current.
  2. A battery has resistance inside itself, called internal resistance, written as a small r.
  3. That internal resistance eats some of the battery's voltage before it ever reaches the terminals. The amount it eats is Ir.
  4. The more current you draw, the more it eats. So the lights dim.

V = ε - Ir  and  ε = I(R + r)

Key point

Whenever current flows, what you MEASURE at the terminals is always less than what the battery HAS. The gap is exactly Ir.

That measured value has a name: the terminal voltage. It is the voltage across the source while current flows, and it is always below the emf.

The one edge case worth knowing: with NO current flowing, I = 0, so Ir = 0 and the terminal voltage equals the emf exactly. That is how an emf is measured in practice, with a high-resistance voltmeter drawing almost nothing.

Your turn. A question tells you the terminal p.d. is 1 V and asks for the emf. Before any arithmetic: must your answer be bigger than 1 V, or smaller?

Answer: bigger, always.

Why
V = ε - Ir
so ε = V + Ir
and Ir is never negative
Therefore
ε is greater than V whenever current flows

That single check eliminates two of the five options on Test 2 question 4 before you calculate anything. The trap option offered is 1 V itself, the number copied straight out of the question.

Say it bluntly

What you measure is always less than what the battery has.

What emf actually is, and why the name is wronga definition the exam asks for

emf stands for electromotive force, and that name has misled students for a century.

Definition

emf is the energy per unit charge supplied by a source, and it is measured in VOLTS, not newtons. It is not a force at all.

Once you see that it is energy per coulomb, everything lines up:

  • A volt IS a joule per coulomb, so emf in volts is exactly energy per unit charge.
  • That is why ε and V can be subtracted from each other in V = ε - Ir. You can only subtract like from like.
  • And it is why W = ΔQ × ε from the previous lesson gives joules: charge times energy-per-charge.

The everyday picture: think of the source as a pump lifting each coulomb to a certain height. The emf is how high it lifts them. The internal resistance is a leak in the pump itself, so what actually leaves the outlet is a little lower than the lift.

Capital R and small r are the whole topic

R is outside the battery. r is inside it.
Swap them and every emf question goes wrong.
Note too that r here is a RESISTANCE, not the distance r of the electrostatics formulas. Same letter, entirely different quantity, and the only guide is which topic you are in.

Test 2 question 4, the two-step onehardest circuit question on the paper

A cell of unknown emf ε and internal resistance 2 ohms is connected to a 5 ohm resistor. If the terminal p.d. is 1 volt, calculate ε.

Given
r = 2 Ω (internal)
R = 5 Ω (external)
V = 1 V (terminal p.d.)
ε = ?

Step 1: the terminal p.d. is the voltage across the EXTERNAL resistor, so it gives you the current.
I = V/R
  = 1/5
  = 0.2 A

Step 2: the emf must push that current through BOTH resistances.
ε = I(R + r)
  = 0.2 x (5 + 2)
  = 0.2 x 7
  = 1.4 V

Check
ε = 1.4 V is greater than V = 1 V, as it must be. The difference, 0.4 V, is the internal drop Ir = 0.2 x 2. It agrees.

Answer ε = 1.4 V

Redo it. The offered trap is 1 V, the terminal p.d. copied straight out of the question. Whenever a question gives you a terminal p.d. and asks for emf, the answer must be BIGGER. Redo it with r = 5 ohms and confirm you get 2 V.

Your turnthe mirror image

A cell has an emf of 9 V and an internal resistance of 1 ohm. The current flowing is 2 A. What is the terminal voltage?

Then, in words: what IS the terminal voltage, and what IS internal resistance?

Answer

Given
ε = 9 V
r = 1 Ω
I = 2 A

Formula
V = ε - Ir

Substitute
V = 9 - (2 x 1)
V = 9 - 2

Answer
V = 7 V

Check
7 V is LESS than the 9 V emf, as it must be.

This is the previous question run backwards: there you were given V and asked for ε, here you are given ε and asked for V. Either way the terminal voltage is the smaller of the two. An answer of 11 V would mean the internal resistance was ADDING energy, which is impossible.

In words: the terminal voltage is the voltage across a source while current flows, always less than the emf. The internal resistance is the resistance of the source itself, and it is what causes that shortfall.
