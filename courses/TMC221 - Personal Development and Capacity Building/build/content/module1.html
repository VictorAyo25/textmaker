Module One · Unit 1

What a Goal Really Is

Most people never miss their goals. They simply never set any. They live in wishes and dreams, which feel purposeful but ask nothing of them. This unit draws the exact line between a wish and a goal, and names the one kind of goal that actually changes you.

Teach: what this module trainsL1 S3
By the end of Lecture 1 you should be able to do four things: distinguish between wishes, dreams, intentions, and properly structured goals; apply at least two evidence-based goal-setting frameworks to your own life; identify the psychology behind achievement and why goals fail; and build a personal goal architecture using proven modern strategies. Every one of those four is drilled below.
Teach: clarity is the differenceL1 S2
Two roommates in 2008 each wrote about their futures. One wrote, "I want to be successful." The other wrote a precise plan: a named certification, an NGO role, and a specific sum saved, all by a specific date. By 2012 the second had done all three; the first was still writing the same sentence. The difference was not intelligence, family background, or luck. It was clarity.
"The indispensable first step to getting what you want is this: decide what you want." Ben Stein

Answer False. The slide is explicit: the difference was not intelligence, background, or luck. It was clarity, the second roommate wrote a specific, dated, measurable plan.

The four levels: not every aspiration is a goal

Teach: wish, dream, intention, goalL1 S4
An aspiration climbs through four levels. Only the fourth is a goal.
LevelExampleThe problem with it
Wish"I want to be rich"No direction or deadline
Dream"I want to own a business someday"Indefinite timeframe
Intention"I plan to start saving this year"No commitment or measure

Most people live in the wish and dream layers, which feel purposeful without requiring anything of them. A real goal is a declaration with evidence: what will happen, when, and how you will know.

Framework 2: OKRs, ambition meets evidence

Teach: Objective + Key ResultsL1 S9
Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) have been used by Google since 1999. An OKR has two parts.
  1. Objective: one bold, inspiring, qualitative statement. It answers "Where am I going?" and should be ambitious enough to feel slightly uncomfortable.
  2. Key Results (2 to 4): specific, measurable outcomes that prove the Objective was achieved. They answer "How will I know I got there?"

The critical distinction: a Key Result (KR) is not an activity. "Attend networking events" is an activity (wrong). "Secure 3 mentors by end of quarter" is a Key Result (right). Activities describe what you do; Key Results describe what changes.

Must MemoriseL1 S9
A Key Result is not an activity. Activities describe what you do ("attend events"); Key Results describe what changes ("secure 3 mentors"). This single distinction is tested again and again.
Memory hook: a KR is a result you can count, not a task you can tick.
Pick all that applyL1 S9

  • Grow to 500 connections at 3% engagement by May 31

Answer a and c. Both name a measurable change. b and d are activities: things you do, with no measured result.
TrapL1 S9
"Attend networking events" sounds like solid progress, so it is the classic trap answer. It is an activity, not a Key Result: it describes what you do, not what changes. The Key Result is "Secure 3 mentors".

Framework 3: the 12-Week Year

Teach: four fresh starts a yearL1 S11
Brian Moran's solution to lost urgency: treat every 12 weeks as a full year. When you have 12 months, your brain behaves as though there is always more time. Urgency is low in January, relevance fades by June, and panic arrives in October, too late for a full recovery. A 12-week cycle has five elements.
ElementWhat it is
1. VisionYour long-term "why" that does not change weekly
2. 12-Week GoalOne to three specific goals for this cycle only
3. Weekly PlanSpecific scheduled actions per week, written not mental
4. Weekly ScorecardPercent of planned actions completed. Target: 85% or more
5. Weekly ReviewWhat worked? What changes next week?
Fill in the GapL1 S11

In the 12-Week Year, the Weekly Scorecard tracks the percent of planned actions completed, with a target of or more.

Answer 85%.

  • 12 weeks is the scientifically optimal duration for all human goal-setting
  • When timelines are short and repeated, urgency stays psychologically active; annual timelines let the brain perceive abundance of time and procrastinate
  • Annual goals are outdated and should never be used
Answer c. This is a real test question. The point is urgency and time perception, not that 12 weeks is magic or that annual plans are banned.

Framework 4: Implementation Intentions

Teach: pre-decide the momentL1 S12
An Implementation Intention pre-decides behaviour so your future self does not get to negotiate in the moment. The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer found that people who specified when and where they would act followed through two to three times more often than those who only stated strong intentions. The formula is: "When [specific cue] occurs, I will [specific behaviour]." The cue must be concrete, a time, a place, or a completed action, never "when I feel motivated". In the research, specifying exactly when and where gave a 71% completion rate, against 32% for intention only.
Must MemoriseL1 S12
The Implementation Intention formula: "When [cue], I will [behaviour]." The cue must be concrete (time, place, or completed action), never emotional. Gollwitzer's numbers: 71% follow-through with a specified when-and-where, versus 32% with intention only.
Memory hook: When-Then beats want-to. 71 over 32. Concrete cue, no negotiation.
Fill in the GapL1 S12

Answer 71%; 32%.

Answer False. "When I feel stressed" is an emotional cue, not a concrete one. A valid cue is a time, place, or completed action. This is a real test question, and the trap is that it looks correctly structured.

Teach: the six steps to build oneL1 S13
To build an Implementation Intention:
  1. Name the goal behaviour: one specific action.
  2. Identify your failure mode: when and why it typically does not happen.
  3. Find a reliable daily cue: a time, a place, or an existing routine.
  4. Write the if-then plan: "When [cue], I will [action]."
  5. Pre-empt the obstacle: "If [temptation], then I will [counter-action]."
  6. Chain it to an existing habit for automatic triggering.

The Psychology of Achievement

Goals rarely fail from laziness. They fail from three predictable psychological forces, and they are sustained not by willpower and inspiration but by environment and systems.

Teach: three reasons goals failL1 S15
None of the three is laziness.
ReasonWhat it isThe fix
Planning FallacyWe overestimate short-term capacity and underestimate long-term. Add a 40% time buffer.Work backwards from the deadline; build in the buffer.
Motivation DecayMotivation peaks at the start and end. Weeks 3 to 8, the dangerous middle, is when most quit.Design your system for the middle, not the launch.
Willpower DepletionSelf-control tires with use; relying on it alone is a losing strategy.Design your environment so the right behaviour needs least resistance.

Teach: WOOP in practiceL1 S17
WOOP stands for Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, and Plan (WOOP). Its power is mental contrasting: you visualise the win, then deliberately confront the obstacle, which outperforms positive visualisation alone.
  1. Wish: state your most important goal clearly and specifically.
  2. Outcome: visualise the best result vividly. How does it feel to get there?
  3. Obstacle: name the biggest internal obstacle honestly. What has stopped you before?
  4. Plan: write the if-then response, "If [obstacle], then I will [action]."

Example. Wish: finish my research proposal this month. Outcome: my supervisor approves it; I feel proud and ahead. Obstacle: I get distracted by social media when working alone. Plan: when I sit to write, I switch my phone to airplane mode first.
