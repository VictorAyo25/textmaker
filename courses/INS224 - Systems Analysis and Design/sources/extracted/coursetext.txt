    Unit 1 — The System Analyst and Information System Development 
 
INTRODUCTION 
 
Organisations rely heavily on information systems to improve effi-
ciency, support decision-making, and gain a competitive edge. At the 
centre of this process is the system analyst, a professional responsi-
ble for understanding business needs and translating them into ef-
fective technological solutions. This lesson introduces the role of the 
system analyst, the skills required to perform the job, and the stages 
involved in developing information systems. By the end, learners will 
understand how system analysts bridge the gap between users, 
business processes, and technical teams to ensure that information 
systems are well-designed, reliable, and aligned with organisational 
goals.

  1.1.1 
What is System Analysis?  
 
System Analysis is the systematic process of studying an existing or proposed sys-
tem to understand its structure, functions, processes, and interactions in order to 
identify problems, determine requirements, and recommend improvements or so-
lutions. 
In the context of information systems development, system analysis examines how 
an organisation collects, processes, stores, and uses information to ensure the sys-
tem effectively supports business objectives. 
System Analysis can also be defined as the process of understanding and specifying 
in detail what an information system should accomplish. 
System analysis is all about:  
 Understanding the business and its goals and strategies,  
 Defining requirements for information systems that support those goals and 
strategies and  
 Supporting the business. 
Purpose of System Analysis 
 To ensure that the system meets user and organisational needs 
 To reduce the risk of system failure 
 To provide a clear foundation for system design and development 
 To improve efficiency, accuracy, and decision-making within the organization 
System Design is the phase of system development that involves translating the 
requirements identified during system analysis into a detailed blueprint for building 
the system. It defines how the system will be structured, how its components will 
interact, and how it will operate to meet specified functional and non-functional 
requirements. 
Systems Design specifies in detail how the many components of an information sys-
tem should be physically implemented.  
 
  1.1.2 
Who is a Systems Analyst? 
 
A Systems Analyst is a professional who investigates, analyses, and evaluates an or-
ganisation’s information systems and business processes with the objective of de-
signing or improving systems that effectively support organisational goals.

The systems analyst acts as a link between business users and technical develop-
ment teams, ensuring that business needs are accurately translated into technical 
specifications. 
Roles and Responsibilities of a Systems Analyst 
1. Requirements Analysis: The analyst gathers, analyses, and documents user 
and system requirements using structured techniques. 
2. System Evaluation: Analyst studies existing systems to identify problems, in-
efficiencies, and areas for improvement. 
3. Feasibility Studies: Analyst assesses technical, economic, operational, legal, 
and schedule feasibility of proposed systems. 
4. System Modelling and Documentation: The analyst prepares models such as 
Data Flow Diagrams (DFDs), Use Case Diagrams, and Entity-Relationship Di-
agrams (ERDs). 
5. Liaison Role: The analyst handles communication between system stakehold-
ers to ensure mutual understanding. 
6. Solution Recommendation: Proposes suitable system solutions, including 
hardware, software, and process improvements. 
7. Support During Development and Implementation: Assists in system design, 
testing, user training, and system deployment. 
We can define a systems analyst as a business professional who uses analysis and 
design techniques to solve business problems using information technology. Essen-
tially, a system analyst is a problem solver. 
An analyst's role requires extensive technical, business, and people knowledge and 
skills to solve business problems. 
How does an analyst solve problems? 
The analyst must first understand the problem and learn everything possible about 
it, including: 
 Who is involved,  
 What business processes come into play, and  
 What other systems would be affected by solving the problem?  
Then the analyst needs to confirm with management that the benefits of solving the 
problem outweigh the costs. 
If solving the problem is feasible, the analyst defines in detail what is required to 
solve it:

 What specific objectives must be satisfied, 
 What data needs to be stored and used,  
 What processing must be done to the data, and what outputs must be pro-
duced.  
What needs to be done must be defined first. How it will be done is not important 
yet. 
Many different alternatives must be considered, and the challenge is to select the 
best, that is, the solution with the fewest risks and most benefits. 
After the systems analyst has determined the best solution in consultation with 
management, the design details must then be worked out.  
The analyst is now concerned with creating a blueprint (design specifications) for 
the new system. 
Systems design specifications describe the construction details of all system com-
ponents, including databases, networks, program modules, operating procedures, 
user interfaces, and conversion plans. 
After detailed design comes programming. Building a system based on incomplete 
or misunderstood requirements may lead to  
 Over budgeting,  
 Late delivery,  
 Or delivery of a system that doesn’t fully solve the problems it was intended 
to address.

  1.1.3 
Information Systems –the Business Solution 
 
A systems analyst is described as a business problem solver, and the solution to the 
problem is usually an information system.  
Some basic information system concepts are: 
A system is a collection of interrelated components that function together to 
achieve some outcome. 
An information system is a collection of interrelated components that collect, pro-
cess, store, and provide as output the information needed to complete business 
tasks. It can also be described as an arrangement of people, data, processes, and 
information technology that interact to collect, process, store, and provide as output 
the information needed to support an organisation.  

===== PAGE 9 =====

Its purpose is to process input, maintain data, produce reports, handle queries, han-
dle online transactions, and generate other outputs. 
Any system can have subsystems. A subsystem is a system that is part of another 
system, so subsystems might be one way to think of a system's components.   
For example, a sales management system might be one subsystem of a customer 
relationship management (CRM) system.  
Another subsystem in the CRM might enable customers to view past and current 
orders, track order fulfilment and shipping, and modify their account information. 
The view of a system as a collection of subsystems is very useful to the analyst. 
It enables the analyst to focus attention on a single area of a business or organiza-
tion, a group of related areas, or the interfaces among areas. 
This approach to dividing a system into components is called functional decompo-
sition.

     Unit 2 — System Development Life Cycle 
 
INTRODUCTION 
 
The System Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is a framework for plan-
ning the development of an information system in an organised 
manner. It provides a step-by-step approach that guides system de-
velopers and analysts from the identification of a business need 
through the delivery and support of a completed system. Infor-
mation systems are important to efficient operations, decision-
making, and competitive advantage. However, developing such sys-
tems without a clear methodology often leads to cost overruns, 
delayed delivery, or systems that fail to meet user requirements. 
The SDLC addresses these challenges by defining distinct phases, 
activities, and deliverables that ensure quality and control through-
out the system development process. This unit introduces the 
SDLC, explains its key phases, and highlights the activities and de-
liverables at each phase.

  1.2.1 
System Development 
 
One of the fundamental concepts in information system development is the sys-
tems development life cycle. Businesses and organisations use information sys-
tems to support the processes they need to carry out their functions.  
The entire process of building, deploying, using, and updating an information sys-
tem is called the systems development life cycle, or SDLC. 
 
  1.2.2 
Principles of a Successful Systems Development 
 
 Get the user involved: Involving users throughout the processes helps en-
sure that requirements are accurate, reduces resistance to change, and in-
creases user acceptance of the final system. 
 Use a problem-solving approach: System development should focus on 
solving real business problems rather than merely applying technology. De-
velopers and analysts must first understand the problem, evaluate alterna-
tive solutions, and then select the option that provides the greatest benefit 
with the least risk. 
 Establish phases and activities: Dividing system development into clearly 
defined phases and activities provides structure and control. This phased 
approach makes projects easier to manage, monitor, and evaluate, ensuring 
that each step is completed before moving to the next. 
 Establish standards for development and documentation: Standards en-
sure consistency, quality, and clarity across system development activities. 
Using standard methods, tools, and documentation formats makes systems 
easier to understand, maintain, and enhance over time. 
 Justify systems as capital investments: Information systems require sig-
nificant financial resources and should be evaluated like other capital invest-
ments. Cost–benefit analysis helps management determine whether the ex-
pected benefits justify the costs and risks involved. 
 Don't be afraid to cancel or revise scope: If a project becomes too costly, 
risky, or no longer aligns with organisational goals, it may be necessary to 
revise its scope or cancel it entirely. Early termination or scope adjustment 
can prevent further resource waste. 
 Divide and conquer: Breaking a large system into smaller, manageable 
components or subsystems simplifies development and reduces complexity. 
This principle allows teams to focus on individual parts while ensuring the 
overall system works as a whole. 

===== PAGE 17 =====
 
INS224 | System Analysis and Design 
 
16 
 Design systems for growth and change: Systems should be flexible and 
scalable to accommodate future business expansion, technological ad-
vancements, and changing user requirements. Designing for change ex-
tends the system’s useful life and protects the organisation’s investment. 
 Proper planning and project management: Effective planning and project 
management ensure that system development stays on schedule, within 
budget, and meets quality expectations. This includes setting realistic time-
lines, allocating resources, managing risks, and monitoring progress 
throughout the project.  
 
  1.2.3 
Approaches to SDLC 
 
In system development, approaches are often grouped into predictive (traditional) 
and adaptive models based on how they handle requirements, change, and uncer-
tainty. 
A predictive approach assumes that the development process can be planned and 
organised in advance and that the new information system can be developed ac-
cording to the plan. In this approach, the project progresses through a fixed se-
quence of phases, with each phase completed before the next begins. Predictive 
SDLCs are useful for building systems that are well-understood and well-defined. 
An adaptive approach is an SDLC approach that is more flexible, assuming the 
project cannot be planned in full in advance and must be modified as it progresses. 
The adaptive approach is designed to handle changing requirements and uncer-
tainty. Instead of following a rigid sequence, development is carried out in short 
cycles or iterations, with continuous user feedback and incremental delivery of sys-
tem functionality. 
In predictive approaches, the first group of activities, usually called project planning, 
involves planning, organising, and scheduling the project. These activities map out 
the overall structure of the project.  
The next group of activities focuses on understanding the business problem that 
needs to be solved and defining the business requirements. This set of activities is 
referred to as analysis activities. The intent is to understand exactly what the sys-
tem must do to support the business processes.  
A third group of activities focuses on designing the new system. Those activities, 
called design activities, use the requirements that were defined earlier to develop 
the program structure and algorithms for the new system. 

===== PAGE 18 =====

The fourth group of activities is necessary to build the system. This is called imple-
mentation activities, and it includes programming, testing, and installing the sys-
tem for business users. 
These four groups of activities (planning, analysis, design, and implementation) 
are referred to as phases, and they are the elements that provide the framework for 
managing a project.  
Another phase, called the support phase, includes activities to upgrade and main-
tain the system after deployment.  
The support phase is part of the overall SDLC but is not normally considered part of 
the initial development project.

In the adaptive SDLC approach, system development activities are not performed 
once in a fixed sequence. Instead, they are repeated in short cycles known as itera-
tions or sprints, with each cycle producing a working version of the system or a 
system component. Typical phases in the adaptive approach can be described as 
follows: 
Initiation and requirements planning: At the start of the project, a high-level sys-
tem scope is defined. This includes identifying the business problem, the overall 
objectives, the key stakeholders, and the major system features. Detailed require-
ments are not fully specified at this stage. 
Requirements are captured at a high level and prioritised based on business value 
and urgency. These requirements are often documented as user stories or feature 
lists. 
Iterative Analysis and Design: Analysis and design are carried out incrementally 
during each iteration. Only the requirements selected for the current iteration are 
analysed and designed in detail.

short cycles. Each iteration results in a working and testable piece of the system. 
Continuous Testing and Quality Assurance: Testing is performed throughout the 
development process rather than at the end. This ensures early detection and cor-
rection of defects.

In-Text Questions (ITQs) 
Why is it important to choose an appropriate software development approach for 
successful system development? 
In-Text Answers (ITAs) 
The selected approach determines how requirements are gathered, how changes 
are managed, how risks are controlled, and how the final system is delivered. An 
approach that aligns with project characteristics such as size, complexity, and user 
involvement increases the likelihood of meeting project objectives. 
For example, a predictive approach is effective when requirements are well under-
stood and unlikely to change. It provides clear structure, documentation, and con-
trol for the development process. On the other hand, an adaptive approach is better 
suited to dynamic business environments that require flexibility and rapid response 
to change. Selecting the wrong approach can lead to cost overruns, delays, user 
dissatisfaction, and system failure. Therefore, careful evaluation of organisational 
needs, project constraints, and stakeholder expectations is important when select-
ing a development approach to ensure successful system delivery. 
 
 
  1.2.4 
Software Development Life Cycle 
 
The entire process of building, deploying, using, and updating an information sys-
tem is called the systems development life cycle, or SDLC. It ensures that systems 
are developed in a disciplined and systematic manner to meet user requirements, 
organisational objectives, and quality standards. The SDLC is commonly organised 
into five major phases: Planning, Analysis, Design, Implementation, and Sup-
port. 
Planning Phase 
The planning phase focuses on identifying the need for a new information system 
and determining whether the project is feasible and worthwhile. The primary objec-
tives of project planning are to identify the scope of the new system, ensure that 
the project is feasible, and develop a schedule, resource plan, and budget for the 
remainder of the project. 
 

===== PAGE 21 =====
 
INS224 | System Analysis and Design 
 
20 
We identify five activities in project planning: 
 Define the problem (business problem & scope) 
 Produce the project schedule. 
 Confirm project feasibility: Feasibility analysis investigates economic, or-
ganisational, technical, resource, and schedule feasibility. 
 Staff the project: allocating funds, assigning project members, and obtain-
ing other necessary resources such as office and development tools. 
 Launch the project. 
Analysis Phase 
The analysis phase involves studying the existing system and business processes to 
determine detailed system requirements. The primary objective of the analysis ac-
tivities is to understand and document the business needs and the processing re-
quirements of the new system. Analysis is essentially a discovery process. The key 
words that drive the activities during analysis are discovery and understanding. 
Six primary activities are considered part of this phase: 
 Gather information: observing the users as they do their work; by inter-
viewing and asking questions of the users; by reading existing documents 
about procedures, business rules, and job responsibilities; and by reviewing 
existing automated systems. 
 Define system requirements: Analysts must review, analyse, and structure 
the information obtained to develop an overall understanding of the new 
system’s requirements. 
 Prioritise requirements. 
 Build prototype model for discovery of requirements: Process modelling, 
data modelling, etc. 
 Generate and evaluate alternatives. 
 Review recommendations with management. 
Design Phase 
The design phase translates system requirements into detailed specifications that 
describe how the system will be built. The objective of the design activities is to 
design the solution system based on the requirements defined and decisions 
made during analysis.  
High-level design consists of developing an architectural structure for the software 
components, databases, user interface, and operating environment.  

===== PAGE 22 =====
 
INS224 | System Analysis and Design 
 
21 
Low-level design entails developing the detailed algorithms and data structures that 
are required for software development.  
Major activities that must be completed during the design phase: 
 Design the application architecture. 
 Design and integrate the network. 
 Design the user and system interfaces. 
 Design and integrate the database. 
 Design and integrate the system controls. 
Implementation Phase 
The implementation phase involves building, testing, and deploying the system 
based on the design specifications. Implementation activities result in the final 
system being built, tested, and installed. The objective is to produce a reliable, fully 
functional information system and to ensure that all users are trained to use the 
system effectively and efficiently.  
Five major activities make up the implementation phase: 
 Construct software components. 
 Verify and test. 
 Convert data. 
 Train users and document the system. 
 Install the system. 
System construction is the first step in the implementation phase. The system is built 
and tested to ensure it performs as designed.  
Since the cost of bugs can be immense, testing is one of the most critical steps in 
implementation.  
Most organisations spend more time and attention on testing than on writing the 
programs in the first place.

What is Software System Development Methodology? 
 
System development methodology refers to the structured approaches, models, 
and techniques used to plan, analyse, design, implement, and maintain information 
systems. Developing information systems without a clear methodology often leads 
to cost overruns, missed deadlines, and systems that fail to meet user and business 
requirements. 
Systems Development Methodology is the standard process followed by an organ-
isation to conduct all the steps necessary to analyse, design, implement, and main-
tain information systems. systems. It is a framework used to structure, plan, and 
control the development of an information system. 
Some methodologies include: Waterfall, Rapid Application Development (RAD), 
Prototyping, Incremental model, Agile, etc. 
A. The Waterfall Model 
The Waterfall methodology is a traditional, linear approach to system development 
in which each phase must be completed before the next phase begins. Here, the 
development proceeds sequentially from one phase to another in a manner like the 
natural waterfall. 
The key deliverables for each phase are usually presented to the approval commit-
tee and project sponsor for approval before the project moves to the next phase. 
As the project progresses from phase to phase, it follows the same waterfall model. 
Strict monitoring is ensured throughout the project through extensive written doc-
umentation, as well as formal reviews and user and information technology man-
agement sign-off at the end of each phase. 
 
Figure 1: Schematic view of the waterfall model

This model has two very important variants: Parallel Development and V-Model  
Advantages of the Waterfall Model 
1. Easy to understand, easy to use 
2. Provides structure to inexperienced staff 
3. The sequential approach and strict controls help to ensure adequate docu-
mentation and design reviews, which result in quality, reliability, and main-
tainability of the developed software  
4. Progress of system development is measurable  
Disadvantages of the Waterfall Model 
1. Inflexible, slow, costly and cumbersome due to significant structure and tight 
controls  
2. Project progresses forward, with only slight movement backward can give a 
false impression of progress. 
3. Little opportunity for the customer to preview the system (until it may be too 
late). 
4. Problems are often not discovered until system testing  
5. Difficult to respond to changes.  
6. Produces excessive documentation, and keeping it updated as the project 
progresses is time-consuming  
When to Use Waterfall 
 Project is for the development of a transaction-oriented batch system.  
 Project is large, expensive, and complicated.  
 Project has clear objectives and a solution.  
 No pressure for immediate implementation.  
 Project requirements are clear and comprehensive.  
 Project requirements are stable during the system development life cycle.  
 When the project team are not so experienced or may change during imple-
mentation. 
Variants of the Waterfall Model 
Parallel Model 
The Parallel Model is a variant of the traditional Waterfall model designed to reduce 
the long development time associated with strictly sequential phases. While it re-
tains the structured and predictive nature of the Waterfall approach, it allows certain

after the other. 
In the Parallel Model, the overall system is divided into subprojects or modules after 
the general analysis and high-level design phases are completed. Each subproject 
then moves through the remaining phases, detailed design, implementation, and 
testing at the same time as other subprojects. Once all subprojects are completed, 
they are integrated into a single system.

as shown in Figure 3. 
In the V-Shape Model, development activities proceed down the left side of the “V,” 
while testing activities move up the right side. For every development phase, a cor-
responding testing phase is planned in parallel. This ensures that verification and 
validation are built into the system development process from the beginning rather 
than being deferred until the end.

C. Incremental Model 
The Incremental model develops the system in small, manageable portions called 
increments. Each increment adds new functionality and builds on previous releases. 
Combines linear and iterative system development methodologies with the aim of 
reducing inherent project risks by breaking a project into smaller segments. It is 
between waterfall and prototyping methods. Customers outline the services re-
quired of the system; these services are then prioritised and grouped into incre-
ments; each increment provides a subset of the system’s functionality. 
The increments are prioritised, and the first increment is defined in detail and de-
veloped. As subsequent increments are developed, they are integrated with existing 
increments; as such, the system functionality increases with each increment. Further 
requirements analysis can still be performed for later increments. However, changes 
for the current increment cannot be accepted. 
Advantages of the Incremental Model 
1. Develop high-risk or major functions first 
2. Each release delivers an operational product 
3. Lowers initial delivery cost  
4. Uses “divide and conquer” breakdown of tasks 
5. Initial product delivery is faster 
6. Customers get important functionality early; they don’t have to wait until the 
entire system is delivered before they can gain value from it 
7. Risk of changing requirements and overall project failure is reduced 
Disadvantages of the Incremental Model 
1. It might be difficult to map customers’ requirements to increments of the 
right size 
2. Most systems require functionalities used by different parts of the system; 
requirements are not defined in detail until an increment is to be developed, 
and it might be difficult to identify common facilities needed by all require-
ments  
3. Requires good planning and design 
4. Requires early definition of a complete and fully functional system to allow 
for the definition of increments 
5. Well-defined module interfaces are required (some will be developed long 
before others)

Prototyping is a type of development in which prototypes are developed early in 
the process to enable early feedback and analysis. A “quick and dirty” version of the 
system that contains minimal features is built. Following user reactions and com-
ments, the developers reanalyse, redesign, and reimplement a second prototype 
that addresses deficiencies and adds more features. This cycle continues until the 
analysts, users, and sponsor agree that the prototype provides enough functionality 
to be installed and used in the organisation.

Selecting the appropriate methodology 
 
The first challenge for project managers is selecting the methodology to use. Choos-
ing a methodology is not simple, because no one methodology is always best. Be-
low are some criteria to use: 
Clarity of User Requirements: When the user requirements for what the system 
should do are unclear, they are difficult to understand through discussion or written 
reports. System prototyping and throwaway prototyping are usually more appropri-
ate when user requirements are unclear, because they provide prototypes for users 
to interact with early in the SDLC. 
Familiarity with Technology: If the system is designed without familiarity with the 
base technology, risks increase because the tools may not be capable of doing what 
is needed. Throwaway prototyping is particularly appropriate for situations where 
there is a lack of familiarity with technology, because it explicitly encourages the 
developers to create design prototypes for areas with high risks 
System Complexity: Complex systems require careful and detailed analysis and de-
sign. Throwaway prototyping is particularly well-suited to such detailed analysis and 
design, but system prototyping is not. 
System Reliability: For some applications, reliability is truly critical (e.g., medical 
equipment, missile control systems), while for other applications it is merely im-
portant (e.g., games, Internet video). The V-model is useful when reliability is im-
portant, as it emphasizes testing. Throwaway prototyping is most appropriate when 
system reliability is a high priority, because detailed analysis and design phases are 
combined with the project team's ability to test many different approaches through 
design prototypes before completing the design. 
Short Time Schedules: Projects with short schedules are well-suited for RAD meth-
odologies because they are designed to accelerate development. Iterative develop-
ment and system prototyping are excellent choices when timelines are short be-
cause they enable the project team to adjust system functionality to meet a specific 
delivery date. 
Schedule Visibility: RAD methodologies move many critical design decisions earlier 
in the project to help project managers recognize and address risk factors and keep 
expectations in check.

  2.1.1 
Requirements Discovery 
 
Requirements discovery is a major activity in systems analysis that focuses on iden-
tifying, understanding, and documenting what users and organisations need from 
an information system. Requirements discovery (RD) is the process and techniques 
used by systems analysts to identify and extract system problems (undesirable situ-
ations that prevent the organisation from fully achieving its mission, vision, goals, 
and objectives) and solution requirements from the user community. This process 
involves the system analyst and the users, working together. Requirements discov-
ery requires analytical, communication, and interpersonal skills. Analysts must listen 
carefully, ask the right questions, resolve conflicting requirements, and validate find-
ings with stakeholders to ensure accuracy and completeness. 
Requirements discovery begins with an analysis of the current system or business 
process to identify problems and inefficiencies that necessitate the development of 
an improved or new information system. Systems analysts engage users, managers, 
and other stakeholders to understand how work is performed, what information is 
used, and what outcomes are expected.  
In the process, two types of requirements are identified: functional and non-func-

System requirements 
 
System requirements are statements that specify what an information system should 
do and the conditions under which it must operate. They define the expected func-
tions, behaviour, and constraints of a system and serve as a formal agreement be-
tween stakeholders and the development team. System requirements are developed

during the system analysis phase and provide the basis for system design, imple-
mentation, testing, and evaluation. 
In simple terms, system requirements are what the information system must do 
(functional requirements) or what it must have (non-functional requirements). It is 
also called a business requirement. System requirements translate user needs and 
business objectives into testable specifications. They ensure that the system ad-
dresses organisational problems, supports business processes, and delivers meas-
urable value.  
Results of Incorrect Requirements 
 The system may cost more than projected. 
 The system may be delivered later than promised. 
 The system may not meet the users’ expectations, and that dissatisfaction

 Once in production, the costs of maintaining and enhancing the system may 
be excessively high. 
 The system may be unreliable, prone to errors, and downtime. 
 The reputation of the IT staff on the team is tarnished because any failure, 
regardless of who is at fault, will be perceived as a mistake by the team.

Documenting and Analysing Requirements 
Documenting requirements involves formally recording them in a clear, unambigu-

Requirements Definition Document – A formal document that communicates the

The draft requirements are documented with various tools: 
 Use cases: Use cases describe how users (actors) interact with an infor-

(functional or non-functional), source, priority, and status.

all requirements are addressed during design, development, and testing.

  2.2.1 
USE CASES MODELING 
 
Use cases help clarify users’ interactions with the system and can reveal the new 
system's functional requirements. Use case diagrams are widely used in the analysis 
phase to involve users in the requirements elicitation process. “A use case represents 
how a system interacts with its environment by illustrating the activities that are 
performed by the users of the system and the system’s responses.” (Dennis, Wixon 
& Roth). Analysts usually create use cases to describe all the tasks that users need 
to perform with the system. Use cases are regarded as an external or functional 
perspective on a business process, showing how users interact with it rather than its 
internal procedures or technical details. 
Importance of Use Case in Analysis and Design 
 Use cases help to identify and clarify details about the functional require-
ments of the new system. Most system developers agreed that a well-devel-
oped use case diagram captures most of the system's functional require-
ments.  
 Use cases are very helpful in understanding exceptions, special cases, and 
error handling requirements in the new system. These are easily overlooked 
by developers, but use cases help to discover them.  
 The text-based use case that is the use case narrative is easy for the users to 
understand, but it also flows easily into the creation of process models (Unit 
3) and the data model (Unit 4). 
Use-case modelling is the process of modelling a system’s functions in terms of 
business events, who initiates them, and how the system responds to them. 
Two primary outputs or products are involved when performing use case modelling:  
i. 
the use case diagram and  
ii. 
the use case narrative  
Use-case diagram 
A use-case diagram graphically depicts the system as a collection of use cases, ac-
tors (users), and their relationships. It depicts the interactions between the system, 
external systems, and the user. The diagram provides a high-level view of the busi-
ness events the system must process. It graphically depicts who will use the system 
and how users expect to interact with it. Use cases describe the system's functions 
from the perspectives of external users, in a manner and terminology they under-
stand. Figure 1 depicts a simple use case diagram.

Elements of Use Diagram 
Use cases: Use cases describe the system functions from the perspectives of exter-
nal users and in a manner and terminology they understand. A use case documents 
the interaction required between the user and the system to accomplish the user’s 
task. Use cases can also be referred to as system activities. Use cases are created to 
help the development team better understand the steps required to achieve the 
user’s goals. Once created, use cases can often be used to derive more detailed 
functional requirements for the new system. Use cases are represented by Eclipse 
with the name written inside.  
 
Actors: Use cases are triggered by external users called actors. An actor is a use case 
for completing a business task that produces something of measurable value. For 
example, in a course registration system, the student is an actor, and the use case 
or business event is the registration of a course or course registration. Actors are 
always outside the system boundary and initiate or participate in use cases by send-
ing inputs to the system or receiving outputs from it. Each actor is defined based on 
what they do in relation to the system.

Actor Symbol 
There are primarily four types of actors: 
i. 
Primary business actor: This actor primarily benefits from the execution of 
the use case by receiving something of measurable or observable value. For

ii. 
Primary system actor: This actor directly interfaces with the system to ini-

Use-case narrative  
A use-case narrative is the textual description of a use-case. It describes how an 
actor interacts with a system to achieve a specific goal. It explains the sequence of 
events that occur between the user and the system step by step. This provides a 
clear understanding of system behaviour without focusing on technical implemen-
tation details. 
A use-case narrative contains the name of the use case, the primary actor, a brief 
description of the goal, and the main flow of events, also known as the basic course 
of action. It may also include alternative flows and exception flows, which describe 
variations in user actions or system responses when conditions differ, or errors oc-
cur. 
Basic Elements of a Use Case Narrative 
Use Case Name and Unique Identifier: Name is a short text that describes the 
action being performed in the system. Example: Withdraw Money, Check Balance. 
The identifier is a unique number used to reference the use case. 
Actor(s): Actors are the people, systems, or external entities that interact with the 
system. 
Description / Goal: This explains what the actor wants to achieve by performing 
the use case. It provides a summary of the use case's purpose and a quick under-
standing of its objective. 
Example: Allows the customer to withdraw cash from their account. 
Preconditions: Conditions that must be true before the use case begins. If these 
conditions are not met, the use case cannot proceed.

For example, to withdraw money, a customer must have a valid ATM card and enter 
a correct PIN. 
Main Flow (Basic Flow): This lists the step-by-step sequence of actions between 
the actor and the system. It describes the ideal scenario in which everything works 
correctly, showing how the use case operates from start to finish. It is usually num-
bered for clarity. 
Alternative Flows (Extensions): Describes variations, exceptions, or error situations 
that may occur. It illustrates a real-world scenario in which things may not go as 
planned, often referred to as a branch off the main flow. 
For example, in a cash withdrawal use case, the PIN may be invalid, or there may be 
insufficient funds in the account. 
Postconditions: Describes what must be true after the use case is completed. It 
shows the system's final state and confirms the outcome of the use case. 
Example: Account balance updated

Requirements Modelling 
 
Requirements use-case modelling is a structured approach for identifying, analys-
ing, and representing system requirements through use cases. It focuses on captur-
ing how users interact with a system to achieve specific goals.  
The system can be modelled “as-is” or “to be” 
Modelling a system “As-Is” refers to documenting and representing the current state 
of an existing system before changes or improvements are made. It focuses on how 
the system currently operates, including its processes, data flows, rules, users, and 
technologies. Modelling “as-is” is good for gaining a clear and accurate understand-
ing of the existing environment without introducing assumptions or proposed so-
lutions. 
Modelling the “To-Be” system involves representing the desired future state of a 
system after improvements or a new information system have been implemented. 
It describes how the system should operate to better support business goals. 
The steps required to produce this model are: 
1. Identify business actors. 
2. Identify business requirements and use cases. 
3. Construct a use-case model diagram. 
4. Document business requirement use-case narrative 
Identify business actors. 
Actors are users of the system. Actors indicate candidates we can later interview and 
observe to complete the use-case modelling effort. Actors also determine the com-
pleteness of the system requirements, as they can be used to verify and validate the 
use cases upon completion. 
Actors should be named with a noun or noun phrase.

     Unit 3 — Process Modelling 
 
INTRODUCTION 
 
This unit discusses how the requirements definition can be clarified 
through a process model. Process modelling is important in the anal-
ysis and design of software systems. It helps analysts and developers 
understand user requirements, identify system functionality, data 
flow, and data store, and ensure that the final system aligns with 
business needs. A process model is a graphical representation of 
how a business system should operate. It illustrates the processes 
and activities performed and how data move among them. A pro-
cess model can be used to document the current system (i.e., as-is 
system) or the new system being developed (i.e., to-be system) 
(Dennis, Wixon & Roth, 2012). Modelling a process “as-is” helps or-
ganisations to identify inefficiencies, delays, or redundancies and 
can then design an improved version (the “to-be” model) that en-
hances performance, reduces cost, and increases productivity.

Data Flow Diagram (DFD) 
 
The tool for process modelling is a Data Flow Diagram (DFD). A Data Flow Diagram 
shows business processes and the data that flows between them. A DFD depicts the 
flow of data through a system and the work or processing performed by that system. 
Thus, a Data Flow Diagram is a graphical representation of how data moves through 
a system. DFD shows the source of data input, the processes that act on the data, 
and the data store, without focusing on technical details such as programming. 
DFDs allow the analyst to determine:  
 What does the organisation do?  
 How does the organisation do it? 
NOTE: 
 DFDs are purely logical, not physical models.  
 DFDs contain no implementation details.  
 DFD is drawn first because it allows designers and users to concentrate purely 
on capturing business aspects.  
 DFD is not contaminated by distractions of technical details.

Common Mechanical Errors in DFD 
When developing system models using DFD, three (3) common errors need to be 
avoided. These also help system analysts to identify the correctness of the model 
that is being produced. 
The errors are: 
1. Black hole: A black hole is when a process has inputs but no outputs. Data 
enters the process and then disappears. 
2. Miracle: A miracle is when a process has outputs but no input.  
3. Gray hole: A gray hole is when the inputs of a process are insufficient to 
produce the output 
In Figure 4, Process 3.12 has inputs but no outputs. This is a black hole because data 
enters the process and then disappears. In most cases, the modeller simply forgot 
the output. 
Process 3.1.2 has outputs but no input. This is a miracle! In this case, the input flows 
were likely forgotten. 
Process 3: The inputs are insufficient to produce the output. This is a gray hole. There 
are several possible causes, including:  
a. a misnamed process,  
b. misnamed inputs and/or outputs,  
c. incomplete facts.  
Gray holes are the most common errors – and the most embarrassing. Once handed 
to a programmer, the input data that flows to a process (to be implemented as a 
program) must be sufficient to produce the output data that flows.

Conclusion 
 
DFD is a process model. Logical process models describe processes without sug-
gesting how they are conducted. Logical models show what a system ‘is’ or ‘does’,

technical results. Logical models allow us to communicate with end-users in non-

tities. A revised version is then created to improve clarity and minimise crossing data 
flows. In practice, a DFD is refined multiple times before reaching its final form.  
After the Level 0 DFD that identifies all the major processes in the system, create 
lower-level DFDs for each process in the Level 0 DFD that needs a Level 1 DFD. This

     Unit 4 — Data Modelling and Entity-Relationship Diagramming 
 
INTRODUCTION 
 
Think about registering for courses on your school's portal. Behind 
that simple screen, the system is quietly keeping track of two things: 
you (a Student) and the courses you pick (a Course), plus the link 
between them showing exactly which courses you registered for. De-
ciding what pieces of information to keep, and how they connect, is 
exactly what this unit is about. 
The previous unit looked at process modelling, which shows what a 
system does and how data moves through it using a Data Flow Dia-
gram (DFD). This unit looks at the data itself. Data modelling is the 
analysis of data objects and the relationships between them. It is 
usually the first step in database design, and it produces a diagram 
called an Entity-Relationship Diagram (ERD). 
While a DFD answers the question "what does the system do with 
data?", an ERD answers the question "what data does the system 
need to keep, and how is that data connected?" The two models 
complement each other: the DFD shows the verbs of the system, 
and the ERD shows the nouns. Together they form the full require-
ments picture needed before a database can be built.

  2.4.1 
What is Data Modelling? 
 
Data modelling is the process of identifying the data objects (entities) an organisa-
tion needs to keep track of, the properties of each object, and how the objects relate 
to one another. A data model is a diagram that shows these relationships in a way 
that is independent of any database technology, so the same model could later be 
implemented in Microsoft Access, MySQL, or any other database system without 
changing the underlying design. It gives analysts, designers, and users a shared pic-
ture of the information the system must manage, long before anyone writes a line 
of database code. 
Benefits of data modelling include: 
 It clarifies exactly what data the organisation needs to store, reducing the 
risk of missing or duplicated information 
 It provides a stable foundation for physical database design, since tables, 
columns, and keys can be derived directly from the model 
 It improves communication between analysts, designers, and business users, 
since the diagram uses simple, non-technical symbols that anyone can read 
after a short explanation 
 It exposes rules and constraints in the business, such as which relationships 
are mandatory and which are optional, before those rules are buried inside 
program code 
 
  2.4.2 
Elements of an Entity-Relationship Diagram 
 
An ERD is built from four main elements: entities, attributes, identifiers, and rela-
tionships. Each element has a specific symbol, and once you can recognise the four 
symbols, you can read almost any ER diagram. 
Entity 
An entity is a person, place, object, event, or concept about which the organisation 
keeps data. It is a real-world thing that is distinguishable from other things, and it 
always has more than one instance; a single Toyin Ojo is not an entity, but Student 
(the category Toyin Ojo belongs to) is. An entity is drawn as a rectangle and named 
with a singular noun, such as Student rather than Students, since the rectangle rep-
resents the whole category, not any specific member of it. 

===== PAGE 88 =====
 
INS224 | System Analysis and Design 
 
87 
Attribute 
An attribute is a piece of information captured about an entity; it is a property of 
that entity. Attributes of a Student entity might include student_name, student_level, 
and student_programme. Only attributes that the organisation needs should be in-
cluded in the model, since every extra attribute adds a column that must later be 
stored, validated, and maintained. An attribute is drawn as an oval connected to its 
entity. Some attributes are composite, meaning they can be broken down into 
smaller attributes of their own; for example, an Address attribute may be composed 
of Street, City, and State, and the model can show these as smaller ovals branching 
off the Address oval. 
Identifier 
The identifier is the attribute, or combination of attributes, that uniquely identifies 
each instance of an entity. It is also called the primary key. For a Student entity, the 
identifier would typically be student_id, since two students could share the same 
name but never the same ID. Choosing a good identifier matters: it should never be 
blank, and it should never need to change once assigned, which is why systems 
generally prefer an assigned ID number over something like a phone number or 
email address. 
Relationship 
A relationship is an association between two or more entities. It is drawn as a line 
connecting the entities involved, and is usually named with an active verb, such as 
Registers, Teaches, or Supervises, so that the diagram can almost be read like a sen-
tence: Student Registers for Course. A relationship line carries three properties that 
together form the relationship statement: 
 The link phrase, which is the verb describing the relationship 
 The degree or cardinality, which states how many instances of one entity 
relate to instances of the other 
 The participation or ordinality, which states whether the relationship is man-
datory or optional

  2.4.3 
Cardinality of Relationships 
 
Cardinality specifies how many instances of one entity can relate to a single instance 
of another entity. There are three common cardinal relationships, and recognising 
which one applies is usually the trickiest part of building an ERD: 
 One-to-one (1:1): each instance of Entity A relates to exactly one instance of 
Entity B, and vice versa. For example, each employee has one login account, 
and each login account belongs to one employee. 
 One-to-many (1:M): one instance of Entity A relates to many instances of 
Entity B, but each instance of B relates back to only one instance of A. For 
example, one customer can place many orders, but each order belongs to 
one customer only. 
 Many-to-many (M:N): instances of Entity A can relate to many instances of 
Entity B, and instances of B can relate to many instances of A. For example, 
a student can register for many courses, and a course can have many stu-
dents. A many-to-many relationship is normally resolved by introducing an 
associative entity, such as Enrolment or Registration, when the ERD is con-
verted into database tables. 
Ordinality, sometimes shown alongside cardinality, states whether the minimum 
number of instances required is zero (optional) or one (mandatory). For example, a 
Student may be required to have at least one Enrolment (mandatory), while a Course 
might exist in the catalogue even before any student has registered for it (optional). 
Reading an ER Diagram 
An ERD is read one relationship line at a time, in both directions. Consider three 
entities: Course Section, Enrolment, and Student, connected by the relationships Has

  3.1.1 
What is Object Orientation? 
 
Object-oriented analysis and design are the process of modelling a software appli-
cation using object-oriented techniques. Rather than modelling a system as a set of 
processes acting on separate data stores, object orientation models the system as 
a collection of objects that each manage their own data (state) and their own be-
haviour (operations). A running system, in this view, is simply a set of objects send-
ing messages to one another, each responding according to its own class definition. 
Objects and Classes 
An object is a single instance of something in the system, such as a particular stu-
dent or a particular order. Every object has: 
 State: the data held by the object, represented as attributes, such as a spe-
cific student's ID and name 
 Behaviour: the operations the object can perform, represented as methods 
or functions, such as registering for a course 
 Identity: something that distinguishes it from every other object, even if its 
attributes are the same as another object's, in the same way two students 
could coincidentally share a name but never share a student ID 
A class is the blueprint from which objects are created. It defines the attributes and 
operations that all objects of that type will have, but it does not itself hold any stu-
dent’s data; it only describes the shape that every Student object will follow. For 
example, the class Student defines that every student object has a name and a stu-
dent ID, and can register for a course; a specific student such as Toyin Ojo is then 
an object, or instance, of the Student class. Objects interact with one another by 
sending messages, essentially calling one another's operations, which is how a sys-
tem built this way actually gets work done.

Core Object-Oriented Concepts 
 Encapsulation: bundling an object's data and the operations that act on that 
data together and hiding the internal details from the outside. Other parts 
of the system interact with the object only through its defined operations, 
so, for example, other modules can call a Student object's register() opera-
tion without needing to know how that operation is implemented internally. 
 Inheritance: allowing one class to be defined as a specialised version of an-
other. For example, a Postgraduate Student class can inherit the attributes 
and operations of a general Student class, while adding its own specific at-
tributes, such as a supervisor, which avoids repeating the shared attributes 
and operations in both classes. 
 Polymorphism: allowing different classes to respond to the same operation 
call in ways appropriate to each class. For example, a calculateFee() opera-
tion might work differently for an Undergraduate Student object than for a 
Postgraduate Student object, even though both are called the same way, so 
the rest of the system does not need to know which specific type of student 
it is dealing with.

The Object-Oriented Design Process 
 
Object-oriented design typically follows these broad activities: 
1. Identify the candidate classes relevant to the system, usually by examining 
the requirements model built during analysis (including the use cases from 
Module Two, Unit 2)

2. Identify the relationships between classes, such as association, aggregation, 
composition, and inheritance 
3. Identify the attributes and operations that belong to each class 
4. Model the classes and their relationships in a class diagram 
5. Model how objects interact to fulfil each use case, typically using a sequence 
diagram 
6. Check that the classes identified are consistent with the use cases produced

The Unified Modelling Language (UML) 
 
The Unified Modelling Language (UML) is the industry-standard notation for docu-
menting object-oriented analysis and design. UML is not a method in itself; it is a 
common visual language that different object-oriented methods can use to describe 
a system, in much the same way that standard road signs can be understood by 
drivers regardless of which driving school they attended. UML diagrams are broadly 
grouped into two categories: 
 Structural diagrams, which describe the static structure of a system: the clas-
ses, objects, and physical components that make it up, and how they relate 

===== PAGE 99 =====
 
INS224 | System Analysis and Design 
 
98 
to one another. Examples include class diagrams, component diagrams, and 
deployment diagrams. 
 Behavioural diagrams, which describe how the system behaves over time: 
how objects interact, how control flows through the system, and how objects 
change state. Examples include use case diagrams, sequence diagrams, ac-
tivity diagrams, state chart diagrams, and collaboration diagrams.

This unit introduced object orientation as an alternative to the structured, process-
and-data-separated approach used earlier in this course. Objects combine state and 
behaviour, are created from classes, and are connected through encapsulation, in-
heritance, and polymorphism. The object-oriented design process moves from iden-
tifying candidate classes, through modelling their relationships and behaviour, to 
checking consistency with the use cases identified during analysis. UML is the stand-

  3.2.1 
The Class Diagram 
 
A class diagram depicts the object structure of a system. It shows the classes in the 
system, their attributes and operations, and the relationships between them. A class 
is drawn as a rectangle divided into three compartments: the class name at the top, 
its attributes in the middle, and its operations at the bottom, so that everything you 
need to know about a class is visible in one shape. 
Relationships Between Classes 
 Association: a general relationship between two classes, showing that ob-
jects of one class are connected to objects of another. It is drawn as a line, 
often labelled with the multiplicity (how many objects of one class relate to 
how many of the other), such as 1 or M; for example, one Student is associ-
ated with many Enrolment records. 
 Aggregation: a special form of association representing a whole-part rela-
tionship, where the part can exist independently of the whole. For example, 
a Department has Lecturers, but a lecturer can exist without belonging to 
that specific department, since they could transfer to another department 
and continue to exist as a Lecturer object. 
 Composition: a stronger form of aggregation, where the part cannot exist 
without the whole. For example, an Order is made up of Order Lines; if the 
order is deleted, its order lines no longer make sense on their own and are 
deleted along with it. 
 Inheritance (generalisation): a relationship where one class (the subclass) is 
a specialised version of another (the superclass), and inherits its attributes 
and operations. It is drawn as a line with a hollow triangle pointing to the 
superclass, and is the same relationship discussed in Module Three, Unit 1 
in connection with a Postgraduate Student class specialising a general Stu-
dent class. 
These four relationships look similar briefly, a line is a line, but the small symbol at 
one end of the line is what tells them apart. Figure 2.1 places all four side by side so 
the notation can be compared directly.

The Use Case Diagram 
 
Although use case diagrams were introduced in Module Two, Unit 2 as part of re-
quirements modelling, they are also considered a UML behavioural diagram, since 
they depict how external agents (users and other systems) interact with the system. 
A use case diagram shows actors, use cases, and the relationships between them, 
giving a high-level view of the functions the system must support, without describ-
ing how those functions are implemented internally. 
Two relationships between use cases are commonly used beyond the basic associ-
ation between an actor and a use case: 
 Include: a base use case always requires the behaviour of another use case 
to complete. For example, Record Grades includes Save Grades, since every 
act of recording grades must end with saving them. 
 Extend: a use case optionally adds extra behaviour to a base use case under 
certain conditions. For example, Place Order might be extended by Apply 
Discount, but only when a discount code is entered, so the extension only 
happens some of the time. 
The two relationships are easy to confuse because both are drawn as dashed arrows. 
The direction of the arrow, and the everyday meaning of "always" versus "some-
times," is what distinguishes them, as shown in Figure 2.3.

Sequence Diagram 
 
A sequence diagram describes the flow of messages, events, and actions between 
objects over time. It shows how objects interact with one another in a particular 
scenario of a use case and is typically used during analysis and design to document 
and understand the logical order in which messages are exchanged, which is espe-
cially useful for scenarios that involve several systems talking to each other, such as 
a payment being authorised. 
In a sequence diagram, each object involved is shown at the top of a vertical dashed 
line, called a lifeline. Messages are drawn as horizontal arrows between lifelines, 
ordered from top to bottom to show the sequence in which they occur. A solid 
arrow usually represents a call or request, while a dashed arrow represents a return 
or response, so a quick glance at the arrow style tells you whether something is 
being asked for or being handed back. 
Building a Sequence Diagram 
1. Identify the objects involved in the scenario, and place each one at the top 
of its own lifeline 
2. List the messages that must be exchanged, in the order they occur 
3. Draw each message as a horizontal arrow at the appropriate height, using a 
solid arrow for a request and a dashed arrow for a response 
4. Number the messages if the diagram needs to make the sequence explicit, 
or rely on the vertical position alone if the order is already clear. 
Figure 3.1 applies these four steps to an ATM cash withdrawal: the objects are Cus-
tomer, ATM System, and Bank Server; the messages run from inserting a card 
through to debiting the account; and each message is drawn as an arrow at the 
point in time it occurs, reading from top to bottom.

A state chart diagram (or state machine diagram) shows the different states an ob-
ject can be in during its lifetime, and the events that cause it to move from one state 
to another. It is useful for objects whose behaviour depends heavily on their current 
state, such as an Order object, which behaves quite differently once it has been 
Shipped compared to when it was only just Placed. Each state is drawn as a rounded 
rectangle, and transitions between states are drawn as arrows labelled with the 
event that triggers the change, starting from a solid black circle that marks the initial 
state.

A deployment diagram describes the physical deployment architecture of the hard-

Collaboration (Communication) Diagram 
 
A collaboration diagram, also called a communication diagram, shows the same in-
formation as a sequence diagram, objects and the messages exchanged between 
them, but organises it spatially around the objects rather than chronologically down 
the page. Messages are numbered to show their order. Collaboration diagrams are 
useful when the focus is on how objects are structurally connected, while sequence 
diagrams are more useful when the focus is on the exact timing and order of mes-
sages.

Summary 
This unit completed the survey of UML diagrams. The sequence diagram shows the 
chronological order of messages between objects for a specific scenario. The state 
chart diagram shows the states an object passes through during its life. The activity 
diagram shows the flow of control through a process, like a flowchart. The compo-
nent diagram and deployment diagram both describe physical structure, the soft-
ware components and the hardware they run on. The collaboration diagram shows 
the same interactions as a sequence diagram but organised around the objects ra-
ther than around time. Together with the class and use case diagrams from Unit 2, 
these diagrams give a complete object-oriented picture of a system, ready to be 
built using tools such as Microsoft Visio.
