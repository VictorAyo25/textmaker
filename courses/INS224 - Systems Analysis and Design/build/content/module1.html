Module One · Unit 1

Systems Analysis and the Systems Analyst

By the end of this unit you can say what systems analysis and design is, describe what a systems analyst does and the skills the role needs, define an information system, and explain functional decomposition.

Anchor

A restaurant is a system. It has parts doing different jobs, the kitchen, the waiters, the till, and none of them is the restaurant on its own. It has a boundary: the supplier who delivers the fish is outside it. It takes input, orders and ingredients, turns them into output, meals and receipts, and it has feedback, because complaints change the next service. Every one of those words is used in its technical sense in this unit.

TeachWhat the course is about

Systems analysis is the process of understanding a business problem well enough to say exactly what a new system must do about it. Systems design is the process of deciding how the system will be built to do it. Analysis asks what; design asks how. Together they are the front half of building any information system, and they are what this course examines.

Analysis comes first and design follows, because you cannot sensibly decide how to build something until you know what it has to do. Analysis is a discovery process: its two watchwords are discovery and understanding. Design is a decision process: it turns the understanding into a blueprint of program structure, data, screens and hardware that a programmer can build from.

TeachWho does it

The person who does this work is the systems analyst. The single most important idea about the analyst, and one the examiner asks almost every year, is this: a systems analyst is a business problem solver. The analyst is not primarily a programmer. The analyst studies a business, finds where it is losing time or money or accuracy, works out whether technology can help, and if so specifies the system that will help. The solution to the problem is usually, but not always, an information system.

Because the analyst sits between the people with the problem and the people who will build the solution, the analyst is a translator: turning business objectives into functional requirements, user needs into system specifications, and technical jargon into terms an ordinary manager can understand. Everything else the analyst does grows out of that go-between position.

Must memoriseAsked almost every year

A systems analyst is a business problem solver. Roles and responsibilities: study the existing system and business processes to find problems; gather and analyse user requirements; carry out feasibility studies; document requirements and prepare system models and specifications; guide design, testing, implementation and user training; and act as the link between users and the technical team.

Memory hook: the analyst is a bridge. A bridge touches both banks (users and builders) and carries traffic both ways (needs across to the builders, working systems back to the users).

TeachHow an analyst solves a problem

"How does an analyst solve problems?" is a recurring exam question, and it wants a definite sequence, not a vague answer. The analyst first researches and understands the problem, then follows these steps:

  1. Verify that the benefits of solving the problem outweigh the costs.
  2. Define the requirements for solving the problem.
  3. Develop a set of possible solutions (alternatives).
  4. Decide which solution is best, and make a recommendation.
  5. Define the details of the chosen solution.
  6. Implement the solution.
  7. Monitor to make sure the desired results are obtained.

Notice the shape: understand, justify, explore options, choose, specify, build, check. It is the same shape as the life cycle you meet in Unit 2, because the life cycle is this problem-solving sequence scaled up to a whole project.

TeachThe solution: an information system

When the analyst does solve the problem with technology, the solution is usually an information system: a collection of interrelated components (people, data, processes and information technology) that work together to collect, process, store and output the information an organisation needs. Its job is to process input, keep data, produce reports, answer queries and handle transactions.

Any system can be broken into subsystems: smaller systems that are part of a larger one. A customer relationship management (CRM) system, for instance, has a sales management subsystem, an account management subsystem and a product information subsystem. Splitting a system into its components this way is called functional decomposition, and it is the analyst's single most useful habit of mind: it lets you focus on one area of a business, or the interface between two areas, without having to hold the whole thing in your head at once. Every diagram in this course is a form of functional decomposition.

TeachThe three kinds of skill

Because the role is a bridge, the analyst needs three kinds of knowledge and skill. "Describe the skills of a systems analyst" is a standard question; answer it in these three groups and give an example in each.

  • Technical knowledge and skills. Understanding of computers, storage, input and output, networks, programming languages and operating systems, and of the tools and techniques used to build systems, so the analyst can understand and communicate technical requirements even without doing the programming.
  • Business knowledge and skills. Understanding of how organisations work, how they are structured and managed, and what the specific business does and what makes it succeed, so the system is aligned with real organisational goals.
  • People (interpersonal) knowledge and skills. The most important of the three, because the analyst relies on managers, users, programmers and vendors to take a system from idea to delivery. The analyst must communicate well in interviews, reviews and presentations, manage expectations and resolve conflict.
TeachTools versus techniques

The analyst works with both tools and techniques, and the examiner likes you to tell them apart:

  • A tool is a software product used to develop analysis and design specifications or completed components. Examples: a modelling tool such as a computer-aided visual modelling package, a software package such as a database product, and integrated development environments (IDEs) such as Visual Studio.
  • A technique is a strategy for completing a specific development activity. Examples: project planning techniques, cost-benefit analysis, interviewing techniques, requirements modelling, and database design techniques.

The one-line distinction: a tool is a thing you use; a technique is a way of doing something. You will meet a third and fourth term, model and methodology, in Unit 3, and the four-way distinction between model, tool, technique and methodology is itself an exam question.

RecallCover the answers

1. In one sentence, what is a systems analyst?

2. Name the three kinds of skill a systems analyst needs.

3. What is functional decomposition?

Answers

1. A business problem solver who studies a business, finds problems, and specifies the information system that solves them, acting as the bridge between users and the technical team.

2. Technical, business, and people (interpersonal) skills.

3. Breaking a system into smaller subsystems (its components) so each can be studied on its own.

TrapAnalyst is not coder

Asked "what does a systems analyst do?", weak answers say "writes the program" or "codes the system". That is the programmer. The analyst's product is understanding and specification: models, requirements and recommendations. Say "problem solver" and "bridge between users and technical team", and you have the marks; say "programmer" and you have missed the whole point of the course.

Key point

The analyst is not primarily a programmer. The role is to understand a business problem well enough to specify a solution, and then to hold that specification steady between people who know the business and people who can build software. Both technical and non-technical skills are required, and the paper asks for both.

Blunt version: the analyst is a translator who can be held responsible for the translation.

Module One · Unit 2

The System Development Life Cycle

By the end of this unit you can name the phases of the life cycle, state the objective, the main activities and the deliverables of each, and list the principles of a successful systems development project.

TeachThe single most examined topic

The entire process of building, deploying, using and updating an information system is called the systems development life cycle (SDLC). Businesses use information systems to support the work they have to do, and the SDLC is the orderly, phased path a project follows so that the system is built in a disciplined way, on schedule, within budget and to a standard, instead of by trial and error. Without a methodology, projects run over cost, deliver late, or produce a system that does not do what users needed.

"Discuss the major phases of the SDLC and identify the deliverables in each phase" is worth nine or ten marks and has appeared on almost every paper. It is the single most reliable question on the exam. Learn the five phases, their objectives and their deliverables cold.

Must memoriseThe five phases

The SDLC has five phases: Planning, Analysis, Design, Implementation, and Support. The first four are the development project; Support is what happens for years afterwards and is not usually counted as part of the original project.

  1. Planning: identify the need for the system and decide whether it is feasible and worthwhile. Objective: identify the scope, confirm feasibility, and produce a schedule, resource plan and budget.
  2. Analysis: study the current system and business to determine detailed requirements. Objective: understand and document what the system must do.
  3. Design: turn requirements into detailed specifications. Objective: design the solution system (architecture, network, interfaces, database, controls).
  4. Implementation: build, test and deploy the system. Objective: produce a reliable, fully functional system and train the users.
  5. Support: keep the system running productively after deployment. Objective: maintain, enhance, and support the users for years.

Memory hook: P A D I S: "Please Analyse, Design, Implement, Support." Five letters, five phases, in order.

TeachThe activities inside each phase

Each phase is a group of activities. You do not have to recite every one, but knowing the shape lets you write a full answer.

  • Planning: define the problem (business problem and scope), produce the project schedule, confirm project feasibility, staff the project, and launch it.
  • Analysis: gather information; define system requirements; prioritise requirements; build prototypes to discover requirements; generate and evaluate alternatives; review recommendations with management.
  • Design: design the application architecture; design and integrate the network; design the user and system interfaces; design and integrate the database; design the system controls.
  • Implementation: construct software components; verify and test; convert data; train users and document the system; install the system.
  • Support: maintain the system (fix bugs, make minor adjustments); enhance the system (add capability as the business changes); and support the users, which includes running a help desk.
TeachDeliverables, phase by phase

A deliverable is the concrete output a phase hands to the next. This is the part of the question most candidates forget, so it is the part that separates a six-mark answer from a ten-mark one.

WorkedInstallation is part of implementation

Installation, turning off the old system and turning on the new, sits in the Implementation phase, and there are four conversion strategies. They come up again in Module Six, but meet them here:

  1. Direct cutover: the new system immediately replaces the old one. Cheapest and fastest, but riskiest: if the new system fails there is nothing to fall back on.
  2. Parallel: both systems run together for a month or two until it is clear the new one has no bugs. Safest, but the most expensive, because everything is done twice.
  3. Phased: small parts of the new system gradually replace small parts of the old. Lower risk, but drawn out.
  4. Pilot: one small group of users runs the new system before everyone else. Limits the blast radius of a failure to one group.

Now you try: a hospital with a live patient-safety system is replacing it. Which conversion strategy would you recommend, and why? (Think about what "the new system fails" costs a hospital, then re-read the four options.)

Must memorisePrinciples of a successful project

"What are the principles of a successful systems development?" is a Section A question in its own right. The principles are:

  • Get the user involved.
  • Use a problem-solving approach.
  • Establish phases and activities.
  • Establish standards for development and documentation.
  • Justify systems as capital investments (cost-benefit).
  • Do not be afraid to cancel or revise scope.
  • Divide and conquer (break the system into manageable parts).
  • Design systems for growth and change.
  • Apply proper planning and project management.

Memory hook: if you remember nothing else, remember get the user involved and use a problem-solving approach.

RecallCover the answers

1. List the five phases of the SDLC in order.

2. Which phase produces the cost-benefit analysis and the schedule as first deliverables?

3. In which phase does installation happen, and name its four strategies.

Answers

1. Planning, Analysis, Design, Implementation, Support (PADIS).

2. Planning.

3. Implementation; direct cutover, parallel, phased, pilot.

Key point

Every phase of the life cycle produces a named deliverable, and the deliverable is what makes the phase examinable. A phase named without its output is half an answer: planning yields a project plan and a feasibility report, analysis yields a requirements specification, design yields the design specification.

Blunt version: name the phase and its deliverable together, always. One without the other is half a mark.

Module One · Unit 3

Methodologies, Feasibility and Approaches

By the end of this unit you can distinguish the predictive and adaptive approaches, describe the main methodologies and say when to use each, and carry out the feasibility checks a project must pass in the Planning phase.

TeachTwo families of approach

How a project handles requirements, change and uncertainty puts it in one of two families:

  • A predictive (traditional) approach assumes the project can be planned in full in advance and built to that plan, progressing through a fixed sequence of phases each completed before the next begins. It suits systems that are well-understood and unlikely to change.
  • An adaptive approach assumes the project cannot be fully planned in advance and must be modified as it goes. Development runs in short cycles (iterations or sprints), each producing a working piece of the system, with continuous user feedback. It suits changing requirements and high uncertainty.
TeachPredictive versus adaptive, side by side
Predictive approachAdaptive approach
Requirements are defined upfrontRequirements evolve over time
Development is linear and structuredDevelopment is iterative and incremental
Emphasis on detailed documentationStrong user involvement and collaboration
Limited flexibility to accommodate changeWorking systems valued over extensive documentation
TeachThe named methodologies

A methodology is a complete, named way of running a project: it bundles a life-cycle model with the tools, techniques and rules for using them. These are the methodologies the examiner names, with the one thing you must be able to say about each and when to choose it.

  • Waterfall: the classic predictive model. Phases flow strictly downward, each finished before the next starts. Choose it when requirements are stable and well understood. Weakness: change late in the project is very costly.
  • Prototyping: build a quick working model, show it to users, refine it. Choose it when requirements are unclear and need to be discovered. Weakness: the prototype can be mistaken for the finished system, and scope can creep.
  • Rapid application development (RAD): fast delivery through prototyping, reusable components and heavy user involvement. Choose it when the schedule is short and requirements are unclear. Weakness: less suited to large, complex systems.
  • Spiral: an adaptive model that repeats a loop of plan, analyse and design, build a prototype, then test and integrate, adding risk analysis each turn. Choose it for large, high-risk projects.
  • Incremental: deliver the system in working increments, each adding capability. Choose it when you want value in users' hands early.
  • Agile: the modern adaptive family; short iterations, continuous feedback, working software over documentation. Choose it when requirements change often.
  • V-shape: waterfall bent into a V, pairing each development stage with the test that validates it. Choose it when verification and traceability matter.
  • Object-oriented design (OOD): analyses and designs the system as cooperating objects rather than as separate data and processes. It underlies the UML diagrams of Module Five.
WorkedA classic Section A question

A recurring question: "You are a systems analyst on a project with unclear user requirements, a short time schedule and schedule visibility. What methodology do you recommend? Give its advantages and disadvantages."

  1. Match the clues to a family. "Unclear requirements" rules out a predictive model like waterfall, which needs requirements fixed upfront. "Short schedule" points to fast, adaptive delivery.
  2. Name the methodology. Rapid application development (RAD), or prototyping, is the textbook answer: both discover requirements as they go and deliver quickly.
  3. Give advantages. Fast delivery; requirements discovered with the user, so the result fits real needs; strong user involvement raises acceptance.
  4. Give disadvantages. Less discipline and documentation; can struggle on large complex systems; scope creep if the prototype keeps growing.

Now you try: your manager wants to replace waterfall with prototyping on a large and complex project. Do you agree? (Re-read RAD's weakness: what does "large and complex" do to an adaptive method?)

TeachModel, tool, technique, methodology

Now the full four-way distinction, an exam question by itself. Keep the one-line version of each:

  • A model is a representation of an important aspect of the real world (a diagram of the system). It leaves out detail so you can reason about the whole.
  • A tool is a software product used to help do the work (a modelling package, an IDE).
  • A technique is a strategy for completing one activity (interviewing, cost- benefit analysis).
  • A methodology is the complete package: a life-cycle model plus the tools, techniques and rules that say how to run the whole project (waterfall, agile).

Reading order of size: a methodology contains techniques, techniques use tools, and tools produce models.

TeachFeasibility in the Planning phase

Before a project is allowed to proceed, the Planning phase runs a feasibility study to decide whether the proposed system can be built and is worth building. A feasibility study reduces risk: by finding constraints early, management can proceed, modify or abandon before money is wasted. It checks a project from several angles, and each angle carries a matching risk:

  • Technical feasibility: is the technology and skill available? Risk: the team cannot build what is proposed.
  • Economic feasibility: do benefits outweigh costs (cost-benefit analysis)? Risk: the system costs more than it saves.
  • Operational feasibility: will users accept and use it, and does it fit the organisation? Risk: a working system nobody adopts.
  • Schedule feasibility: can it be finished in the time required? Risk: delivered too late to be useful.
  • Legal feasibility: does it comply with laws and regulations? Risk: fines or forced shutdown.
Must memoriseFive feasibilities

The five feasibility types: Technical, Economic, Operational, Schedule, Legal.

Memory hook: TEOSL: or read it as "The Exam Only Suits the Legal", any sentence that fixes the five initials. Note that "confirming project feasibility" is one of the five Planning activities from Unit 2, so feasibility links the two units.

RecallCover the answers

1. Give the one-line difference between the predictive and adaptive approaches.

2. Which methodology suits unclear requirements and a short schedule?

3. Name the five feasibility types.

Answers

1. Predictive plans the whole project upfront and builds to the plan; adaptive plans as it goes, in short iterations with user feedback.

2. RAD (rapid application development), or prototyping.

3. Technical, economic, operational, schedule, legal.

TrapName the methodology, then judge

When a question describes a situation and asks which methodology, weak answers list every methodology they know. The marks are for choosing one that fits the clues in the scenario and justifying it from those clues, then giving its advantages and disadvantages. Read the scenario for the tell-tale words, "unclear requirements", "short schedule", "large and complex", "high risk", "stable requirements", and let them pick the methodology for you.

Key point

Methodologies divide on ONE question: how much is decided up front. Predictive approaches, waterfall above all, fix the requirements first and are right when those requirements are well understood and stable. Adaptive approaches, prototyping, rapid application development, agile, expect the requirements to move and are right when they will.

Blunt version: predictive plans the whole road before setting off. Adaptive drives and looks ahead. Choose by how well you know the destination.
