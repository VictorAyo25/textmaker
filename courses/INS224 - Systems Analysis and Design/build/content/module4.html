This is the most heavily drawn topic on the paper. The text teaches it in Chen notation; the exam asks for the crow's foot, so learn both symbols cold.
Module Four · Unit 1

The Entity-Relationship Model

By the end of this unit you can draw an entity-relationship diagram from a narrative, read cardinality off a diagram, use the crow's-foot and Chen notations, choose an identifier, and interpret a relationship by forward and backward chaining.

TeachTwo notations, one for the exam

The entity-relationship diagram is examined heavily and is taught in the course text, but in the Chen style (relationships as diamonds, attributes as ovals). The exam, by contrast, asks you to draw it in the Information Engineering style (the crow's foot). This unit teaches both and says clearly which the paper wants, so you are never caught out. Read it slowly and draw every figure yourself.

The entity-relationship (E-R) model describes the data a business needs to store as a set of entities and the relationships between them. Drawn out, it is an entity-relationship diagram (ERD). It answers the question "what things does this business keep information about, and how are they connected?" It says nothing about process; that is the data flow diagram's job. This is the single difference the examiner asks for between a data model and a process model.

Must memoriseThe elements of an ERD

An ERD is built from four things:

  • Entity: a thing the business stores data about (Customer, Order, Patient). Drawn as a rectangle with the entity name, singular, in capitals.
  • Attribute: a fact about an entity (a customer's name, an order's date). In crow's-foot notation the attributes are listed inside the entity's rectangle.
  • Identifier (or key): the attribute that uniquely identifies each instance of an entity. The chosen one is the primary key (PK). When an entity holds the key of another to record a relationship, that borrowed key is a foreign key (FK).
  • Relationship: an association between entities, drawn as a line with a verb naming it (a customer places an order).
TeachChoosing an identifier

The paper asks you to give "two options for an identifier for a student entity and justify your choice." An identifier must be unique and unchanging. For a student, the two options are typically the matriculation number and the email address. Justify: the matriculation number is best because it is assigned once, never reused, and never changes; an email can be changed or reassigned, so it is a weaker key. The lesson generalises: prefer a stable, system-assigned identifier over one the user can change.

Must memoriseCardinality: the cardinal relationships

Cardinality is how many instances of one entity relate to how many of another. There are three cardinal relationships, and naming and drawing them is worth marks every year:

  • One-to-one (1:1): each instance of A relates to at most one of B, and vice versa. One employee manages one department, and one department has one manager.
  • One-to-many (1:M): one instance of A relates to many of B, but each B to one A. One customer places many orders, but each order belongs to one customer.
  • Many-to-many (M:N): each A relates to many B and each B to many A. A student takes many courses, and each course has many students.

Memory hook: read the sentence both ways. "One customer places many orders" and "one order is placed by one customer" gives you 1:M. Always test a relationship in both directions; that is what tells you the cardinality.

TeachCardinality versus ordinality

The paper asks you to "differentiate between cardinality and ordinality." Keep them apart:

  • Cardinality is the maximum number on each side: one or many. It is the 1, the M and the N above.
  • Ordinality (also called optionality or the minimum) is whether the relationship is mandatory or optional: must there be at least one, or is zero allowed? "An order must have a customer" is mandatory (minimum one); "a customer may have no orders yet" is optional (minimum zero).

Together they give the full rule: a customer relates to zero or many orders (optional, many), an order relates to exactly one customer (mandatory, one).

Must memoriseCrow's-foot symbols

The Information Engineering style, the crow's-foot notation, puts the cardinality and ordinality at the ends of the relationship line with two marks: an inner mark for the minimum and an outer mark for the maximum. Learn the four combinations.

TeachThe notation styles

Three notations appear in the papers. They say the same things with different symbols, and you should be able to use whichever a question asks for:

  • Information Engineering (crow's foot): entities are rectangles with attributes listed inside; cardinality is the bar-circle-crowsfoot marks above. This is the style the current paper asks for ("using Information Engineering style") and the one to default to.
  • Martin's notation: a close cousin of crow's foot, also using the crow's-foot marks for "many"; the paper sometimes says "the Martin's ERD notation style."
  • Chen notation: the original style. Entities are rectangles, but each relationship is a diamond on the line, and attributes are ovals hung off the entity. Cardinality is written as 1, M or N beside the line rather than as symbols.
WorkedAn ERD from a narrative

Take the patient billing scenario: a patient must be assigned to only one doctor, but a doctor can have many patients; a patient can have only one insurance ID. Draw the ERD.

  1. Find the entities (the nouns kept as records). PATIENT, DOCTOR, INSURANCE. Three rectangles.
  2. Find the relationships (the verbs). A doctor treats patients; a patient has insurance. Draw a line for each with the verb on it.
  3. Work out the cardinality of each, both ways. "One doctor, many patients" and "each patient, one doctor" gives DOCTOR 1:M PATIENT. "A patient has only one insurance" gives PATIENT 1:1 INSURANCE (or M:1 if insurers are shared, but the fraud rule fixes it at one each).
  4. Put the crow's-foot marks on. On the DOCTOR-PATIENT line: one bar at DOCTOR (one), a crow's foot at PATIENT (many). On PATIENT-INSURANCE: a bar at each end (one to one).
  5. Choose the keys. patientId (PK) in PATIENT, doctorId (PK) in DOCTOR; PATIENT holds doctorId and insuranceId as foreign keys (FK) to record its relationships.

Now you try: KHMS schools have one or more annexes; each annex has one or more teachers, but a teacher is assigned to one annex; each teacher coordinates one or more classes, but a class is coordinated by one teacher. Draw the ERD, marking every cardinality.

TeachReading an ERD: forward and backward chaining

The 25/26 paper prints an ERD and asks you to "read and interpret it using forward and backward chaining", and to read the cardinal relationships between the entities. Reading an ERD means turning its symbols back into English sentences, one relationship at a time, in both directions:

  • Forward chaining reads the relationship in the direction of the verb, from the first entity to the second: "a CUSTOMER places zero or many ORDERs."
  • Backward chaining reads it the other way: "an ORDER is placed by one and only one CUSTOMER."

Do this for every line and you have interpreted the whole diagram. The cardinality you report for each relationship is exactly what the crow's-foot marks say: look at the mark nearest each entity, translate it (bar = one, foot = many, circle = optional), and write the sentence.

TrapM:N needs a link, and the verb belongs on the line

Two ERD mistakes cost marks. First, a many-to-many relationship that carries its own data (a STUDENT takes a COURSE with a grade) cannot store that data on either entity: the grade belongs to the pairing. In logical design this becomes a linking (associative) entity between them, turning one M:N into two 1:M. Second, the relationship name is a verb and sits on the line, not inside a box; leaving a line unlabelled, or writing a noun on it, loses the reading marks.

RecallCover the answers

1. Give the three cardinal relationships and an example of each.

2. What is the difference between cardinality and ordinality?

3. In crow's foot, what do a bar, a circle and a crow's foot each mean?

Answers

1. One-to-one (employee manages department), one-to-many (customer places orders), many-to-many (student takes courses).

2. Cardinality is the maximum (one or many); ordinality is the minimum, whether the relationship is mandatory (at least one) or optional (zero allowed).

3. Bar = one, circle = zero (optional), crow's foot = many.

Key point

The marks at the end of a relationship line describe the entity at the OTHER end, and each end carries two of them: the inner mark gives the maximum and the outer mark gives the minimum. A crow's foot is many, a bar is one, a circle is zero. Read every line out loud in both directions before you commit to it.

Blunt version: read the far end, and say the sentence both ways round. Most lost ERD marks are a correct diagram read backwards.
