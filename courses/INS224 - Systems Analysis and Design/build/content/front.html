How to use this manual

Read this page once. It is short, and it decides whether the rest of the manual works for you.

TeachThe bargain

This manual assumes you have never analysed a system, never drawn a use case or an entity-relationship diagram, and never heard of a class diagram. It carries you from there to a full mark in the examination. It is also meant to be enough on its own: you should never need a lecturer, a textbook or a website to follow it. Anything it uses, it teaches first, including every symbol of its own notation.

In exchange it asks one thing: draw the diagrams yourself. Reading a finished use case diagram or a solved data flow diagram creates a feeling of understanding that evaporates in the hall, where you face a blank page and a paragraph describing a business. Every diagram in this book was drawn by hand first from the words of a real question. Do the same, and the gap between your diagram and the one printed here is exactly the information you were missing.

Reading a figure

Must memoriseThe notation is the exam

More than half of this course is drawn, not written. An entity is a rectangle, a relationship a line with a verb on it, and the small marks at the ends of that line, the crow's foot, a circle, a single bar, carry the whole meaning of a business rule. A hollow diamond and a filled diamond look almost the same and mean opposite things. Get a symbol wrong and the answer is wrong, however good the prose around it. Every symbol used in this manual is defined the first time it appears, and the full legend sits in the Reference sheet. Where a figure is a diagram you must be able to draw, redraw it yourself until it comes out without looking.

One promise, and one label

Teach

The promise. Every diagram, every solved question, and every past question quoted in this manual was worked out and transcribed independently, not copied from a model answer. The course's own materials and the papers themselves contain slips (you will meet a question that says "answer parts i to vii" and then prints eight, and a paper that misspells a word inside its own scenario), so nothing here is trusted just because a source printed it. Where a paper is wrong, this manual quotes it exactly and flags the error underneath, because in the hall you must answer the paper as printed.

The label. The course text covers most of what the examiner asks: the life cycle, requirements, use cases, data flow diagrams, entity-relationship diagrams and the UML diagrams are all in it. A few topics are examined but are not in the text, chiefly system architecture and delivery: the client-server tiers, hardware architecture, system acquisition, interface design and relational database design in Module Six. Where a topic is not in the course text, it carries a Not in the course text label. It is still taught and solved in full, because it is worth real marks, and the label simply tells you that you will not find it if you go back to the text alone. This manual is where you find it. One more thing to know: the text draws entity-relationship diagrams in the Chen style (diamonds and ovals), while the exam asks for the Information Engineering style (the crow's foot). This manual teaches both and tells you which the paper wants.

Before you start

The Exam at a Glance

What the paper looks like, how long you have, and which topics earn the marks. Learn this before you learn anything else: it tells you where to aim.

TeachThe shape of the paper

The current paper runs for three hours and is worth 70 marks. It is split into two sections, and the instruction at the top is the first thing you read:

  • Section A has two questions and you answer one of them. These are the broad, written questions: the life cycle and its phases, choosing a methodology, requirements and how you would gather them, feasibility, project planning. Few or no diagrams. This is where a candidate who has memorised the core lists scores fastest.
  • Section B has four questions and you answer any three. These are the modelling questions, each built on a short business scenario: draw an entity-relationship diagram, a set of data flow diagrams, a use case diagram, a class diagram, an activity or sequence diagram, and explain the object-oriented concepts. Three of the four questions almost always ask for diagrams.

So you answer four questions in all (one from A, three from B) in three hours: about forty minutes each. That is generous, but only if you can draw the diagrams without hesitating, which is what the practice in this book buys you.

Must memoriseThe topic map

Across every past paper, the marks come from the same bank of topics. Learn this list; it is the whole course, and each item is a module in this manual.

  • The analyst and the life cycle (Section A): what an analyst does, the five phases of the life cycle and their deliverables, the principles of a successful project.
  • Methodologies and feasibility (Section A): predictive versus adaptive, waterfall, prototyping, rapid application development, the spiral, agile; when to use each; the feasibility checks.
  • Requirements (Section A and B): elicitation techniques with their stakeholders and trade-offs; functional versus non-functional requirements.
  • Use case modelling (Section B): actors, the four relationships, the diagram, the narrative.
  • Data flow diagrams (Section B): the four elements, the context diagram, Process 0 and Level 1, and the mechanical errors.
  • Entity-relationship diagrams (Section B): entities, keys, cardinality, the notation styles, and reading an existing diagram.
  • Class diagrams and object-oriented design (Section B): classes with attributes and methods, relationships and multiplicities, and the concepts (encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism, composition, aggregation).
  • Activity and sequence diagrams (Section B): modelling a process flow and an interaction over time.
  • Design, architecture and delivery (Section A and B): system architecture, acquisition strategy, installation, and support.
TrapRead the rubric

The number of questions and the split between sections has changed between sittings. Older papers under the previous code said "answer any three of five" in two hours with no sections at all; one year used "answer all of Section A and any two of Section B". The current paper is one from Section A and three from Section B in three hours. Never assume the rubric from a past paper you practised on. The first minute in the hall is spent reading the instruction at the top of the paper and counting how many questions from where. Answering the wrong number of questions, or all four from one section, throws away marks you had already earned.

TeachHow to spend forty minutes

A Section B question gives you a paragraph of business description and asks for several diagrams from it. The winning routine is always the same, and this manual drills it on every worked question:

  • Read for nouns and verbs first. The nouns that matter become entities, classes or data stores; the verbs become relationships, processes or use cases. Underline them on the paper before you draw anything.
  • Draw in pencil-order: boxes first, then the lines between them, then the symbols on the lines, then the labels. A diagram built in that order rarely has to be rubbed out.
  • Answer the verb in the instruction. "Draw", "identify", "differentiate" and "explain" want different things. Drawing when it says explain, or explaining when it says draw, loses the marks even when what you wrote is correct.

Every worked question in this book opens with a Break it down box that does exactly this reading step, so that by the exam it is automatic.
