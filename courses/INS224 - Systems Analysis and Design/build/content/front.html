How to use this manual

Read this page once. It is short, and it decides whether the rest of the manual works for you.

TeachThe bargain

This manual assumes you have never analysed a system, never drawn a use case or an entity-relationship diagram, and never heard of a class diagram. It carries you from there to a full mark in the examination. It is also meant to be enough on its own: you should never need a lecturer, a textbook or a website to follow it. Anything it uses, it teaches first, including every symbol of its own notation.

In exchange it asks one thing: draw the diagrams yourself. Reading a finished use case diagram or a solved data flow diagram creates a feeling of understanding that evaporates in the hall, where you face a blank page and a paragraph describing a business. Every diagram in this book was drawn by hand first from the words of a real question. Do the same, and the gap between your diagram and the one printed here is exactly the information you were missing.

Trap

A mistake that is easy to make and costly in marks. Most are taken from the exact places the examiner sets a snare: a use case joined to another with the wrong arrow, a data flow drawn straight from an entity to a data store, a many-to-many relationship left with an attribute stranded on the line, an aggregation drawn where the question describes a composition.

Recall

Questions with the answers printed underneath, below a dashed line. Cover the answers. Say your answer out loud, or write it, and only then look. Checking whether you knew something is not the same as knowing it, and the difference is the whole point of this box.

Exam question

A question reproduced as it appeared on the paper, in a dark As printed bar, plain and uncoloured, so you meet it the way the examiner set it. A violet Break it down box then reads it in your shoes, and a teal Answer box works it in full.

Reading a figure

Must memoriseThe notation is the exam

More than half of this course is drawn, not written. An entity is a rectangle, a relationship a line with a verb on it, and the small marks at the ends of that line, the crow's foot, a circle, a single bar, carry the whole meaning of a business rule. A hollow diamond and a filled diamond look almost the same and mean opposite things. Get a symbol wrong and the answer is wrong, however good the prose around it. Every symbol used in this manual is defined the first time it appears, and the full legend sits in the Reference sheet. Where a figure is a diagram you must be able to draw, redraw it yourself until it comes out without looking.

One promise, and one label

Teach

The promise. Every diagram, every solved question, and every past question quoted in this manual was worked out and transcribed independently, not copied from a model answer. The course's own materials and the papers themselves contain slips (you will meet a question that says "answer parts i to vii" and then prints eight, and a paper that misspells a word inside its own scenario), so nothing here is trusted just because a source printed it. Where a paper is wrong, this manual quotes it exactly and flags the error underneath, because in the hall you must answer the paper as printed.

The label. The course text covers most of what the examiner asks: the life cycle, requirements, use cases, data flow diagrams, entity-relationship diagrams and the UML diagrams are all in it. A few topics are examined but are not in the text, chiefly system architecture and delivery: the client-server tiers, hardware architecture, system acquisition, interface design and relational database design in Module Six. Where a topic is not in the course text, it carries a Not in the course text label. It is still taught and solved in full, because it is worth real marks, and the label simply tells you that you will not find it if you go back to the text alone. This manual is where you find it. One more thing to know: the text draws entity-relationship diagrams in the Chen style (diamonds and ovals), while the exam asks for the Information Engineering style (the crow's foot). This manual teaches both and tells you which the paper wants.

The Exam at a Glance

What the paper looks like, how long you have, and which topics earn the marks. Learn this before you learn anything else: it tells you where to aim.

TrapThe shape has changed. Read this box twice.

Everything printed on the past papers in this book describes a paper with Section A and Section B, where you answered one of two and then any three of four. That is genuinely what the 2025/2026 and 2024/2025 papers printed, and both are reproduced here exactly as they were set.

The paper you are about to sit is not that paper. The lecturer confirmed the shape on 9 August 2026, and it differs in the two ways that matter most:

  • There is now an objective section as well as the written questions, on the same paper, inside the same three hours.
  • The written half is three questions with NO CHOICE: one from Module One, one from Module Two, one from Module Three.

No choice is a harder rubric than one of two plus three of four, not an easier one. Under the old shape a weak topic could be traded away. Under this one it cannot.

TeachThe shape of the paper you will sit

Three hours, one paper, two halves.

  • The objective section. Its format is the one used in the second class test, which is the multi-select form: questions asking which TWO of the options are correct, alongside single-answer questions and matching questions. Both class tests are reproduced and answered in the Objective Section of this book, all sixty questions, every option explained.
  • Question One, compulsory, from the text's Module One. The systems analyst and what the job actually involves, the system development life cycle and its phases with their deliverables, methodologies (predictive against adaptive, waterfall, prototyping, rapid application development, spiral, agile) and feasibility.
  • Question Two, compulsory, from the text's Module Two. Fact-finding techniques and their trade-offs, functional against non-functional requirements, use case modelling, data flow diagrams, and entity-relationship diagrams. This is the widest of the three, and it is where most of the drawing lives.
  • Question Three, compulsory, from the text's Module Three. Object orientation, classes and objects, UML class diagrams and their relationships, and the behavioural diagrams, that is activity and sequence.

What this means for revision. Modules One to Five of this book must all be ready to WRITE and to DRAW, not merely to recognise. The one part that sits outside the three compulsory questions is Module Six, system design and architecture, acquisition, installation, support and project management, which the course text does not carry either. Revise Module Six for the objective section, and give the drawing time to Modules Three, Four and Five.

Key point

A rubric offering a choice lets a candidate trade a weak topic for a strong one. A rubric with no choice removes that trade, so a mark for a topic you cannot do is simply lost, and the cheapest marks available are the ones gained by making your worst compulsory topic merely adequate.

Blunt version: no choice means no hiding. Spend the revision on your worst diagram, not your best.

TeachHow to spend the three hours

Two halves, and the objective section is the one that quietly eats the clock.

  • Five minutes: read the whole paper. Count the objective questions and check the rubric with your own eyes. The shape described here is what the lecturer confirmed; the instruction printed at the top of your paper is what governs.
  • The objective section first, at pace. About 45 seconds a question. A multi-select question needs BOTH of its choices to score, so read every option before deciding and never stop at the first one that looks right.
  • Then the three written questions, about forty minutes each. Take the one you are most confident about first. An early complete answer is worth real marks in confidence alone.
  • Read for nouns and verbs before you draw. The nouns that matter become entities, classes, processes or data stores; the verbs become relationships, flows or use cases. Underline them on the question paper before a single box is drawn.
  • Answer the verb in the instruction. Draw, identify, differentiate and explain want different things. Drawing where it says explain loses the marks even when what you drew is correct.
  • Leave ten minutes for the objective questions you flagged and for any part left blank. Something written always beats nothing.

Every worked question in this book opens with a Break it down box that does the reading step for you, so that by the exam it is automatic.
