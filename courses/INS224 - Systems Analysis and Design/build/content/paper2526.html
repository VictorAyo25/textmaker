AnswerPQ 25/26 Q1

a. Entity-relationship diagram (4.5 marks)

The nouns kept as records are LANDLORD, HOUSE, AGENT, SUPERVISOR (the tenant) and PAYMENT. The relationships from the story: a landlord owns many houses; an agent manages many houses; an agent serves a supervisor; a supervisor occupies a house; a supervisor makes payments; an agent forwards payments to the landlord.

Answer, continued

b. Data flow diagrams (9 marks)

Context diagram: one process "0 Rental system" in the middle, with SUPERVISOR and LANDLORD as external entities. Flows: supervisor sends "house request" and "rent payment" in; the system sends "house availability" back to the supervisor and "forwarded payment" to the landlord. No data stores at this level (draw it exactly as Figure 3.2 in Module Three, with these labels).

Level 0: explode process 0 into the major processes: 1.0 Match tenant to house, 2.0 Agree and clean, 3.0 Collect and forward rent. Add data stores D1 Houses and D2 Payments. Wire the flows so each process has an input and an output, and so the "house request" in and "forwarded payment" out still match the context diagram (balance).

Level 1 of process 3.0 (rent payment): explode 3.0 into 3.1 Receive payment from supervisor, 3.2 Record payment (to D2 Payments), 3.3 Forward payment to landlord. The total flows in and out (payment in, forwarded payment out) balance process 3.0.

Answer, continued

c. Three more reasons to succeed and three to fail (3 marks)

Succeed (besides customer acceptance): clear and agreed requirements; strong user involvement throughout; a realistic schedule and budget with good project management.

Fail (besides lack of upper-management commitment): unclear or constantly changing requirements; little or no user involvement; poor planning, estimation or testing.

d. Reading the fragment (1 mark)

The bar at the Customer end means "one" and the circle-with-crow's-foot at the Order end means "zero or many". So, forward chaining: one CUSTOMER places zero or many ORDERs. Backward chaining: each ORDER is placed by one and only one CUSTOMER.

AnswerPQ 25/26 Q2

a. Class diagram (5 marks)

Four classes: PATIENT, NURSE, DOCTOR, INSURANCE. Give each attributes and a method. The rules give the multiplicities: a DOCTOR treats many PATIENTs but each PATIENT has one DOCTOR (1 to *); a PATIENT has one INSURANCE and an insurance covers one patient (1 to 1); a NURSE attends many PATIENTs (1 to *).

b. Use case diagram (5 marks)

Actors: PATIENT, NURSE, DOCTOR, INSURANCE CARRIER. Use cases inside the boundary: Make enquiry, Check vitals, Refer to doctor, Confirm insurance, Advise on drugs. "Attend patient" includes "Check insurance" (it must always happen, since he will not be attended without insurance). Draw it as Figure 2.1 in Module Two with these labels.

c. Activity diagram (5 marks)

Start, then action "Patient makes enquiry", then a decision "has insurance?": the "no" branch ends (not attended); the "yes" branch flows to "Nurse checks vitals", "Refer to doctor", "Doctor confirms insurance with carrier", "Advise on drugs", then end. Draw it as Figure 5.3 with this flow.

d. Drawbacks of multiple inheritance (2.5 marks)

The diamond problem (ambiguity when two parents give the same method); increased complexity of the class hierarchy; and name clashes between members inherited from different parents.

AnswerPQ 25/26 Q3

a. Three feasibility questions (3 marks)

Feasibility analysis asks whether the project is worth pursuing, from three angles: Is it technically feasible? (do we have the technology and skill to build it?); Is it economically feasible? (do the benefits outweigh the costs?); and Is it operationally feasible? (will the users accept and use it, and does it fit the business?). Schedule and legal feasibility complete the five, but these three are the core questions.

b. Six more problem-solving steps (3 marks)

After researching and understanding the problem: verify the benefits outweigh the costs; define the requirements; develop alternative solutions; recommend the best solution; define the details of the chosen solution; implement it (and then monitor the results).

c. Explaining what a model is (4 marks)

Tell the stakeholders a model is a simplified picture of the system that leaves out detail so everyone can see and agree the important parts before it is built, the way an architect's drawing lets a client approve a building before a brick is laid. The use case diagram, DFD and class diagram they will be shown are all such models.

d. Another skill (3 marks)

Besides people skills, Billy needs technical knowledge and skills: an understanding of computers, networks, databases and the tools and techniques of system building, so he can understand and communicate technical requirements even though the programmer writes the code. (Business knowledge is an equally acceptable answer.)

e. Three support activities (3 marks)

Maintain the system (fix bugs and make minor adjustments); enhance the system (add capability as the business grows); and support the users, which includes running a help desk.

f. Three other job roles (1.5 marks)

Project manager, software architect, and product designer (also acceptable: software tester, business analyst).

Break it down

Given A hospital replacing fragmented systems, safety critical, twelve-month deadline, stakeholders who disagree.

Asked Compare seven methodologies, list five planning activities, and formulate requirements at every level. Modules One, Two and Six.

Watch Part a wants a comparison with a reason each is suitable or not for this safety-critical project, not just a list. Part c wants the exact counts across five levels.

AnswerPQ 25/26 Q5

a. Compare seven methodologies (7 marks)

  • Waterfall: strong documentation and traceability suit a safety-critical system, but its rigidity is risky if requirements shift.
  • Prototyping: good for the unclear priorities here, but the prototype can be mistaken for the finished safety system.
  • RAD: fast, which suits the twelve-month deadline, but weak for a large, complex, safety-critical hospital system.
  • Spiral: its risk analysis each cycle fits a safety-critical project well, but it is heavy and slow.
  • Incremental: delivers working pieces early, useful given the deadline, but integration across departments must be managed.
  • Agile: handles the disagreeing stakeholders through constant feedback, but its light documentation clashes with the audit-trail requirement.
  • V-shape: pairs each stage with its test, excellent for the reliability and traceability a hospital demands, but as rigid as waterfall.
Answer, continued

b. Five project planning activities (5 marks)

Define the problem; produce the project schedule; confirm feasibility; staff the project; and launch the project.

c. Formulate requirements (5.5 marks)

  • Business: cut medication-administration delays; achieve regulatory compliance with full audit trails.
  • User: a nurse can administer and record medication at the bedside; a doctor can see a complete patient record in one place.
  • Functional: the system shall record each patient appointment; the system shall flag duplicated laboratory test requests; the system shall log every change to a record for audit.
  • Non-functional: the system shall be available 99.9% of the time; the system shall encrypt all patient data (privacy).
  • System: the system shall store records as time-stamped entries linked to a patient ID; the system shall integrate with the existing departmental applications.

AnswerPQ 25/26 Q6

a. As-is, to-be and assumptions (3.5 marks)

As-is weaknesses: no single platform, so learning is unsupported; no automated feedback or plagiarism checking; no analytics for administrators. To-be improvements: one platform teaching three languages with automated feedback, debugging support, plagiarism alerts and analytics. Assumptions to validate: that 8,000 students across three campuses have reliable network access; that the platform can integrate with the existing learning management system; that the AI feedback is accurate enough to be trusted.

b. Elicitation plan (4 marks)

  • Interview lecturers: information on the learning outcomes and marking rules; suitable because they hold the academic requirements and can be probed in depth.
  • Questionnaire to students: information on what help they want; suitable because it cheaply reaches 8,000 scattered students.
  • Observation of a lab session: information on how students really code and get stuck; suitable because it shows real behaviour.
  • Document review of the learning management system: information on existing data and integration points; suitable because it grounds the design in what exists.

c. Short notes on UML (2 marks)

UML is a standard set of diagram notations for modelling object-oriented systems, created by Booch, Rumbaugh and Jacobson. Its uses: to visualise, specify, construct and document a software system in one shared language.

d. Two categories of UML diagram (4 marks)

Structural diagrams model what the system is: the class diagram (classes and relationships) and the object diagram (objects at a moment). Behavioural diagrams model what it does: the use case diagram (what users can do) and the activity diagram (a process flow); the sequence diagram is another.

e. Four class relationships (4 marks)

Inheritance (hollow triangle, is-a): a Postgraduate is a Student. Composition (filled diamond, life-or-death has-a): an Order is made of Order Lines. Aggregation (hollow diamond, loose has-a): a Department has Lecturers who survive it. Association (plain line): a Doctor treats a Patient. Draw the four side by side as Figure 5.2 in Module Five.
