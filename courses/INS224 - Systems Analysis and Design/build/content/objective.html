How to Attack an Objective Question

The paper carries an objective section as well as its three written questions, and it is answered under more time pressure than anything else on it. This part is where you practise that half.

Teachwhat is here, and where it came from

Both class tests are reproduced below, thirty questions each, sixty in all, grouped by the part of this book that teaches them rather than by which test they came from, so that a run of questions drills one topic at a time. Every option is explained, the right one and the wrong ones alike.

The stems and options were read off the test screenshots by eye. That is a weaker guarantee than the two written papers in this book carry, since those are held character for character against a transcript by a gate, and it is said here rather than left for you to assume.

Trapthe form that costs marks all by itself

The lecturer named test two's format as the one the examination will use, and test two is multi-select: a question that asks which TWO of the options are correct. Test one was single-answer. That difference is worth real marks on its own, and it is the reason this part exists in the form it does.

On a which-two question, both choices must be right for the mark. A candidate who knows the topic perfectly and stops at the first correct option scores nothing. So the habit to build is: read all four options every time, decide about each one separately, and only then count how many you have marked. If you have marked one, or three, you have not finished the question.

The other habit is that the two correct options usually do different jobs. A typical pair is one option that NAMES the thing and one that states its PURPOSE, as in the balancing question below: one says the practice is called balancing, the other says why it is done. Looking for that split makes the pair easier to find than hunting for two statements that both feel true.

Key point

In a four-option question, the options that are wrong are wrong for a reason you can state. Finding those reasons is faster and safer than recognising the right answer, because recognition is exactly what a well-built distractor attacks.

Blunt version: do not hunt for the right one. Kill the wrong ones, and on a which-two, count what is left.

Teachthe four moves, in order
  1. Read the stem twice and underline the instruction. NOT, BEST, TWO, PRIMARILY and EXCEPT each flip or narrow the question. A stem asking which two is a different question from one asking which one, and the paper mixes both.
  2. Answer it before you read the options where you can. If you know a black hole is a process with inputs and no outputs, decide that first, then read the options as a lookup rather than as a suggestion.
  3. Strike the impossible. On these tests two of the four usually die at once: an option that names a different concept, or one that is true of the subject but does not answer the question asked.
  4. On the last two, split on the exact wording. It is nearly always one word: only, all, every, eliminates, requires. An option saying a context diagram "eliminates the need for" something is almost always the distractor.
How to use this part

Sit one group at a time, timed at 45 seconds a question, with the answers covered. Then read the whole answer block for that group, including the questions you got right: on an objective paper a right answer for the wrong reason is a mark you will not get twice.

Answers, and why every option is what it is

1. The answer is The standard process followed by an organisation to conduct all the steps necessary to analyse, design, implement, and maintain information systems. A methodology is a PROCESS, not a product and not a toolset. It is the organisation's standard way of getting from a business need to a working, maintained system, and it names the steps, their order, and what each must produce. Waterfall, RAD, Agile and the spiral are all methodologies because each answers the same question differently: in what order do we analyse, design, implement and maintain.

  • A regulatory compliance framework mandating the documentation standards for government information systems. Wrong. Compliance frameworks constrain how you document; they do not tell you how to develop.
  • The set of hardware and software tools used by developers to construct and test information systems. Wrong, and it is the commonest confusion: those are CASE tools. Tools support a methodology, they are not one.
  • The standard process followed by an organisation to conduct all the steps necessary to analyse, design, implement, and maintain information systems. Correct. Standard process, covering analyse, design, implement and maintain.
  • A specific programming language or development framework chosen by an organisation for building software. Wrong. The language is an implementation choice made INSIDE a methodology.

2. The answer is Helps ensure that requirements are accurate, reduces resistance to change, and increases user acceptance of the final system. Three benefits, and the question wants all three: accuracy, less resistance, more acceptance. Users are the only people who know what the work actually involves, so keeping them in the room is how requirements stay true. The second and third benefits are political rather than technical: a system people helped shape is a system people will use.

  • Is only necessary for consumer-facing systems and not required for internal back-office applications. Wrong. Internal systems fail for exactly the same reason, and their users are easier to reach.
  • Should be limited to the initial requirements phase to prevent users from disrupting the technical development work. Wrong, and it is the mistake the principle exists to prevent. Requirements change, and users are the ones who notice.
  • Helps ensure that requirements are accurate, reduces resistance to change, and increases user acceptance of the final system. Correct. Accuracy, reduced resistance, and acceptance.
  • Increases development costs significantly but is mandatory for regulatory compliance in most industries. Wrong on both halves: involvement REDUCES the cost of rework, and it is good practice rather than a regulation.

3. The answer is A traditional, linear approach in which each phase must be completed before the next phase begins, with key deliverables presented for approval at each phase end. Linear, sequential, and gated. Water falls downhill and does not flow back up, which is the whole image: finish a phase, get it approved, and only then start the next. Its strength is control and documentation; its weakness is that a requirement discovered late is expensive, because going back means going back a long way.

  • A traditional, linear approach in which each phase must be completed before the next phase begins, with key deliverables presented for approval at each phase end. Correct. Linear, each phase complete before the next, deliverables approved at each gate.
  • A flexible framework that allows requirements to evolve and development activities to be revisited throughout the project. Wrong. That flexibility describes the ADAPTIVE approaches, which exist because Waterfall lacks it.
  • An iterative development cycle where system components are built, tested, and refined through multiple rapid iterations. Wrong. That is iterative or incremental development.
  • A collaborative approach that integrates users into every phase of development through continuous prototyping and feedback. Wrong. Continuous prototyping and user integration describe RAD and JAD.

4. The answer is Over budgeting, late delivery, or delivery of a system that does not fully solve the problems it was intended to address. Bad requirements are the most expensive defect in the whole life cycle, because everything downstream is built on them. The error is not discovered until testing or delivery, by which time the design, the code and the data structures have all committed to it. That is why fact-finding gets its own phase rather than being folded into design.

  • Over budgeting, late delivery, or delivery of a system that does not fully solve the problems it was intended to address. Correct. Over budget, late, and still not solving the problem.
  • Improved system performance but with reduced functionality compared to what users originally requested. Wrong. Misunderstanding requirements does not improve performance; it wastes effort on the wrong thing.
  • Increased user satisfaction because a simpler system is easier to learn and operate. Wrong, and it confuses a system that is SMALLER by accident with one that is SIMPLER by design.
  • Faster delivery due to reduced scope, though with some features deferred to a future release. Wrong. Deferring scope on purpose is a plan. Misunderstanding it is not, and it costs time rather than saving it.

JAD and prototyping are partners, not rivals. JAD puts users, analysts and managers in one room to agree requirements quickly; a prototype gives that room something concrete to react to, because people criticise a screen far better than they criticise a paragraph. Put them together and you confirm requirements AND obtain design approval in the same session.

  • JAD is used exclusively during the implementation phase to coordinate the joint efforts of developers and testers. Wrong phase. JAD belongs to requirements and design, not implementation.
  • When JRP/JAD incorporates prototyping as a means for confirming requirements and obtaining design approvals, the benefits of prototyping are realised within a structured group collaboration framework.   • Information systems require significant financial resources and should be evaluated like other capital investments using cost-benefit analysis to determine whether expected benefits justify the costs and risks involved. Correct. Evaluate like any capital investment, using cost-benefit analysis against costs and risks.

3. The answer is Because they capture what the organisation does and how it processes data without specifying implementation details such as programming languages or hardware.   • Because they can only be drawn using computer-aided software engineering tools. Wrong. The tool used to draw it has nothing to do with whether the model is logical.

1. The two answers are Student is an entity and matriculation number is one of its attributes and The matriculation number is a candidate identifier, because it is unique to one student. An identifier is the attribute that picks out exactly one instance, which is why uniqueness, not universality, is the test.

  • Student is an entity and matriculation number is one of its attributes. Correct. Student is the thing; matriculation number, name and date of birth are facts about it.

  • The matriculation number is a candidate identifier, because it is unique to one student. Correct. It is unique to one student, which is exactly what a candidate identifier means.
  • Date of birth is the natural primary key, because everyone has one. Wrong. Everyone having one is not enough: many students share a date of birth, so it cannot identify a single instance.

2. The two answers are The relationship between Member and Book is many to many and A many-to-many relationship should be resolved into an associative entity such as Loan. It is resolved by an associative entity, which is where the attributes of the relationship itself, such as the date borrowed, finally have somewhere to live.

  • The relationship between Member and Book is many to many. Correct. Many in each direction is the definition of many to many.
  • The relationship between Member and Book is one to many. Wrong. One to many would mean a book could only ever be borrowed by one member, which the description contradicts.
  • A many-to-many relationship should be resolved into an associative entity such as Loan. Correct. Loan holds the two foreign keys plus the borrow date and due date, which have no home on either original entity.
  • A many-to-many relationship is drawn with a single bar at each end. Wrong. A single bar means exactly one. Many is the crow's foot.

  • One or many employees. Wrong. One or many would be a crow's foot with a BAR, not a circle. The circle is what makes it optional.
  • Zero or one employee. Wrong. Zero or one is a circle with a bar. There is no crow's foot in it.

  • In Chen notation a relationship is drawn as a diamond on the line. Correct. The diamond holds the relationship name, with the two entities either side of it.
  • In Chen notation attributes are drawn as ovals attached to the entity. Correct. Each attribute gets its own oval joined to the entity rectangle, which is why a Chen diagram of a real system covers a wall.

  • A primary key uniquely identifies one instance of an entity. Correct. Unique identification of a single instance is the whole job of a primary key.
  • A foreign key is an attribute that holds the primary key of a related entity. Correct. The foreign key is the copy of the other entity's identifier, and it is where the relationship physically lives.
  • A foreign key must be unique within its own table. Wrong, and it is the common confusion. A foreign key repeats freely: many employees carry the same department number, which is what many to one MEANS.
  • An entity may have several primary keys at once. Wrong. There may be several CANDIDATE keys, but exactly one of them is chosen as the primary key.

6. The two answers are Each customer has at least one order and A customer may exist with no orders. Read each end for the entity at the OTHER end. This is the single most common reading error on the paper, and the cure is to say the sentence out loud in both directions before writing anything.

  • Each order belongs to exactly one customer. Wrong as a reading of the marks given. The Customer end carries a bar and a circle, which is zero or one, not exactly one.
  • Each customer has at least one order. Correct. The Order end has a foot and a bar, which is one or many, so at least one order per customer.
  • A customer may exist with no orders. Correct as the other half of the same pair: the circle at the Customer end makes the customer optional to an order.
  • An order may exist with no customer. Wrong. Optionality on the Customer end says a customer need not be attached, not that an order floats free of the relationship entirely.

Answers, and why every option is what it is

1. The answer is The arrow is drawn from the extension use case to the use case being extended, labelled with the extends stereotype. EXTENDS points from the extension BACK to the base, which feels backwards until you read it as a sentence: "Generate Warehouse Packing Order EXTENDS Place New Member Order". The arrow follows the sentence. Contrast INCLUDES, which points from the base to the thing it always uses: "Place New Member Order INCLUDES Check Credit". Get the two directions straight and this whole family of questions falls.

  • The arrow is drawn from the extension use case to the use case being extended, labelled with the extends stereotype. Correct. From the extension to the base, with the extends stereotype.
  • The arrow is drawn from the base use case to the extension use case, labelled with the extends stereotype. Wrong direction. That is how INCLUDES is drawn.
  • The arrow is drawn from the extension use case to the actor, labelled with the include stereotype. Wrong twice: the arrow does not point at an actor, and the stereotype is extends, not include.
  • No arrow is used; extends relationships are shown only as dashed rectangles. Wrong. It is a dashed arrow with a stereotype label, not a rectangle.

2. The two answers are A class is a blueprint describing structure and behaviour and An object is an instance of a class, with actual values for its attributes. The class is the definition and the object is one filled-in copy of it. The Contacts app is the standard image: one idea of what a contact is, and hundreds of saved contacts that are instances of it.

  • A class is a blueprint describing structure and behaviour. Correct. It defines what every instance will have and be able to do.
  • An object is an instance of a class, with actual values for its attributes. Correct. Instantiating the class produces an object with real values in its attributes.
  • A class holds the values and an object holds the definitions. Wrong, and it is exactly backwards. The definitions are in the class; the values are in the object.
  • There can be only one object of any given class. Wrong. A class exists precisely so that many objects can be made from it.

3. The two answers are It hides an object's internal data and exposes it only through its methods and It protects an object from being changed in ways its own methods do not allow. Encapsulation is data hiding plus controlled access. The two halves go together: hiding the data is pointless without a defined way in, and a defined way in is what lets the object keep itself valid.

  • It hides an object's internal data and exposes it only through its methods. Correct. Attributes are private, methods are the public door.
  • It means one class inherits the attributes of another. Wrong. That is inheritance, a different concept entirely.
  • It protects an object from being changed in ways its own methods do not allow. Correct. Because every change goes through a method, the object can refuse an invalid one.
  • It allows the same method name to behave differently in different classes. Wrong. That is polymorphism.

4. The two answers are Inheritance lets a subclass reuse the attributes and methods of a superclass and Polymorphism lets the same message produce different behaviour in different classes. Inheritance is about REUSE and is a relationship between classes. Polymorphism is about BEHAVIOUR and is what makes that reuse worth having: the caller sends one message and each subclass answers it in its own way.

  • Inheritance lets a subclass reuse the attributes and methods of a superclass. Correct. The subclass gets everything the superclass has and adds or overrides what it needs.
  • Polymorphism lets the same message produce different behaviour in different classes. Correct. Draw() sent to Circle and to Square produces two different drawings from one call.
  • Inheritance is drawn as a filled diamond on the superclass end. Wrong. Inheritance, or generalisation, is a hollow TRIANGLE pointing at the superclass. A filled diamond is composition.
  • Polymorphism removes the need for classes to define their own methods. Wrong. Polymorphism depends on each class defining its own version. Without those definitions there is nothing to vary.

5. The answer is Name, attributes, operations. Name, then data, then behaviour. It is worth writing in that order every time, because an examiner scanning a page of class boxes reads the top line first and a box with no name is a box with no marks.

  • Name, attributes, operations. Correct. The class name on top, its attributes in the middle, its operations or methods at the bottom.
  • Name, operations, attributes. Wrong. Data comes before behaviour: what the thing HAS, then what it DOES.
  • Attributes, name, operations. Wrong. The name is always the top compartment.
  • Name, attributes, relationships. Wrong. Relationships are drawn as lines BETWEEN boxes, never inside one.

6. The two answers are Department to Lecturer is aggregation, drawn with a hollow diamond and House to Room is composition, drawn with a filled diamond. The test is LIFETIME, not ownership. If the part survives the whole it is aggregation and the diamond is hollow; if the part dies with the whole it is composition and the diamond is filled. The two symbols look almost identical and mean opposite things, which is exactly why the paper asks.

  • Department to Lecturer is aggregation, drawn with a hollow diamond. Correct. The lecturer outlives the department, so the whole-to-part link is the weaker one.
  • House to Room is composition, drawn with a filled diamond. Correct. A room cannot exist without its house, so the diamond is filled.
  • Department to Lecturer is composition, because a lecturer belongs to a department. Wrong. Belonging is not the test. A lecturer belongs to a department and still survives it.
  • Both are drawn with a hollow triangle, because both are whole to part. Wrong. A hollow triangle is generalisation, that is inheritance, which is a different relationship altogether.

7. The two answers are An include relationship means the included use case ALWAYS runs as part of the base use case and An extend relationship means the extending use case runs only under a stated condition. Include is compulsory and factors out shared behaviour; extend is conditional and adds behaviour in special cases. Both arrows are dashed with an open head, and the direction is what separates them: include points AT what is included, extend points AT what is being extended.

  • An include relationship means the included use case ALWAYS runs as part of the base use case. Correct. Verify PIN is included by Withdraw Cash because it happens every time.
  • An extend relationship means the extending use case runs only under a stated condition. Correct. Print Receipt extends Withdraw Cash because it happens only if the customer asks.
  • An include relationship means the included use case is optional. Wrong. Optional is extend. Include is always.
  • The arrow of an include points from the included use case to the base use case. Wrong. The include arrow points FROM the base use case TO the included one.

8. The two answers are A diamond represents a decision, with a guard condition on each outgoing branch and A thick bar represents a fork into, or a join of, parallel flows. The diamond BRANCHES, the bar PARALLELISES, and the rounded rectangle acts. A swimlane answers a different question: not when, but WHO.

  • A diamond represents a decision, with a guard condition on each outgoing branch. Correct. The guards must be mutually exclusive and cover every case, or the flow can stall or split.
  • A thick bar represents a fork into, or a join of, parallel flows. Correct. One bar in and several out is a fork; several in and one out is a join.
  • A diamond represents an action carried out by the system. Wrong. An action is a rounded rectangle. A diamond never does work.
  • A swimlane shows the order in which actions occur. Wrong. A swimlane shows WHO or WHAT performs each action. Order is carried by the arrows.

9. The two answers are Time runs down the page, so the vertical order of messages is their order in time and A vertical dashed line under an object is its lifeline. A sequence diagram is a BEHAVIOURAL diagram: participants across the top, time down the side, messages as horizontal arrows between lifelines. Structure is what a class diagram is for.

  • Time runs down the page, so the vertical order of messages is their order in time. Correct, and it is the one rule that makes the diagram readable. Read it top to bottom like a script.
  • A vertical dashed line under an object is its lifeline. Correct. The lifeline is the object's existence over time, with an activation bar drawn on it while it is actually doing something.
  • Time runs left to right across the page. Wrong. The participants are arranged left to right; TIME is the vertical axis.
  • A sequence diagram shows the static structure of the classes involved. Wrong. Static structure is the class diagram. A sequence diagram shows one scenario unfolding.

10. The answer is One order has one or more order lines, and each order line belongs to exactly one order. Multiplicity is written at the END NEAR the class it describes, so read across the line to find which number applies to which class. 1..* is one or more, 0..* is zero or more, and a bare 1 is exactly one.

  • One order has one or more order lines, and each order line belongs to exactly one order. Correct. 1..* at the OrderLine end is one or more lines; 1 at the Order end is exactly one order.
  • One order has exactly one order line. Wrong. The star means many. Exactly one would be written 1.
  • One order may have no order lines. Wrong. Zero would need 0..*, and the lower bound here is 1.
  • Many orders share the same order line. Wrong. The 1 at the Order end forbids that: a line belongs to one order only.

Answers, and why every option is what it is

1. The two answers are Direct cutover switches off the old system and starts the new one at once, which is cheapest and riskiest and Parallel running operates both systems together for a period and compares their output. The four strategies trade cost against risk. Direct is cheap and dangerous, parallel is expensive and safe, phased brings the system in a part at a time, and pilot brings it in at one place at a time.

  • Direct cutover switches off the old system and starts the new one at once, which is cheapest and riskiest. Correct. There is no fallback, which is exactly why it is the cheapest and the most exposed.
  • Parallel running operates both systems together for a period and compares their output. Correct. Running both and comparing is what makes it safe, and paying for both is what makes it costly.
  • Phased installation means installing the whole system at one site before the others. Wrong. Installing at one site first is the PILOT strategy. Phased brings the system in module by module.
  • Parallel running is the cheapest strategy because only one system is paid for. Wrong, and it is the reverse: parallel running is the most expensive, because both systems and both sets of staff effort are paid for at once.

2. The two answers are Building in house gives a closer fit to the organisation's own processes and Buying a package usually delivers faster and at a lower initial cost. The trade is FIT against TIME and COST. Building fits exactly and takes longer; buying arrives sooner and asks the organisation to bend towards the software.

  • Building in house gives a closer fit to the organisation's own processes. Correct. It is built to the requirements you gathered, so it matches them.
  • Buying a package usually delivers faster and at a lower initial cost. Correct. The development has already been paid for by everyone else who bought it.
  • Buying a package guarantees a closer fit to the business processes. Wrong, and it is the main argument against buying: the package embodies someone else's idea of the process.
  • Building in house removes the need for a feasibility study. Wrong. Feasibility is assessed whichever route is chosen, and the choice itself is part of what feasibility examines.

3. The answer is Presentation, application logic, and data. The tiers separate what the user sees, what the system decides, and where the data lives. The point of the split is that any one tier can be changed or scaled without rewriting the others.

  • Presentation, application logic, and data. Correct. Presentation at the front, business logic in the middle, the database behind.
  • Input, processing, and output. Wrong. Input, process, output is the general model of any program, not an architecture.
  • Hardware, software, and people. Wrong. Those are components of an information SYSTEM, not tiers of an architecture.
  • Analysis, design, and implementation. Wrong. Those are phases of the life cycle.

4. The two answers are Corrective maintenance fixes faults found after the system went live and Adaptive maintenance changes the system to suit a new environment or a new regulation. Four kinds, and the examiner wants them separated: corrective fixes what is broken, adaptive responds to a changed environment, perfective improves what already works, and preventive reduces future failure.

  • Corrective maintenance fixes faults found after the system went live. Correct. It is the reactive one: something is wrong, and it is repaired.
  • Adaptive maintenance changes the system to suit a new environment or a new regulation. Correct. A new tax rate or a new operating system forces adaptive change though nothing is broken.
  • Perfective maintenance means removing features that are no longer used. Wrong. Perfective maintenance IMPROVES performance or maintainability, or adds requested enhancements.
  • Preventive maintenance is the same thing as corrective maintenance. Wrong. Preventive works on code that has not failed yet; corrective works on code that has.

5. The two answers are A Gantt chart shows tasks as bars against a calendar, making durations and overlaps visible and The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks, and it sets the shortest possible project duration. A Gantt chart shows WHEN; a network diagram shows WHAT DEPENDS ON WHAT. The critical path comes out of the second and is the sequence with no slack, so a day lost anywhere on it is a day lost on the project.

  • A Gantt chart shows tasks as bars against a calendar, making durations and overlaps visible. Correct. It is the calendar view, and it is what a client is usually shown.
  • The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks, and it sets the shortest possible project duration. Correct. It is longest and therefore binding: nothing can finish sooner than the critical path allows.
  • The critical path is the chain of tasks that can slip without affecting the end date. Wrong, and it is exactly backwards. Tasks that can slip have FLOAT, and they are by definition not on the critical path.
  • A Gantt chart shows the dependencies between tasks better than any other tool. Wrong. Dependencies are what a PERT or network diagram shows. A Gantt chart shows them poorly, which is why both tools exist.

6. The two answers are Be consistent, so the same action produces the same result everywhere in the system and Give feedback, so the user always knows what the system is doing and what happened. Consistency and feedback are the two that appear on every list. Both reduce the load on the user's memory, which is the underlying goal of the whole subject.

  • Be consistent, so the same action produces the same result everywhere in the system. Correct. Consistency means the interface can be learned once rather than relearned per screen.
  • Give feedback, so the user always knows what the system is doing and what happened. Correct. A user with no feedback cannot tell a slow system from a broken one.
  • Maximise the amount of information on every screen so fewer screens are needed. Wrong. Crowding a screen raises the error rate. The principle is the opposite: reduce what the eye must sort through.
  • Require the user to memorise codes, since experts work faster with codes. Wrong. Recognition over recall is the principle. The system should show the options, not demand that the user remembers them.
