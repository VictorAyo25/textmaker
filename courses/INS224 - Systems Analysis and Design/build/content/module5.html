Module Five · Unit 1

UML and Class Diagrams

By the end of this unit you can say what UML is and who created it, sort UML diagrams into the two categories, draw a class with its attributes and methods, show relationships and multiplicities, and tell an object from a class.

TeachWhat UML is

The Unified Modeling Language (UML) is a standard set of diagram notations for modelling object-oriented systems. It was created in the 1990s by Grady Booch, James Rumbaugh and Ivar Jacobson, often called the "three amigos", who merged their separate methods into one, and it is maintained by the Object Management Group. Its uses: to visualise, specify, construct and document the parts of a software system, so that analysts, designers and programmers share one language.

The paper asks you to "write short notes on UML, stating its inventors and its uses." That is the paragraph above: what it is, the three inventors, and the four uses (visualise, specify, construct, document).

Must memoriseThe two UML categories

The paper asks you to "categorise UML diagrams into two broad categories, mention two diagrams in each, and describe their function." The two categories are:

  • Structural diagrams show the static structure, the things that make up the system and how they are arranged. Two examples: the class diagram (classes and their relationships) and the object diagram (objects at a moment in time). Their function is to model what the system is.
  • Behavioural diagrams show the dynamic behaviour, how the system acts over time. Two examples: the use case diagram (what users can do) and the activity diagram (the flow of a process); the sequence diagram is a third. Their function is to model what the system does.

Memory hook: structure is a noun, behaviour is a verb. Class and object diagrams are nouns (things); use case, activity and sequence diagrams are verbs (happenings).

TeachObject versus class

"Differentiate between an object and a class" is a standard 2-mark question. A class is a blueprint or template that defines the attributes and methods a kind of thing has; an object is a specific instance created from that class, with real values. Car is a class; your particular car, with its registration and colour, is an object. One class, many objects. This idea comes from object-oriented programming (OOP), where a program is built from objects that hold data and behaviour together.

Must memoriseThe class box

A class is drawn as a rectangle in three compartments: the class name at the top, its attributes (data) in the middle, and its methods or operations (behaviour) at the bottom. A visibility mark sits before each member: minus for private (hidden inside the class), plus for public (visible outside). The paper asks you to "draw a class with at least two attributes and two methods", so practise the box until it is automatic.

Must memoriseRelationships and multiplicity

Classes are joined by relationships, each with a different line-end, and each line carries a multiplicity saying how many objects take part. Multiplicity is the class-diagram version of cardinality: 1 (exactly one), 0..1 (optional), * or 0..* (many), 1..* (one or more). The relationships:

  • Association (plain line): the classes are connected. A ward has patients.
  • Aggregation (hollow diamond at the whole): a "has-a" where the part can exist without the whole. A department aggregates lecturers; the lecturers survive if the department closes.
  • Composition (filled diamond at the whole): a stronger "has-a" where the part cannot exist without the whole. A ward is composed of a convenience room; no ward, no room.
  • Generalization / inheritance (hollow triangle at the parent): an "is-a". A new patient and an old patient are both a patient.

Memory hook: hollow diamond is a loose has-a, filled diamond is a life-or-death has-a, hollow triangle is an is-a.

WorkedA class diagram from a scenario

Take the hospital scenario: a patient is made up of one patient detail (PId, name, age, gender); a patient is either a new patient or an old patient; a ward holds at least 8 patients; a ward is made up of one ward detail (WardNo, No_of_Bed, WType); a convenience room is part of a ward; a ward has at least two nurses. Draw the class diagram with relationships and multiplicities.

  1. Make a class for each noun. Patient, PatientDetail, NewPatient, OldPatient, Ward, WardDetail, ConvenienceRoom, Nurse. Give each its attributes.
  2. "Is made up of one detail" is composition. Patient has a filled diamond to PatientDetail (1); Ward has a filled diamond to WardDetail (1). The detail cannot exist without its owner.
  3. "Either a new or an old patient" is generalization. NewPatient and OldPatient both point with a hollow triangle to Patient.
  4. "A ward holds at least 8 patients", "at least two nurses" are associations with multiplicity. Ward to Patient marked 1 on the ward end and 8..* on the patient end; Ward to Nurse marked 2..*.
  5. "A convenience room is part of a ward" is composition or aggregation. "Part of" a ward, and it makes no sense without the ward, so a filled diamond from Ward to ConvenienceRoom.

Now you try: draw the class diagram for an order system where an order is made up of three order items, an order is either a special order or a normal order, and a customer places at most 20 orders. Which relationship is each?

RecallCover the answers

1. Who invented UML, and name the two diagram categories.

2. What are the three compartments of a class box?

3. Which line-end means composition, and what does composition imply?

Answers

1. Booch, Rumbaugh and Jacobson; structural and behavioural diagrams.

2. Name, attributes, methods.

3. A filled diamond at the whole; the part cannot exist without the whole.

Module Five · Unit 2

Object-Oriented Design Concepts

By the end of this unit you can explain encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism, composition, aggregation and association with an example each, and state the drawbacks of multiple inheritance.

TeachThe concepts, with an example each

"Briefly describe the following object-oriented design concepts with relevant examples" is on almost every paper. Learn one clean sentence and one example for each:

  • Encapsulation: bundling data and the methods that act on it inside one class, and hiding the data behind those methods (private attributes, public methods). Example: a BankAccount hides its balance and exposes deposit() and withdraw().
  • Inheritance: a class (the child or subclass) takes on the attributes and methods of another (the parent or superclass) and can add its own. Example: a SavingsAccount inherits from Account and adds interest.
  • Polymorphism: the same method name behaves differently on different classes. Example: draw() on a Circle and draw() on a Square both draw, each in its own way.
  • Composition: an object is built from other objects that cannot exist without it (a strong "has-a"). Example: a House is composed of Rooms.
  • Aggregation: an object is built from other objects that can exist independently (a weak "has-a"). Example: a Team aggregates Players, who exist without the team.
  • Association: two classes are related and can use each other, but neither owns the other. Example: a Doctor treats a Patient.
Must memoriseComposition vs aggregation

The examiner loves to test composition against aggregation because they look almost the same. The one distinction: composition is a life-or-death has-a (the part dies with the whole, filled diamond); aggregation is a loose has-a (the part lives on, hollow diamond). House and Rooms is composition; Team and Players is aggregation.

Memory hook: break a House and the Rooms go with it (composition); disband a Team and the Players walk away (aggregation).

TeachMultiple inheritance and its drawbacks

Multiple inheritance is when one class inherits from more than one parent at once. It is powerful but the paper asks for its drawbacks (worth 2.5 to 3.5 marks), so learn three:

  • The diamond problem (ambiguity): if two parents both provide a method with the same name, the child does not know which to inherit.
  • Increased complexity: the class hierarchy is harder to understand, design and maintain.
  • Name clashes: attributes or methods with the same name in different parents collide, and resolving them is error-prone.

This is why several modern languages (such as Java) forbid multiple inheritance of classes and offer interfaces instead.

RecallCover the answers

1. Define encapsulation and give an example.

2. What is polymorphism?

3. State two drawbacks of multiple inheritance.

Answers

1. Bundling data with the methods that use it and hiding the data behind them; a BankAccount hides its balance and exposes deposit() and withdraw().

2. The same method name behaving differently on different classes (draw() on a Circle versus a Square).

3. Any two of: the diamond problem (ambiguity), increased complexity, name clashes.

Module Five · Unit 3

Activity and Sequence Diagrams

By the end of this unit you can draw an activity diagram of a process flow and a sequence diagram of an interaction over time, the two behavioural diagrams the paper asks for by name.

TeachThe activity diagram

An activity diagram is a flowchart of a process: it shows the order in which actions happen, including choices and parallel work. Its symbols:

  • A filled circle for the start, and a filled circle in a ring for the end.
  • A rounded rectangle for each action ("Check patient record").
  • A diamond for a decision, with the condition on each outgoing arrow ("current patient?" splitting into yes and no).
  • A solid bar (fork and join) where flows split to run in parallel and merge again.
  • Arrows for the flow of control from one action to the next.
TeachThe sequence diagram

A sequence diagram shows how objects interact over time, message by message. Time runs down the page. Its parts:

  • A box at the top for each participating object or actor, with a dashed lifeline hanging down from it.
  • A thin activation bar on a lifeline while that object is doing something.
  • A solid arrow for a message (a call) from one lifeline to another, labelled with the operation; a dashed arrow for the return.

You draw one when a question says "draw a sequence diagram for the interaction", for example a sales representative entering an order of up to twelve products: the messages go from the rep to the system and back, one per product.

RecallCover the answers

1. What does a diamond mean in an activity diagram?

2. In a sequence diagram, which way does time run, and what is a lifeline?

3. Is a sequence diagram structural or behavioural?

Answers

1. A decision, with a condition labelling each outgoing branch.

2. Time runs down the page; a lifeline is the dashed vertical line showing an object's existence through time.

3. Behavioural (it shows behaviour over time).
