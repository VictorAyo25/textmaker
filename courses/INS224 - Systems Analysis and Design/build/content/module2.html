Module Two

Requirements:
Discovery and Modelling

A system can only be as good as the understanding it is built on. This module is the Analysis phase in detail: how to find out what a business really needs, how to write those needs down so nothing is lost, and how to draw the first model of the system, the use case diagram.
Module Two · Unit 1

Fact-Finding and Requirements

By the end of this unit you can tell a functional requirement from a non-functional one, name and justify the requirement elicitation techniques with their stakeholders and limitations, and explain the as-is and to-be view of a system.

TeachWhat a requirement is

A requirement is a statement of something the system must do or a quality it must have. The whole of the Analysis phase exists to discover, agree and record requirements, because everything designed and built afterwards is built to satisfy them. Get the requirements wrong and every later phase faithfully builds the wrong system. Requirements are divided two ways, and the examiner asks about both.

By what they describe:

  • A functional requirement is something the system must do: a function or feature. "The system shall let a customer place an order." "The system shall calculate the total of an order." They map directly onto use cases and processes.
  • A non-functional requirement is a quality the system must have: how well it does things, not what it does. Performance, security, usability, reliability, availability. "The system shall respond within two seconds." "The system shall encrypt all passwords."
Must memoriseFunctional vs non-functional

Functional = what it does (a verb). Non-functional = how well it does it (a quality). The paper asks you to formulate three functional and two non-functional requirements from a scenario. Functional ones start "the system shall [verb]"; non- functional ones name a quality (fast, secure, reliable, usable, available).

Memory hook: non-functional requirements are the "-ilities": security, reliability, usability, scalability, availability.

TeachThree levels of requirement

By whose language they are in, requirements come at three levels. The 25/26 paper asks you to formulate all three from one scenario, so keep them separate:

  • Business requirements: the high-level goals of the organisation. "Reduce the time to process an admission by half." Written in the language of the business.
  • User requirements: what a particular user needs to do with the system. "A nurse needs to record a patient's vitals at the bedside." Written from the user's point of view, often as user stories.
  • System requirements: the detailed, technical statements the developers build to. "The system shall store vitals as time-stamped records linked to a patient ID." These are the functional and non-functional requirements above.

They nest: business goals drive user needs, which drive system requirements.

TeachAs-is and to-be

Analysis studies two versions of the system. The as-is system is the current one, with all its weaknesses; the to-be system is the proposed one, with the improvements. A common exam task is to "identify the weaknesses of the as-is system, the expected improvements in the to-be system, and the assumptions that must be validated." Answer it in exactly those three parts: list what is wrong now, list what the new system will fix, and list the things you are assuming that had better be true (for example, that users have smartphones, or that the network reaches every ward).

TeachHow requirements are gathered

Discovering requirements is called fact-finding, requirements elicitation, or gathering information. The examiner asks you to choose two or three techniques and, for each, justify why it suits this project, say what information it collects and from whom, and give a limitation. Learn the techniques with those hooks:

TechniqueWhat / whoA limitation
InterviewOne-to-one questions with a key user or manager; rich, deep information, can follow up.Time-consuming; quality depends on the interviewer; only a few people.
QuestionnaireWritten questions to many people at once; cheap way to reach a large, scattered group.No follow-up; low response rate; answers can be shallow.
ObservationWatching users actually do the work; reveals what people really do, not what they say.People behave differently when watched; time-consuming; a snapshot.
Document reviewReading existing forms, reports, procedures and business rules; grounds you in facts.Documents may be out of date or not match real practice.
JADJoint application development (JAD): a facilitated workshop bringing users and analysts together; fast consensus.Hard to schedule; needs a skilled facilitator; dominant voices can skew it.
PrototypingBuilding a quick model for users to react to; surfaces requirements people cannot state in the abstract.Users may think it is finished; can encourage scope creep.
WorkedDesigning an elicitation plan

The 25/26 paper asks for a full requirements elicitation plan: for each technique, the stakeholders involved, the information collected, and why it suits the project. Suppose the project is a new library management system.

  1. Pick techniques that cover different sources. One for depth (interview), one for breadth (questionnaire), one for reality (observation), one for facts (document review).
  2. Interview the head librarian. Information: the rules for borrowing, reservations and fines. Why: they hold the business rules and can be probed in depth.
  3. Questionnaire to student borrowers. Information: what they want from self-service. Why: cheaply reaches a large, scattered user group.
  4. Observe the issue desk at a busy hour. Information: the real check-out workflow and its bottlenecks. Why: shows what staff actually do, not what they report.
  5. Review existing forms and the current catalogue. Information: the data already held on books and members. Why: grounds the new system in real records.

Now you try: write the same four-line plan for a hospital appointment system. Who do you interview, who gets the questionnaire, what do you observe, and which documents do you review?

TeachGoals and overall strategy

Two smaller questions round the unit off. The goals of requirement analysis are to understand the business need, to define what the system must do, and to produce a clear, agreed, prioritised set of requirements. The overall strategy for fact- finding is the sensible order of work: start with document review and background reading to get oriented, then observe, then interview key people, then broaden with questionnaires, and confirm findings with the users before you model. Start broad and cheap, then go deep and targeted.

RecallCover the answers

1. Classify: "the system shall email a receipt" and "the system shall be available 99.9% of the time".

2. Which technique reaches many scattered people cheaply, and what is its main weakness?

3. What are the as-is and to-be systems?

Answers

1. The first is functional (a thing it does); the second is non-functional (a quality, availability).

2. A questionnaire; its weakness is no follow-up and often a low response rate.

3. The as-is is the current system with its weaknesses; the to-be is the proposed improved system.

Module Two · Unit 2

Use Case Modelling

By the end of this unit you can draw a use case diagram from a scenario, use the four relationships correctly, and write a use case narrative. This is the first Section B diagram, and it appears almost every year.

TeachActors and use cases

A use case is a single thing a user wants to achieve with the system, named as a verb phrase: "Place order", "Cancel appointment", "Print report". A use case diagram shows all the use cases of a system and who uses them. It has three pieces:

  • An actor, drawn as a stick figure: a role outside the system that interacts with it (a customer, a store keeper, an insurance carrier). An actor is a role, not a person, and can be another system.
  • A use case, drawn as an oval with the verb phrase inside.
  • The system boundary, a box drawn around all the use cases, with the actors outside it.

A plain line (an association) joins an actor to each use case it takes part in. That is the fourth relationship, and the simplest.

Must memoriseThe four relationships

"Identify the types of relationships in use cases" is worth marks every year. There are four, and the two dashed ones are where marks are won and lost:

  • Association (solid line): joins an actor to a use case it participates in.
  • Include (dashed arrow labelled «include»): one use case always uses another as a sub-step. "Place order" includes "Verify card": the check happens every time. The arrow points to the included use case.
  • Extend (dashed arrow labelled «extend»): one use case optionally adds behaviour to another under some condition. "Place order" is extended by "Apply discount": only sometimes. The arrow points from the extension to the base use case.
  • Generalization (solid line with a hollow triangle): one actor or use case is a special kind of another. A "New patient" and an "Old patient" are both a "Patient"; the triangle points to the general one.

Memory hook: Include is always, Extend is maybe. The include arrow points to the step that always runs; the extend arrow points back to the base it might add to.

TrapWhich way does the arrow point?

The commonest use case mistake is the direction of the include and extend arrows. Fix them with one sentence each: the include arrow points at the thing always done (base → included), and the extend arrow points at the thing being added to (extension → base). Draw both dashed, both labelled with the stereotype in guillemets, or the examiner cannot tell them from an association.

WorkedBuilding the diagram from words

A recurring scenario: a sales management system lets a sales representative record orders placed by customers and check out orders, and lets a store keeper keep inventory. Recording an order includes updating the order; checkout extends edit-order; keeping inventory includes updating product details. Draw the use case diagram.

  1. Find the actors. The roles outside the system that use it: Sales representative and Store keeper. Draw them as stick figures outside the boundary.
  2. Find the use cases (the verbs). Record order, Check out order, Update order, Edit order, Keep inventory, Update product details. Draw each as an oval inside the boundary.
  3. Join actors to their use cases with associations. Sales rep to Record order and Check out order; Store keeper to Keep inventory.
  4. Add the include relationships (always). Record order «include» Update order; Keep inventory «include» Update product details. Dashed arrows to the included case.
  5. Add the extend relationship (maybe). Check out order «extend» Edit order: the dashed arrow points from Check out to the base it extends.

Now you try: redraw it for the video store, where a member can Rent video (which includes Check for overdue) and Return video, and a manager can Print overdue report. Which relationship is the include?

TeachThe use case narrative

A diagram shows which use cases exist; a use case narrative (or use case description) tells the story of one of them step by step. A simple narrative has: the use case name, the actor, a precondition, the main flow (the normal sequence of steps), and alternative flows (what happens when something is different, for example the card fails). You write a narrative when a question says "describe" or "give the flow of" a use case rather than "draw".

RecallCover the answers

1. Name the four use case relationships.

2. "Withdraw cash" always runs "Authenticate". Which relationship, and which way does the arrow point?

3. What does a use case narrative add that the diagram does not?

Answers

1. Association, include, extend, generalization.

2. Include; the dashed arrow points from "Withdraw cash" to "Authenticate" (the step always done).

3. The step-by-step flow of one use case (precondition, main flow, alternatives).
