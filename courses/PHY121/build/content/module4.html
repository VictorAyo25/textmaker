U N I T 4 . 1

Magnetic fields and the Lorentz force

Everything here follows from one formula and one hand rule. Get those two secure and the unit is done.

Teach · what a magnetic field is

A magnetic field is a vector field describing the magnetic influence on moving electric charges, electric currents, and magnetic materials. Its symbol is B. The crucial sentence, which contains the whole unit: a moving charge in a magnetic field experiences a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field. Unit: the tesla (T), where 1 T = 1 N s / (C m).

Must-memorise · properties of magnetic field lines

▸They form closed loops. ▸Outside a magnet they run from the north pole to the south pole. ▸They never intersect. ▸Their density shows the strength: closer lines mean a stronger field.

MEMORY HOOK: the key difference from electric field lines. Electric lines begin on positive

charges and end on negative ones. Magnetic lines neither begin nor end: they always close on themselves. This is because there is no such thing as an isolated magnetic pole.

Teach · two more differences from the electric field

1. Electric field lines lie along the direction of the force on a positive charge. Magnetic field lines are perpendicular to the magnetic force on a moving charge. So the field does not tell you the force direction directly, which is why we need a hand rule. 2. Electric field lines begin and end on charges; magnetic field lines never do. Sources of magnetic fields: moving charges (that is, currents), permanent magnets, and changing electric fields.

Must-memorise · the lorentz force on a moving charge
F = qvB sin θ

F = magnetic force, in newtons (N) q = charge, in coulombs (C) v = speed of the charge, in m/s B = magnetic flux density, in tesla (T) θ = angle between the velocity v and the field B

MEMORY HOOK: the sin θ does all the work. Move along the field (θ = 0) and sin 0 = 0, so there

is no force at all. Move across it (θ = 90°) and sin 90° = 1, giving the maximum force, F = qvB.

Trap · a stationary charge feels no magnetic force

Look at F = qvB sin θ. If v = 0, then F = 0. A charge sitting still in a magnetic field feels nothing, no matter how strong the field. This is completely unlike an electric field, which pushes a charge whether it moves or not. Magnetic fields influence only moving charges. Remember this and half the conceptual questions in this module answer themselves.

Must-memorise · the right-hand rule

To find the direction of the force on a positive charge: ▸Point your fingers along the velocity v. ▸Curl or point your palm in the direction of the field B. ▸Your thumb now points along the force F.

MEMORY HOOK, AND A WARNING THE SLIDES GIVE IN CAPITALS: the right-hand rule is for a

POSITIVE charge. For an electron or any negative charge, work out the direction for a positive charge and then reverse it. Forgetting this flip is the most common error in the module.

Teach · field into and out of the page

Because this is a three-dimensional problem drawn on flat paper, two symbols are used: ▸× means the field points into the page (think of the tail feathers of an arrow flying away from you). ▸⊙ (a dot) means the field points out of the page (the tip of an arrow coming towards you).

Must-memorise · a magnetic field does no work

The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the motion. Work is force times displacement times cos θ, and here θ = 90°, so:

W = F d cos 90° = 0

Therefore, in a magnetic field: ▸Kinetic energy remains constant. ▸Speed remains constant. ▸Only the direction changes.

MEMORY HOOK: a magnetic field steers but never speeds up. It cannot change the speed or the

kinetic energy of a particle, only bend its path. An electric field, by contrast, can accelerate a charge and change its energy.

Teach · the three cases of motion

1. Motion parallel to the field (θ = 0). sin 0 = 0, so F = 0. The particle carries straight on, undeflected. 2. Motion perpendicular to the field (θ = 90°). The force is maximum and always at right angles to the velocity, so it acts as a centripetal force. The particle moves in a circle. 3. Motion at an angle θ. Split the velocity into components (Foundations F.3):

vparallel = v cos θ      vperpendicular = v sin θ

The perpendicular component produces circular motion; the parallel component carries the particle steadily forward. Combine a circle with a forward drift and you get a helix, that is, a spiral. The forward distance covered in one full revolution is called the pitch.

Must-memorise · radius, period and cyclotron frequency

For circular motion, the magnetic force supplies the centripetal force: qvB = mv2/r. Cancel one v and rearrange:

r = mv / (qB)

The angular velocity is ω = v/r, and substituting the radius gives ω = qB/m. The frequency f = ω/2π is therefore:

f = qB / (2πm)

MEMORY HOOK, AND THE REASON CYCLOTRONS WORK: look hard at that frequency. It does

not contain v. The frequency is completely independent of the particle's speed. Speed it up and the radius grows, but it still completes each circle in the same time. This is the cyclotron frequency, and it is the entire principle behind particle accelerators.

Teach · reading the radius formula

From r = mv/(qB), the radius depends on the particle's mass, velocity, charge and the field strength. Specifically: ▸Increasing the velocity increases the radius (v is on the top). ▸Increasing the magnetic field decreases the radius (B is on the bottom). A stronger field bends the particle more tightly. That is exactly what you would expect, and it is how a mass spectrometer separates ions by mass.

Answer

θ = 0, so sin θ = 0, so F = 0. It feels no force at all and travels in a straight line, completely undeflected. A magnetic field only acts on the component of velocity that is across it.

Sources of magnetic fields

Currents make magnetic fields. Two laws compute them: Biot-Savart for any shape, Ampere's law for symmetric ones.

Teach · magnetic force on a current-carrying conductor

A wire carrying a current, placed in a magnetic field, experiences a force. This is not a new phenomenon: the wire is full of moving charges, and each one feels the Lorentz force. Add up the effect on all of them and you get a net force on the whole wire. This is the operating principle of electric motors, loudspeakers, galvanometers, moving-coil meters and rail guns.

Must-memorise · force on a current-carrying conductor
F = BIL sin θ

F = magnetic force (N) B = magnetic flux density (T) I = current (A) L = length of conductor inside the field (m) θ = angle between the current and the field

MEMORY HOOK: this is F = qvB sin θ for a whole wire, with the charge and speed replaced by

current and length. Same physics, tidier packaging.

Teach · the two special cases

(a) Conductor parallel to the field (θ = 0): F = BIL sin 0 = 0. No force at all. (b) Conductor perpendicular to the field (θ = 90°): F = BIL, which is the maximum force. These are not two extra formulas to memorise. They are the one formula with sin 0 = 0 and sin 90° = 1 substituted in. The force increases with the field strength B, the current I, the length L, and the angle, reaching its maximum at 90° and vanishing at 0°.

Must-memorise · fleming's left-hand rule

For the direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor, hold the left hand so that: ▸First finger points along the magnetic Field (B) ▸SeCond finger points along the Current (I) ▸ThuMb points along the Motion or force (F)

MEMORY HOOK: the initials line up with the fingers themselves. First is Field, seCond is Current,

thuMb is Motion. Note this is the LEFT hand for the force on a current, whereas the right hand is used for the field produced by a current. Confusing the two hands is a classic error.

The Biot-Savart law
Teach · where the law came from

In 1820 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Oersted noticed that a compass needle deflected when placed near a current-carrying wire. That was the discovery that electricity and magnetism are connected: a current produces a magnetic field. In the same year the French physicists Jean-Baptiste Biot and Felix Savart worked out the quantitative relationship. It lets us calculate the magnetic field produced by any current distribution, which makes it fundamental to electromagnetism. FORMULA · THE BIOT-SAVART LAW The field dB produced by a small current element I dl is directly proportional to the current and the length of the element, proportional to the sine of the angle between the element and the line joining it to the point, and inversely proportional to the square of the distance:

dB = (µ0 / 4π) · (I dl × r̂) / r2

µ0 = permeability of free space = 4π × 10−7 H/m I = current (A), dl = length element in the direction of the current r = distance from the element to the point of interest

Note this is directly analogous to Coulomb's law for the electric field of a point charge: an inverse

square law, with a constant out the front. The direction of dB is perpendicular to both r and dl.

Must-memorise · the two standard biot-savart results

1. A long straight wire, at distance r from it:

B = µ0I / (2πr)

2. At the centre of a circular loop of radius R:

B = µ0I / (2R)    and for N turns    B = µ0NI / (2R)

MEMORY HOOK: the straight wire has 2πr underneath (the circumference of the circle you would

walk around it). The loop has just 2R. The field increases with current and decreases with distance.

Worked example · field 5 cm from a wire carrying 12 aSlide

The question. A long straight conductor carries a current of 12 A. Calculate the magnetic field at a point 5 cm from the conductor.

  1. Use the straight-wire result: B = µ0I / (2πr).
  2. Convert: r = 5 cm = 0.05 m. The usual trap. Centimetres are 10−2 m.
  3. Numerator: µ0I = (4π × 10−7)(12) = 1.508 × 10−5. 4π = 12.566, times 12 gives 150.8, so 150.8 × 10−7 = 1.508 × 10−5.
  4. Denominator: 2πr = 2π(0.05) = 0.3142.
  5. B = (1.508 × 10−5) / 0.3142 = 4.8 × 10−5 T. B = 4.8 × 10−5 T

Now redo it: I recomputed this as 4.80 × 10−5 T, matching the slide. For scale, the Earth's magnetic

field is about 5 × 10−5 T, so a 12 A current at 5 cm produces roughly the same field as the entire planet.

That is why a compass needle jumps near a wire, which is precisely what Oersted saw.

Ampere's law
Teach · why we need a second law

Biot-Savart can compute the field of any current distribution, but for complicated geometries the integral becomes very unpleasant. In 1826 Andre-Marie Ampere found a shortcut for situations with high symmetry. The relationship between the two laws is exactly the relationship between Coulomb's law and Gauss's law in Module 1. One is general but laborious; the other is effortless when the symmetry is right. Ampere's law is one of the four Maxwell equations.

Must-memorise · ampere's circuital law

The line integral of the magnetic field around any closed path equals the permeability of free space multiplied by the total current enclosed by that path:

∮ B · dl = µ0 Ienclosed

The circle on the integral sign means integration around a closed path, called an Amperian loop. This is an imaginary closed path that you choose to make the calculation easy, exactly like a Gaussian surface.

MEMORY HOOK: the stronger the enclosed current, the stronger the magnetic field circulating

around it. Compare with Gauss's law: flux out equals charge enclosed. Here: circulation around equals current enclosed. Same idea, different geometry.

Worked example · deriving the straight-wire field from ampere's lawSlide

The task. Use Ampere's law to derive the magnetic field at a distance r from a long straight conductor carrying current I. (Biot-Savart gives the same result, but takes an integral to get there.)

along it.

  1. Take a long straight conductor carrying current I. Choose a circular Amperian loop of radius r centred on the wire. Chosen because by symmetry B has the same magnitude everywhere on this circle and points
  2. Since B is constant on the loop, the integral collapses to a simple product: ∮B · dl = B(2πr). 2πr is the circumference, from Foundations F.9.
  3. Set that equal to µ0I: B(2πr) = µ0I.

Look what happened: this is the same result Biot-Savart gives, obtained in four lines instead of an

Must-memorise · the right-hand grip rule

For the direction of the field around a current: ▸Point your thumb in the direction of the current. ▸Curl your fingers. ▸Your fingers now show the direction the magnetic field circulates.

MEMORY HOOK: thumb is current, fingers are field. The field wraps around the wire in circles,

like spaghetti coiled around it. It does not point along the wire or away from it.

Must-memorise · solenoid and toroid

A solenoid is a long coil of wire. Inside a long solenoid the field is nearly uniform, and outside it is very small. Applying Ampere's law with a rectangular loop gives BL = µ0NI, and with N = nL this becomes:

B = µ0n I

n = number of turns per metre. A toroid is a solenoid bent round into a ring. Ampere's law gives B(2πr) = µ0NI, so:

B = µ0NI / (2πr)

N = total number of turns.

MEMORY HOOK: for the solenoid, note that r does not appear. The field inside is uniform: the

same everywhere. That is exactly why solenoids are used whenever a controlled uniform field is needed, as in MRI scanners. FORMULA · THE AMPERE-MAXWELL LAW Ampere's law as stated is incomplete. Maxwell noticed that a changing electric field also creates a magnetic field, and added a term:

∮ B · dl = µ0 ( I + ε0 dΦE/dt )

The additional term is called the displacement current. In differential form, the original law reads ∇ × B = µ0J, where J is the current density. This completed set of equations predicted the existence of electromagnetic waves, and hence light itself.

Recall and check

The current in a long straight wire is doubled, and you also move twice as far away from it. What happens to the magnetic field?

Answer

B = µ0I/(2πr). Doubling I doubles B. Doubling r halves B. The two effects cancel exactly, so B is unchanged. Note this is a 1/r law, not an inverse square, so doubling the distance halves the field rather than quartering it.

Electromagnetic induction

Oersted showed that a current makes a magnetic field. Faraday asked the reverse question: can a magnetic field make a current? The answer, found in 1831, is the basis of the entire electrical power industry.

Teach · what induction is

Electromagnetic induction is the production of an emf, and in a closed circuit an electric current, whenever the magnetic flux linking a conductor changes. Michael Faraday discovered in 1831 that a changing magnetic field could induce a current in a nearby conductor with no direct electrical contact at all. A current is induced only if something changes: ▸The magnetic field changes. ▸The conductor moves relative to the field. ▸The area of the conducting loop changes. ▸The orientation of the conductor changes. It is the underlying principle of electric generators, transformers, induction motors, wireless charging, microphones, metal detectors and induction cookers.

Must-memorise · magnetic flux

Magnetic flux measures the amount of magnetic field passing through a surface. It is the number of field lines through a given area:

Φ = BA cos θ

Φ = magnetic flux, in webers (Wb), where 1 Wb = 1 T × 1 m2 B = magnetic flux density (T) A = area (m2) θ = angle between the field and the normal to the surface If the coil has N turns, the flux linkage is NΦ.

MEMORY HOOK: at θ = 0 the field is straight through the loop and cos 0 = 1, so the flux is

maximum at Φ = BA. At θ = 90° the field skims along the surface and cos 90° = 0, so the flux is zero. Same hoop-and-rain picture as the electric flux in Module 1.

Must-memorise · faraday's laws of induction

Faraday's first law. Whenever the magnetic flux linking a conductor changes, an emf is induced in the conductor. If the circuit is closed, an induced current flows. Faraday's second law. The magnitude of the induced emf is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux:

ε = −N · dΦ/dt

N = number of turns in the coil dΦ/dt = rate of change of flux, in Wb/s The minus sign is Lenz's law, explained next.

MEMORY HOOK: the word to underline is CHANGE. A huge steady flux induces nothing. A small

but rapidly changing flux induces a large emf. It is the rate that matters, not the size.

Must-memorise · lenz's law and the minus sign

The direction of the induced current is such that it opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it.

MEMORY HOOK: Lenz's law is a consequence of the conservation of energy. If the induced

current helped the change instead of opposing it, you would get a runaway: a tiny push would produce ever-growing current and energy from nothing. Nature forbids that. The induced effect always fights back, which is why you must do work to turn a generator. FORMULA · MOTIONAL EMF AND INDUCED CURRENT When a conductor of length L moves with velocity v through a field B:

ε = BLv

If the conductor forms part of a closed circuit, Ohm's law then gives the induced current:

I = ε / R

Teach · eddy currents, self-induction and mutual induction

Eddy currents. When a solid conductor experiences a changing flux, closed loops of current are set up inside it. These are eddy currents. They are used in magnetic braking, induction heating, metal detectors and speedometers. Self-induction. When the current changes in a coil, it induces an emf in that same coil. The constant of proportionality is the inductance L. Mutual induction. When the current changes in one coil, it induces an emf in another nearby coil. This is the principle of the transformer, of wireless charging, and of induction cookers.

Must-memorise · the transformer equation

A transformer transfers electrical energy between two coils by mutual induction:

Vs / Vp = Ns / Np

Vp, Vs = primary and secondary voltages Np, Ns = primary and secondary numbers of turns

MEMORY HOOK: the voltage ratio equals the turns ratio. More turns on the secondary means a

higher voltage out (a step-up transformer); fewer turns means a lower voltage (step-down). This is why transformers work only on AC: DC produces no change of flux, so it induces nothing at all.

Recall and check

A strong bar magnet is held perfectly still inside a coil of 1000 turns. What emf is induced?

Answer

Zero. The flux is large, but it is not changing, so dΦ/dt = 0 and therefore ε = 0. Faraday's law responds only to change. To induce anything you must move the magnet, move the coil, or vary the field.

emf, internal resistance and terminal voltage

A real battery is not ideal. It has resistance of its own, and that single fact explains why your headlights dim when you start the engine.

Teach · what emf really is

Electromotive force is the energy per unit electric charge imparted by an energy source such as a generator or a battery. Energy is converted from one form to another inside the device as it does work on the charge passing through it, leaving one terminal positive and the other negative. The work done per unit charge is the emf. Its unit is the volt: driving one coulomb through a potential difference of one volt requires one joule of work. Despite the name, emf is not a force. It is an energy per unit charge.

Must-memorise · emf versus terminal voltage

emf (E) is the potential difference of a source when no current is flowing. It is what the battery would give you if nothing were connected. Terminal voltage (V) is the voltage actually measured across the terminals while current flows. It is always less than the emf.

E = I(R + r)    so    I = E / (R + r)

E = emf of the cell (V) R = external resistance (Ω) r = internal resistance of the cell (Ω) I = current (A)

MEMORY HOOK: the total resistance is R + r. The battery has to push current through itself as

well as through the circuit, and that wastes some voltage internally. Terminal voltage V = E − Ir: the emf, minus what is lost inside.

Worked example 1 · find the emfSlide

An external resistor of 5 Ω is connected to a cell of internal resistance 0.5 Ω. The current flowing is 1 A. Calculate the emf of the cell.

the two add in series.

  1. Use I = E / (R + r), rearranged as E = I(R + r). The current must pass through both the external resistor and the cell's own internal resistance, so
  2. E = 1 × (5 + 0.5) = 1 × 5.5.
  3. E = 5.5 V. E = 5.5 V

Now redo it: the terminal voltage here would be V = IR = (1)(5) = 5.0 V, which is less than the 5.5 V

emf. The missing 0.5 V is dropped across the internal resistance and wasted as heat inside the battery.

Worked example 2 · from terminal p.d. to emfSlide

A cell of unknown emf E and internal resistance 2 Ω is connected to a 5 Ω resistance. If the terminal p.d. is 1 V, calculate E.

  1. The terminal p.d. is the voltage across the external 5 Ω resistor, so V = 1 V across R = 5 Ω. Terminal voltage is what appears across the external circuit.
  2. Find the current: I = V/R = 1/5 = 0.2 A. Ohm's law on the external resistor alone.
  3. The p.d. lost across the internal resistance: Ir = (0.2)(2) = 0.4 V. This is the voltage wasted inside the cell.
  4. The emf is the sum of the two: E = (p.d. across R) + (p.d. across r) = 1 + 0.4. E = 1.4 V

Check it the other way: using E = I(R + r) = 0.2(5 + 2) = 0.2 × 7 = 1.4 V. Both routes agree, which

is exactly the alternative solution given in the slides. Note the internal resistance is eating a large

fraction of this cell's emf, because r is not small compared with R.

Worked example 3 · two measurements, two unknownsSlide

The terminal p.d. of a battery is 12 V when an external resistance of 20 Ω is connected, and 13.5 V when 45 Ω is connected. Calculate the emf.

  1. Two unknowns (E and r), so we need two equations. Each measurement gives one. This is a simultaneous equation problem, exactly as in Foundations F.5.
  2. First measurement: I = V/R = 12/20 = 0.6 A. And E = I(R + r), so E = 0.6(20 + r) = 12 + 0.6r.   (1) Expand the bracket: 0.6 × 20 = 12.
  3. Second measurement: I = 13.5/45 = 0.3 A. So E = 0.3(45 + r) = 13.5 + 0.3r.   (2) Note the current is different in the two cases, because R changed.
  4. The emf E is the same battery in both, so set (1) equal to (2): 12 + 0.6r = 13.5 + 0.3r.This is the step that unlocks it. The battery has one fixed emf.

so the answer is consistent. This matches the slides exactly. Note the pattern: a larger external resistance

draws less current, so less voltage is lost internally, so the terminal voltage climbs closer to the true emf.

Teach · the physical meaning, and why headlights dim

Terminal voltage V = E − Ir. The larger the current drawn, the more voltage is lost inside the battery, and the lower the terminal voltage. Start a car engine and the starter motor draws an enormous current. That large I makes the Ir term large, so the terminal voltage collapses briefly, and the headlights dim. They recover the instant the engine catches and the current falls. Internal resistance is not an abstraction: you can watch it happen. As the external resistance R rises, the current falls, and the terminal voltage approaches the emf. With no current flowing at all (an open circuit), V = E exactly. This is the definition we started with.

Recall and check

A battery of emf 6 V and internal resistance r is connected to a 4 Ω wire, and 1 A flows. Find r. (This is the class drill from the slides.)

Answer

E = I(R + r), so 6 = 1(4 + r), giving 4 + r = 6 and therefore r = 2 Ω. Check: the terminal voltage would be IR = 4 V, and the loss inside is Ir = 2 V. Together, 4 + 2 = 6 V, the emf. It balances.

Inductance and energy stored in an inductor

This topic is examined in CBT Test 2, Q1, but it does not appear in any slide deck. It is here because the assessment demands it.

⚠ WHY THIS SECTION EXISTS The slides mention self-induction and name the inductance L, but they never give the en‐ ergy formula. Test 2 opens by asking for it. If you revise from the slides alone, you walk into the first question of the paper with nothing. That is the entire argument for blending the slides with the assessments rather than trusting either on its own.

Teach · what an inductor is

An inductor is usually a coil of wire. From Unit 4.3, when the current through a coil changes, the changing flux induces an emf in that same coil which opposes the change. That is self-induction. The constant relating the induced emf to the rate of change of current is the inductance L, measured in henries (H):

ε = −L · dI/dt

An inductor therefore resists changes in current, in the same way that mass resists changes in velocity. It is electrical inertia.

Must-memorise · energy stored in an inductor
W = ½L I2

W = energy stored, in joules (J) L = inductance, in henries (H) I = current through the inductor, in amperes (A)

MEMORY HOOK: this is the exact magnetic twin of the capacitor's W = ½CV2 from Unit 2.2. Swap

capacitance for inductance, and voltage for current. A capacitor stores energy in an electric field and cares about voltage; an inductor stores energy in a magnetic field and cares about current. Learn them as a pair and you get both for the price of one.

Teach · the symmetry that makes this free

If you can write one row of the bottom line, you can write the other. The whole of electromagnetism is built on symmetries like this one.

Test 2, q1 worked example · a 2 h inductor carrying 5 a

A 2 H inductor carries 5 A of current. What is the stored energy?

  1. Use the energy formula for an inductor: W = ½LI2. Given an inductance and a current, this is the only formula that fits.
  2. Square the current, not the inductance: I2 = 52 = 25. This is the one place people slip. It is L times I squared, not L squared times I.
  3. W = ½(2)(25) = ½(50). W = 25 J   (option a)  Method: §4.5

Now redo it: I recomputed this independently and confirm 25 J. Watch the distractor: if you forgot the

½ you would get 50 J, and if you squared the inductance instead you would get 10 J, which is offered as

option (d). Both traps are sitting there waiting for you.

Recall and check

A capacitor stores 8 J at 4 V. An inductor stores 8 J at 4 A. Write the formula you would use for each, and find C and L.

Answer

Capacitor: W = ½CV2, so C = 2W/V2 = 16/16 = 1 F. Inductor: W = ½LI2, so L = 2W/I2 = 16/16 = 1 H. Identical algebra. Only the letters change. That is the symmetry doing the work for you.

Square the current, not the inductance. Squaring L gives 10 J on Test 2 Q1, which is a planted distractor.

2. voltage, and current. 3. henries (H).

Torque on a current loop and the magnetic dipole

moment

Examined in CBT Test 2, Q7, and again absent from every slide deck. It is, however, a near- copy of the electric dipole from Unit 2.1, so you already know most of it.

Teach · a current loop is a magnet

From Unit 4.2, a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force F = BIL sin θ. Now bend that wire into a closed rectangular loop and put it in a uniform field. Two opposite sides of the loop carry current in opposite directions, so they feel forces in opposite directions. The forces cancel, so the loop does not fly away. But because they act on different sides of the loop, they produce a twist: a torque that rotates the loop until it lines up with the field. This is exactly what an electric dipole does in an electric field, and it is the operating principle of the electric motor and the moving-coil galvanometer.

Must-memorise · magnetic dipole moment and torque

The magnetic dipole moment of a loop of N turns, each of area A, carrying current I, is:

m = NIA

m is measured in ampere square metres (A m2). The torque on that loop in a uniform field B is:

τ = m B sin θ    (and so τ = NIAB sin θ)

θ = angle between the loop's magnetic moment and the field.

MEMORY HOOK: compare with the electric dipole from Unit 2.1, which had Τ = PE SIN Θ. It is the

identical formula with p replaced by m and E replaced by B. Torque uses sine. Spot the analogy and this becomes a question you can answer instantly.

Teach · the two dipoles, side by side

One structure, two contexts. Memorise the shape once.

Test 2, q7 worked example · find the magnetic dipole moment

A rectangular current loop in a uniform field experiences a torque of 4.8 × 10−3 N m. The field is 0.6 T and the angle between the loop's magnetic moment and the field is 30°. Calculate the magnetic dipole moment.

  1. Torque on a magnetic dipole: τ = mB sin θ. Same shape as the electric dipole result from Unit 2.1. Torque uses sine.
  2. Rearrange to isolate the unknown: m = τ / (B sin θ). Rearrange in symbols first, substitute afterwards.
  3. sin 30° = 0.5 exactly. One of the four values from Foundations F.4. No calculator needed.
  4. Denominator: B sin θ = (0.6)(0.5) = 0.3.
  5. m = (4.8 × 10−3) / 0.3 = 1.6 × 10−2. m = 1.6 × 10−2 A m2   (option a)  Method: §2.1, §4.64.8 / 0.3 = 16, so 16 × 10−3 = 1.6 × 10−2.

Now redo it: I recomputed this independently and confirm 1.6 × 10−2 A m2. The trap here is using cos

instead of sin: that would give 1.6 × 10−2 / √3 ≈ 0.92 × 10−2, which is not offered, so the examiner is

being kind. In a harder paper it would be. Torque always uses sine.

Recall and check

A 50-turn coil of area 2 × 10−3 m2 carries 0.4 A in a field of 0.2 T, at 90° to the field. Find the torque.

Answer

m = NIA = (50)(0.4)(2 × 10−3) = 4 × 10−2 A m2. τ = mB sin 90° = (4 × 10−2)(0.2)(1) = 8 × 10−3 N m. At 90° the torque is at its maximum, since sin 90° = 1.
