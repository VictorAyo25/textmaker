Module Two · Unit 1

The Entity-Relationship Model

A business hands you a description in words. Your job is to draw it: the things it cares about, how they connect, and the rules governing those connections. That drawing is the entity-relationship diagram, and drawing it correctly is half of this course's marks.

By the end of this unit you can: extract business rules from a description; draw entities, attributes, and relationships in a consistent notation; read and set cardinality with the crow's-foot symbols; and turn any of the recurring exam scenarios into a correct ER diagram.

1.1 From words to a model: business rules

A conceptual model is a high-level picture of the data an organisation cares about and how it relates, drawn without any concern for how a computer will store it. The entity-relationship (E-R) model is the most widely used one, and an ER diagram (ERD) is its picture. It is independent of any particular DBMS.

Everything in the diagram comes from business rules: statements, drawn from the organisation's policies and procedures, about the structure and behaviour of its data. A business rule is also called an integrity constraint once it is in the database. A rule such as "each project is contracted by exactly one client" is what fixes a cardinality on your diagram.

Must memoriseCharacteristics of a good business rule

A good business rule is:

  • Declarative: it states what should be true, not how to enforce it.
  • Precise: it has one clear, unambiguous meaning.
  • Atomic: it states one single thing, not several.
  • Consistent: it does not contradict another rule.
  • Expressible: it can be written in plain language.
  • Distinct: it does not duplicate another rule.
  • Business-oriented: it is stated in terms the business understands.
WorkedPQ 23/24 Q2b · PQ 21/22 Q6b

Q. State four qualities you would consider in making a good business rule (21/22 asks for four characteristics of a good business rule).

Any four of the seven above, each named and given a phrase: a good business rule is declarative (states what is true, not how to enforce it), precise (one unambiguous meaning), atomic (states a single fact), and business-oriented (expressed in the organisation's own terms). Naming and explaining four earns full marks; a bare list of words does not.

1.2 The notation, all of it, on one page

There is no single world standard for ER notation. This manual uses the common crow's-foot style that the recent papers use. Learn these symbols cold: an answer with the right idea and the wrong symbol is still marked wrong.

1.3 Entities: the things

An entity is a person, place, object, event, or concept the organisation keeps data about. It is named with a noun, in capitals, singular: EMPLOYEE, PROPERTY, SALE. Two distinctions the examiner tests by name:

  • Entity type vs entity instance. The entity type is the general category, EMPLOYEE, described once. An instance is one particular occurrence, the employee "S011, Tony Smith".
  • Strong vs weak entity. A strong (independent) entity exists on its own and has its own identifier. A weak (dependent) entity cannot exist without another entity, its identifying owner, and has no full identifier of its own, only a partial identifier. It is drawn with a double border.
WorkedTEST 1 C(a) · concept definitions

Q. Explain, with an example: weak entity type, strong entity type, unary relationship.

Weak entity type: an entity type whose existence depends on another entity type; a dependent entity that has no business meaning in the diagram without its owner. Example: a DEPENDENT of an employee (it exists only while the EMPLOYEE does).

Strong entity type: an entity type that exists independently of any other; an independent entity with its own identifier. Example: EMPLOYEE.

Unary relationship: a relationship between the instances of a single entity type; also called a recursive relationship. Example: an EMPLOYEE "manages" another EMPLOYEE. (Diagram in section 1.5.)

1.4 Attributes: the details

An attribute is a property of an entity, named with a noun. The types you must distinguish, because the examiner asks you to:

Must memoriseAttribute types
  • Simple: cannot be broken down (age).
  • Composite: made of parts (Name = firstName + lastName; Address = street + city + postcode).
  • Multivalued: may hold more than one value per instance (an employee's {skills}). Shown in curly braces.
  • Derived: calculated from other data ([age] from date of birth). Shown in square brackets.
  • Identifier (key): distinguishes instances; no two share it. Shown underlined. A composite identifier is made of more than one attribute.
WorkedPQ 24/25 Q2a(ii) · PQ 21/22 Q1(iv)

Q. Differentiate, with an example: (i) multivalued vs derived attribute; (ii) composite vs multivalued attribute.

A multivalued attribute can take several values at once for one instance, for example an employee's {skills} = {Java, SQL, French}. A derived attribute holds a value worked out from other stored data, not stored in its own right, for example [age] computed from dateOfBirth and today's date. One holds many real values; the other holds a computed one.

A composite attribute is one attribute divided into sub-parts, for example Address into (street, city, postcode). A multivalued attribute is one attribute with several values of the same kind, for example {phoneNumbers}. Composite is one value in parts; multivalued is several values.

Attributes are worth drawing when the question is about the attributes themselves, as in this one, where the whole task is to represent an entity's attribute structure.

1.5 Relationships and their degree

A relationship is an association between entity instances, named with a verb phrase on the line: "a STAFF manages a BRANCH". The degree of a relationship is the number of entity types it joins.

When a relationship itself has attributes (a grade for an enrolment, a date a certificate was completed), and especially when it is many-to-many, it is drawn as an associative entity: a rounded box on the relationship. An attribute cannot sit on a one-to-many relationship; it moves to the many side or becomes an associative entity.

WorkedTEST 1 A(a) · TEST 1 B(a) concept definitions

Q. Explain with an example: attribute; ternary relationship; entity; multivalued attribute; associative entity; binary relationship. (Combined from Test 1 A and B.)

Attribute: a property or characteristic of an entity type of interest to the organisation (a PATIENT's Patient Name). Entity: a person, place, object, event, or concept about which the organisation keeps data (PATIENT). Ternary relationship: a simultaneous relationship among the instances of three entity types (CHEMIST uses EQUIPMENT on a PROJECT). Binary relationship: a relationship between the instances of two entity types, the most common kind (STUDENT enrols in COURSE). Multivalued attribute: an attribute that may take more than one value for one instance ({skills}). Associative entity: an entity type that associates instances of other entity types and holds attributes peculiar to that relationship (ENROLMENT, holding grade).

1.6 Cardinality: the rule on the line

The most important marks on the diagram are the cardinality constraints: how many instances of one entity may, or must, relate to one instance of the other. Two numbers per end:

Must memoriseMinimum and maximum cardinality

Minimum cardinality is participation. If it is zero, participation is optional (drawn with a circle). If it is one, participation is mandatory (drawn with a bar).

Maximum cardinality is one (a bar) or many (a crow's foot).

So each end carries two marks, read outer-then-inner: circle-then-crow's-foot is "zero or many", bar-then-crow's-foot is "one or many", bar-then-bar is "exactly one".

With the notation complete, the rest of this unit is drawing. Every scenario below is a real exam question; draw each yourself before reading the answer.

1.7 Worked: ConsultCo

Exam questionPQ 20/21 Q1c · PQ 19/20 Q2b · PQ 15/16 Q2b

Use the following business rules to create the ERD for a consulting company named ConsultCo, showing all entities, relationships, optionalities and cardinalities. A client can contract many projects, but each project is contracted by one client. Some clients have not yet contracted any project. A project may have many employees assigned, and each employee may be assigned to many projects; some employees are assigned to none. One employee leads each project, and an employee may lead more than one. One employee coordinates each project, and may coordinate more than one. Some employees never lead or coordinate. Employees have many job skills, and each job description fits many employees; some job descriptions are unfilled and some employees have no job classification.

Answer

Four entities: CLIENT, PROJECT, EMPLOYEE, JOB. Read each rule into a line with its two cardinalities.

The trap here is drawing one relationship between EMPLOYEE and PROJECT. There are three (assigned, leads, coordinates), and each is a separate line because each is a different business rule.

1.8 Worked: hospital (physician, patient, ward)

Exam questionPQ 24/25 Q2c · TEST 1 A(b)

A hospital has many PHYSICIANs (Physician ID, Specialty) and many PATIENTs (Patient ID, Patient Name). 1.12 Reading a diagram you are given

Some questions hand you a diagram and ask you to read the rules back out of it, the reverse skill. The course text's cellular-operator activity is the model: for each question, name the symbol that decides your answer.

WorkedTEXT M2U1-ACT5 read the cellular-operator ERD

Given an ERD of CUSTOMER, PLAN, HANDSET, MANUFACTURER, OPERATING SYSTEM, you answer by pointing at a cardinality mark:

  • Can a customer have unlimited plans? Look at the maximum cardinality at the PLAN end of customer-plan. If it is a crow's foot, yes; if a bar, no.
  • Can a customer exist without a plan? Look at the minimum at the PLAN end. A circle (zero) means yes; a bar (one) means no.
  • Can a plan exist without a known customer? Look at the minimum at the CUSTOMER end. Circle means yes.
  • Can a handset be kept without a plan? Minimum at the PLAN end of handset-plan: circle means yes.
  • Can one operating system run on many handset types? Maximum at the HANDSET end of OS-handset: a crow's foot means yes.

The method is always the same: the answer to "can/must it..." is a single cardinality mark, and a full-mark answer names which mark and which end.

1.13 The course text's own questions

WorkedTEXT M2U1 self-assessment and assignments

Self-assessment

Is there a difference between data modelling and conceptual modelling? They are closely related: data modelling is the whole activity of documenting an organisation's data rules; conceptual modelling is the first, high-level stage of it that produces the ER diagram, before any logical or physical detail. So conceptual modelling is a part of data modelling, not a separate thing.

What determines how data are handled? The business rules: the organisation's policies and procedures, captured as entities, relationships, and constraints.

Relationship instance vs associative entity. Both link entity instances. A relationship instance is a single occurrence of a relationship (this student in this course). An associative entity is a relationship promoted to an entity because it has its own attributes or its own identifier, and can itself take part in relationships.

Assignments (book/author, and TEACHER)

Book and publisher (ACT1): BOOK (ISBN, title, price, pubDate) is published by one PUBLISHER (pubID, name); a publisher publishes many books. One-to-many. Book and author (ACT2): BOOK to AUTHOR is many-to-many (a book has one or many authors; an author has zero or many books, allowing prospective authors). Royalties (ACT3): the many-to-many carries royaltyPercent, isLeadAuthor, and sequencePosition, so it becomes an associative entity.

TEACHER identifier (TMA4): Trainer and Training Count show a teacher organises many workshops, so a WORKSHOP is a separate entity (or a multivalued attribute), and the identifier for TEACHER is Email (unique and stable), not Name (not guaranteed unique).

Module Two · Unit 2

The Enhanced E-R Model

When some things are special kinds of other things, an employee who is either hourly or salaried, an offering that is either a product or a service, the plain ER model is not enough. The enhanced ER model adds one construct, the supertype and its subtypes, and four rules that go with it. Those rules are examined every single year.

By the end of this unit you can: tell a supertype from a subtype; distinguish generalization from specialization; apply the completeness (total/partial) and disjointness (disjoint/overlap) constraints; use a subtype discriminator; and draw any supertype/subtype hierarchy the paper asks for.

2.1 Supertypes and subtypes

Must memoriseThe two words

A subtype is a meaningful subgrouping of the entities in an entity type, for example GRADUATE and UNDERGRADUATE within STUDENT. A supertype is the generic entity type that holds what the subgroups have in common, for example STUDENT. The relationship is drawn with a circle between the supertype and its subtypes, and the subtypes connect to it with the subset symbol.

You use a supertype/subtype structure when either (a) some attributes apply only to some instances (only salaried employees have an annual salary), or (b) some instances take part in a relationship that others do not (only consultants relate to a CONTRACT).

Must memoriseAttribute inheritance

A subtype inherits every attribute and every relationship of its supertype. Attributes are placed at the highest level to which they apply: SSN at PERSON, dateHired at EMPLOYEE, rank at FACULTY. So a FACULTY instance has SSN, Name (from PERSON), dateHired, salary (from EMPLOYEE), and rank (from FACULTY). This is why the structure avoids redundancy: the common attributes are stored once, at the top.

2.2 Generalization and specialization

WorkedPQ 24/25 Q4a · PQ 23/24 Q4a

Q. Differentiate: (i) supertype and subtype; (ii) generalization and specialization.

Supertype vs subtype: a supertype is the general entity type holding common attributes; a subtype is a specialised subgroup of it holding its own extra attributes and inheriting the supertype's. VEHICLE is a supertype; CAR and TRUCK are subtypes.

Generalization vs specialization: both build the same hierarchy but from opposite ends. Generalization is bottom-up: you start with specialised entity types (CAR, TRUCK) and define a general supertype (VEHICLE) from what they share. Specialization is top-down: you start with a general entity type (VEHICLE) and define subtypes (CAR, TRUCK) for the parts that differ.

2.3 The two constraints you are always asked for

Every EER question turns on two independent choices. Get them both and you have the diagram.

Must memoriseCompleteness and disjointness

Completeness (does every supertype instance have to be a subtype?)

  • Total specialization: yes, every supertype instance must belong to some subtype. Drawn with a double line from the supertype to the circle.
  • Partial specialization: no, a supertype instance need not belong to any subtype. Drawn with a single line.

Disjointness (can an instance be more than one subtype at once?)

  • Disjoint rule (d): no, an instance is at most one subtype. Circle marked d.
  • Overlap rule (o): yes, an instance may be several subtypes at once. Circle marked o.
TeachSubtype discriminator

One more mark the paper asks for by name. The subtype discriminator (also called the discriminating attribute) is a supertype attribute whose value says which subtype a row belongs to. It is written beside the specialization circle. For a disjoint specialization it holds one value per row (for EMPLOYEE over SALARIED and HOURLY, a discriminator empType reads "S" or "H"). For an overlap specialization one value is not enough, so the discriminator is a set of yes/no flags, one per subtype.

WorkedPQ 24/25 Q6b · PQ 23/24 Q2a definitions

Q. Explain: total vs partial specialization; disjoint rule vs overlap rule; entity type; required attribute.

Total specialization: every instance of the supertype must be a member of at least one subtype. Partial specialization: a supertype instance need not be a member of any subtype. Disjoint rule: a supertype instance that is in one subtype cannot at the same time be in another. Overlap rule: a supertype instance may be a member of two or more subtypes at the same time. Entity type: a collection of entities sharing common properties, described once (EMPLOYEE). Required attribute: an attribute that must have a value for every instance (an employee's empID).

2.6 Worked: technology-company offerings

Exam questionPQ 25/26 Q5b

A company provides OFFERINGs (offering ID, description). An offering is either a PRODUCT (product name, standard price, first-release date) or a SERVICE (responsible unit, conditions). Services are of types REPAIR (cost; repairs some product) and MAINTENANCE (hourly rate), among others. Build the EER for the offering hierarchy.

Answer

An offering is exactly one of product or service: total (every offering is one) and disjoint (not both). SERVICE then specializes into REPAIR, MAINTENANCE, and others, so that inner split is partial (the "and other types" leaves room) and disjoint.

WorkedTEXT M2U2 self-assessment and assignments

The two most important constraints in supertype/subtype relationships? Completeness (total or partial) and disjointness (disjoint or overlap).

What does partial specialization specify, and how does it differ from total? Partial says a supertype instance need not belong to any subtype; total says every supertype instance must belong to at least one subtype. Partial allows "none of the above"; total forbids it.

The mental models for developing supertype/subtype relationships? Generalization (bottom-up) and specialization (top-down).

When is each used, and what is attribute inheritance? (TMA) Use a subtype when some attributes or relationships apply only to some instances. Attribute inheritance means each subtype automatically has all attributes and relationships of its supertype, so shared data is stored once at the top.
