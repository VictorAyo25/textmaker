Question One

Answer2021/2022 Q1 · 23.5 marks

a. List the members of five sets (5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 2.1. Set-builder into roster form, one mark each.

(i) {x | x is a real number such that x2 = 1}. x = 1 or x = −1, and both are real: {1, −1}.

(ii) {x | x is a positive prime number less than 20}. A prime has exactly two positive divisors, so 1 is not prime: {2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19}, eight members.

(iii) {x | x is a perfect square and x < 100}. State the reading. As printed the condition does not say x is a positive integer, so strictly it admits 0 as well. Taking x to be a whole number: {0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81}, ten members; omit the 0 if only positive integers are intended, giving nine. Say which you used and the mark is safe either way.

(iv) {x | x is an integer such that x2 = 2}. The only solutions are √2 and −√2, and neither is an integer, as section 9.1 proves. So the set is ∅, the empty set, with 0 members.

(v) {x | x is an integer such that 2x = x}. Subtracting x from both sides gives x = 0. So {0}, a singleton with one member.

Parts iv and v are the discriminating ones. One is empty and the other is a singleton containing zero; writing ∅ for both, or {0} for both, is the error the pair is there to catch. Section 2.1 covers exactly that distinction.

b. The directed graph, in tabular form (6½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.4.

State the reading first. This figure is drawn small with soft arrowheads, and at the resolution we hold the arc count and their directions are not reliably readable. What follows is the method, which is what the marks are for; read the arrowheads off your own copy and fill the numbers in.

The method, in four steps.

  1. List the vertices and count them. Here a, b, c, d and the isolated e, so five. An isolated vertex is still a vertex, and it is usually why it was drawn.
  2. List the arcs as bundles, one row per ordered pair, with how many arcs run that way. Curved parallel arcs are separate arcs.
  3. Tabulate each vertex with its out-degree and its in-degree, naming where each goes and comes from.
  4. Total both columns. Each must equal the arc count, since every arc leaves exactly one vertex and arrives at exactly one. If they do not agree, an arrowhead has been misread.

The layout to reproduce:

The equal totals are the whole point. Section 6.4 works the same tabulation on the 2023/2024 digraph, where the reading is firmer, and there both totals come to 13.

c. Types of undirected graph, with diagrams (12 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.2, where the full catalogue is tabled. Twelve marks, the largest single part on the paper, and the most repeated question on the course.

Name, define and draw each. Simple, multigraph, pseudograph, null, trivial, complete Kn, regular, cycle, path, wheel, bipartite, complete bipartite Km,n, connected, weighted, subgraph. Fifteen types is more than twelve marks needs; give ten with diagrams rather than fifteen without.

The three distinctions the marks turn on. A simple graph forbids loops and repeated edges; a multigraph permits repeated edges; a pseudograph permits loops as well. Getting those three apart, with a sketch each, is worth more than listing the other twelve names.

Answer2021/2022 Q2 · 23.5 marks

a. Labelling the chairs of an auditorium (6 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 5.1. Each label is an uppercase English letter followed by a positive integer not exceeding 100. Both parts must be chosen, so this is the product rule.

letters: 26     integers 1 to 100: 100
labels = 26 × 100 = 2600

Two things to state. "Not exceeding 100" and "positive" together give the integers 1 to 100, which is 100 values and not 99 or 101. And each label names one chair, so 2600 chairs can be labelled differently, which is what "the largest number" asks for.

b. Simple communication links between four buildings (7½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.2. The question names four buildings. A simple link joins two of them, and a link from X to Y is the same link as from Y to X, so this is a combination, not a permutation:

links = C(4, 2) = 4 × 3 / 2 = 6

The diagram is K4: four dots, each joined to the other three, drawn as a square with both diagonals. Label the four dots with the four buildings and count the lines: six.

Watch the count of buildings. The 2020/2021 paper sets the same question with five buildings, where the answer is C(5, 2) = 10. Read the list and count it rather than remembering the number.

Question Three

Answer2021/2022 Q3 · 23.5 marks

a. The convocation invitation cards (10 marks, split 5 and 5)

Quoted as printed in section 7.2. Model it as a rooted tree: the first person is the root, each sender has exactly four children, and the 100 who read but did not send are the leaves. Nobody receives more than one card, which is what makes the structure a tree.

(i) How many have seen the card. For a full m-ary tree, l = (m − 1)i + 1 with m = 4 and l = 100:

100 = 3i + 1, so 3i = 99 and i = 33
n = mi + 1 = 4 × 33 + 1 = 133

133 people have seen the card, including the first.

(ii) How many sent it out. The senders are the internal vertices: 33.

Check: n = i + l = 33 + 100 = 133. The two routes agree.

This is the same problem as 2020/2021 Q3(b) and 2023/2024 Q5(d), told three ways. Learn it once.

b. Four real-life scenarios for graph models, two examples each (6 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.1. Say what the vertices are and what the edges are in each; that is the modelling and it is what the marks are for.

1. Transport networks. Vertices are places, edges are routes. Examples: a road map where edges carry distances, as in Q4(c) below; an airline route map where an edge means a direct flight.

2. Social networks. Vertices are people, edges are relationships. Examples: a friendship network, where the edge is undirected because friendship is mutual; a follower network, where it is directed because following is not.

3. Utility and supply networks. Vertices are junctions or substations, edges are pipes or cables. Examples: an electricity grid; a water distribution network, where edge weights are capacities.

4. Scheduling and dependency. Vertices are tasks, edges are "must come before". Examples: a project plan, where a cycle would mean an impossible schedule; a timetable conflict graph, where an edge means two exams cannot be at the same time.

c. Key terminologies of a rooted tree, with a diagram (7½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.1, where one labelled diagram carries every term at once.

Open with the definition. A rooted tree is a tree in which one vertex is designated the root, and every edge is then directed away from it, so every other vertex has exactly one path from the root.

The terms, each in a line: root, the designated vertex; parent, the vertex just above another; child, a vertex just below its parent; siblings, vertices sharing a parent; ancestors, everything on the path back to the root; descendants, everything below; leaf or terminal vertex, one with no children; internal vertex, one with at least one child; subtree, a vertex together with all its descendants; level, the distance from the root, which is 0 at the root itself; and height, the largest level in the tree.

Then the diagram, labelled. One tree with the root marked, one parent and child pair marked, a pair of siblings, a leaf, an internal vertex, and the levels numbered down the left edge. Marking the terms on a single tree is far stronger than eleven separate sketches.

Close with the two counting facts, which cost a line and show the definitions were understood: a tree on v vertices has exactly v − 1 edges, and there is exactly one path between any two vertices.

Question Four

Answer2021/2022 Q4 · 24 marks as printed

a. Briefly explain the Handshaking theorem (5½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.3.

Statement. In any undirected graph, the sum of the degrees of all the vertices is twice the number of edges: Σ deg(v) = 2|E|.

Why it is true. Count the pairs (vertex, edge-end-meeting-it). Counting by vertices gives Σ deg(v); counting by edges gives 2|E|, since every edge has exactly two ends. The same quantity counted two ways must agree.

Why it is called that. If everyone at a party shakes some hands, the total number of hands shaken across all people is twice the number of handshakes, because each handshake involves two people.

Its two consequences, which is where the extra marks are. The degree sum is always even, so no graph can have an odd degree sum. And the number of vertices of odd degree is always even.

An example. A triangle has three vertices of degree 2 and three edges: 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 = 2 × 3.

b. What a balanced m-ary tree means, with examples (8½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.2. Build the answer in three layers, because the question asks for one term whose definition needs two others.

1. m-ary. A rooted tree is m-ary when every internal vertex has at most m children. At m = 2 it is called binary, at m = 3 ternary, at m = 5 quinary.

2. Full. An m-ary tree is full when every internal vertex has exactly m children, none fewer.

3. Balanced. An m-ary tree of height h is balanced when every leaf is at level h or level h − 1. In words: the leaves are all at the bottom or one step above it, so no branch is much longer than another.

Examples, which the question demands. A binary tree with root, two children and four grandchildren, all four leaves at level 2, is balanced and full. Add two great-grandchildren under one leaf and it is no longer balanced, because there are now leaves at level 2 and level 3 while the height is 3. A tree that is one long chain of five vertices is m-ary for any m but as unbalanced as it can be.

Why balance matters, worth one line: a balanced tree of height h holds close to mh leaves, so searching it costs about h steps, whereas the chain costs as many steps as it has vertices. That is the whole reason the term exists.

c. The travelling missionary from Detroit (10 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.8, where all twelve tours are tabulated. Same five cities and same ten distances as the 2020/2021 and 2023/2024 papers.

Count the tours first. Fixing Detroit as start and end, the other four cities can be ordered in 4! = 24 ways, and each tour is identical to its own reverse, so there are 24 / 2 = 12 distinct tours.

The minimum is 458 miles:

Detroit, Saginaw, Grand Rapids, Kalamazoo, Toledo, Detroit
98 + 113 + 56 + 133 + 58 = 458

List all twelve to justify the word "minimum". Ten marks is buying the enumeration, not the arithmetic: a minimum is a claim about every alternative, so exhibiting one short tour proves only that 458 is achievable, never that nothing beats it.

Question Five

Answer2021/2022 Q5 · 23.5 marks

a. Four computing domains for graph models, one example each (6 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.1. Computing domains, not real-life scenarios; the two questions look alike and want different answers.

1. Networking and routing. Vertices are routers, edges are links. Example: a shortest-path algorithm choosing a route through the network.

2. Compilers. Vertices are program points, edges are possible jumps. Example: a control-flow graph, used to find unreachable code.

3. Databases and the web. Vertices are pages or records, edges are links or foreign keys. Example: ranking pages by how many others link to them, which is a question about in-degree.

4. Operating systems. Vertices are processes and resources, edges are "holds" and "waits for". Example: deadlock detection, which is the search for a cycle in that graph.

b. The size of the discrete structures class (4 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 5.3.

Note the printing first. The paper prints "There of 25 students" where it means "There are". More importantly the sentence can be read two ways: 25 as the number majoring in Computer Science, or 25 as the class total. Only the first makes the question answerable, and that is the reading taken here. Say so in one line.

The computation. Every student majors in one or the other or both, so the class is the union:

n(C ∪ M) = n(C) + n(M) − n(C ∩ M)
= 25 + 13 − 8
= 30 students

Check with the three regions: Computer Science only is 25 − 8 = 17, Maths only is 13 − 8 = 5, both is 8, and 17 + 5 + 8 = 30. Drawing that as two overlapping circles is worth doing.

c. When is a vertex incident to an edge? (3½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.1.

Definition. A vertex v is incident to an edge e when v is one of the two endpoints of e. Equivalently, e is incident to v. Incidence relates a vertex to an edge; adjacency relates a vertex to another vertex, and the two words are not interchangeable.

Example. In a triangle on vertices 1, 2, 3 with edges e1 = {1, 2}, e2 = {2, 3}, e3 = {1, 3}, vertex 2 is incident to e1 and e2 and not to e3. Vertices 1 and 2 are adjacent, because an edge joins them.

The link to degree, worth a line: the degree of a vertex is the number of edges incident to it, counting a loop twice because both its ends meet the same vertex.
