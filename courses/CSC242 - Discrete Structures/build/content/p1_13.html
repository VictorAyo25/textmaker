Logical Equivalence and the Laws

Two statements that always agree can be swapped freely. That is what lets you simplify a condition, redesign a circuit, or rewrite a database query without changing what it does.

By the end of this section you will be able to test two propositions for equivalence with a truth table, state De Morgan's laws properly as laws, and use the standard laws to transform one expression into another.

What equivalence means

Must memoriseDefinition

Two compound propositions are logically equivalent when they have the same truth value for every possible assignment of truth values to their variables. We write P ≡ Q.

Equivalently: P ≡ Q exactly when the biconditional P ↔ Q is a tautology.

Memory hook: ≡ is the equals sign with an extra bar, meaning "equal in every row, not just this one". It is a claim about two propositions, so it is not itself a connective.

TeachHow to show two things are equivalent

There are two accepted methods, and the question usually tells you which it wants.

Method 1: truth table. Build a column for each side and check they match row for row. Always works, never elegant, and cannot fail through a wrong step. Use it when the question says "show that" or "construct a truth table".

Method 2: a chain of known laws. Rewrite one side step by step until it becomes the other, naming the law at every step. Shorter when it works, and it is what "using logical equivalences" means when a paper says so, as 2024/2025 Q3(c) does.

The naming is not decoration. In a law-chain proof the marks are for the justifications, so a chain of correct lines with no names attached scores poorly.

De Morgan's laws

De Morgan's laws

The claim. Negating a conjunction turns it into the disjunction of the negations, and negating a disjunction turns it into the conjunction of the negations. In words: not (both) is the same as (not one) or (not the other), and not (either) is the same as (not one) and (not the other).

In symbols.
¬(p ∧ q) ≡ ¬p ∨ ¬q
¬(p ∨ q) ≡ ¬p ∧ ¬q

Conditions. None beyond p and q being propositions. The laws hold universally, which is what makes them safe to apply anywhere.

The limiting case, and why the wrong version survives. The common error is to write ¬(p ∧ q) as ¬p ∧ ¬q, keeping the connective instead of flipping it. Those two agree in exactly the rows where p and q have the same truth value, and differ in the other two. So a student who checks only the TT and FF rows sees the wrong law confirmed. Check a row where p and q disagree, and the error appears at once.

The same laws for sets. Replacing "and" by intersection, "or" by union and "not" by complement gives the set forms, which Part Two uses:
the complement of (A ∩ B) = (complement of A) ∪ (complement of B)
the complement of (A ∪ B) = (complement of A) ∩ (complement of B)

Consequence. A negation can always be pushed inwards until it sits on single variables. That is why every Boolean expression can be simplified mechanically, and why a compiler can rewrite your if conditions.

TeachWhy it is obviously true

Say it about breakfast. "It is not the case that I had both tea and toast" means "I did not have tea, or I did not have toast" (at least one of them was missing). It does not mean I had neither.

"It is not the case that I had either tea or toast" is the stronger one: it means I had no tea and no toast.

So negation flips the connective as it moves inside. Forgetting to flip it is the mistake De Morgan exists to prevent.

Here T means a tautology (always true) and F a contradiction (always false).

Identityp ∧ T ≡ p     p ∨ F ≡ p

Negationp ∨ ¬p ≡ T     p ∧ ¬p ≡ F
Implicationp → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q
Contrapositivep → q ≡ ¬q → ¬p
Biconditionalp ↔ q ≡ (p → q) ∧ (q → p)

Memory hook: the one that unlocks most problems is implication: p → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q. An arrow is hard to manipulate; an "or" is easy. Turn every arrow into an or as your first move, and most chains fall out.

A real question

As printed2020/2021 · Q4(c)

(c) Show that the following are logically equivalent

(i) (p → q) ∧ (p → r) and p → (q ∧ r). (3marks)

(ii) p ↔ q and (p ∧ q) ∨ (¬p ∧ ¬q) (3marks)

Break it down
Given
Two pairs of compound propositions, the first on three letters and the second on two.
Asked
Show that each pair is equivalent. A truth table is a complete answer, and is safer than a law chain under time pressure.
Marks say
3 marks each. Part (i) has three letters so its table is 8 rows; part (ii) has two letters and 4 rows. Both are quick if the columns are set out before you start filling.
Teaches
This section for equivalence, 1.1 for building the table.
Trap
Part (i) needs 8 rows, and the temptation is to do 4. Count the distinct letters, not the brackets.

Sanity check in words: promising q and promising r separately, on the same condition p, is the same as promising both together on that condition. It should feel obvious, and the table is why it is certain.

In words: "p and q agree" is the same as "either both are true, or both are false". That is exactly what the biconditional means, so the right-hand side is really the definition written out.

Now redo it: on blank paper, show that p → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q with a truth table. It is the law you will lean on most.

TrapEquivalent is not "similar"

Equivalence needs agreement in every row. Two propositions that match in three rows out of four are not "nearly equivalent", they are simply different, and the one row where they differ is the counterexample you should quote.

When a question asks you to show two things are not equivalent, do not build the whole table and say "they differ". Name the row: "take p true and q false; then the first is F and the second is T". One row, fully specified, is a complete disproof.
