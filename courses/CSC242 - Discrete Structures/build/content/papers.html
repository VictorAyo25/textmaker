Question One

Answer2025/2026 Q1 · 17.5 marks

a. Five differences, with two examples each (10 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.1. Each pair needs a contrasting sentence, not two definitions: see F.2.

i. Degree versus valency of a node. They are two names for the same quantity, the number of edge ends meeting at a vertex, whereas "valency" is the older term borrowed from chemistry and "degree" is the one this syllabus uses elsewhere. Examples: in a triangle every vertex has degree 2 and equally valency 2; a pendant vertex has degree 1 and valency 1.

ii. Predicate versus propositional logic. A proposition is either true or false outright, whereas a predicate has a variable in it and only becomes true or false once the variable is given a value. Examples: "3 is prime" is a proposition; "x is prime" is a predicate P(x), true at x = 3 and false at x = 4.

iii. Universal versus existential quantifier. ∀x claims the predicate holds for every value in the domain, whereas ∃x claims it holds for at least one. Examples: over the integers ∀x(x2 ≥ 0) is true; ∃x(x2 = 4) is true, and ∀x(x2 = 4) is false.

iv. Adjacency versus path matrix. The adjacency matrix records whether two vertices are joined by a single edge, whereas the path matrix records whether one can be reached from the other by any sequence of edges. Examples: in the path a to b to c, the adjacency matrix has 0 in the (a, c) position but the path matrix has 1; in a complete graph the two matrices agree everywhere off the diagonal.

v. Logical matrix versus relation matrix. A logical matrix is any matrix whose entries are the truth values 0 and 1, whereas a relation matrix is a logical matrix used specifically to record a relation, with mij = 1 exactly when (i, j) is in the relation. Examples: an adjacency matrix is a logical matrix; the matrix of "divides" on {1, 2, 3, 4} is a relation matrix.

b. i. Nine types of set (3 marks) ii. Two ways to describe a set (2 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 2.1.

i. Empty {}, singleton {5}, finite {1, 2, 3}, infinite ℕ, equal ({1,2} and {2,1}), equivalent ({1,2} and {a,b}), subset ({1} of {1,2}), universal (U, everything under discussion), and power set (all subsets of a set).

ii. Roster form, listing the members between braces, and set-builder form, stating the property they share: {x | x is a positive prime below 10}.

c. A = {11, 12, 13, 14}, B = {13, 14, 15, 16} (2.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 2.2.

A ∩ B = {13, 14}
A − B = {11, 12}
B − A = {15, 16}

Each is checked by reading A once and asking of each member whether it is in B.

a. Five justifications for the subject (5 marks)

Quoted as printed in Foundations F.1. One mark each, so five distinct points with an application named in each.

1. It is the mathematics of the computer itself, which stores and processes separated values rather than a continuum. 2. Logic is the basis of circuit design and of program correctness, as Part Eight shows for gates and Part One for conditions. 3. Graphs and trees are the standard data models for networks, file systems, compilers and routing. 4. Counting gives complexity and capacity analysis: how many passwords, how many states, how many operations. 5. Proof and induction justify algorithms, which is how one shows a program is right for every input rather than for the ones tested.

c. Is R reflexive? (2 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 3.3. R = {(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (4,4), (1,2), (2,3)} on {1, 2, 3, 4}.

Yes. Reflexivity requires (a, a) for every a in the set. All four loops (1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (4,4) are present, so R is reflexive. The extra pairs (1,2) and (2,3) do not affect reflexivity.

d. Is R an equivalence relation? (4 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 3.3. Three properties, each checked explicitly, which is what "detailed explanation" is asking for.

Reflexive: (1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (4,4) are all present. Yes.
Symmetric: the non-loop pairs are (1,3) and (3,1), (2,4) and (4,2). Each appears with its reverse. Yes.
Transitive: (1,3) with (3,1) needs (1,1), present; (3,1) with (1,3) needs (3,3), present; (2,4) with (4,2) needs (2,2), present; (4,2) with (2,4) needs (4,4), present. No other composable pair exists. Yes.

So R is an equivalence relation, and it partitions {1, 2, 3, 4} into the classes {1, 3} and {2, 4}. Naming the classes is the line that shows you know what an equivalence relation is for.

e. Partially versus totally ordered, and the CGPA table (4.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 3.4.

The difference. In a partially ordered set some pairs may be incomparable, whereas in a totally ordered set every two elements are comparable. Both orders are reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive; total order adds comparability.

The table. A has 4.80, B has 4.50, C has 4.10. Order them by "has a CGPA at least as high as". Every pair can be compared, because any two real numbers can: 4.80 ≥ 4.50 ≥ 4.10.

Proof that it is a total order. Reflexive, since each student's CGPA is at least as high as itself. Antisymmetric, since if two are each at least as high as the other their CGPAs are equal and, these three being distinct, the students are the same. Transitive, by the transitivity of ≥ on the reals. Comparable, since ≥ compares any two reals. Therefore totally ordered, and the chain is A, then B, then C.

c. Ten male and ten female praying in pairs (5.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 5.2.

(i) Sessions. Each session pairs one male with one female and every person prays once per round, so ten pairs form at a time and there are 10 sessions if each male is to pray with each female.

(ii) Prayer units. Every male paired with every female is 10 × 10 = 100 distinct units, by the product rule.

(iii) Choice of prayer partners. Fix the order of the males and assign the females to them; that is an arrangement of ten distinct people in ten positions:

10! = 3628800

Question Four

Answer2025/2026 Q4 · 17.5 marks

a. Short notes with examples, five items (10 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.1. Two marks each, so each item wants a definition and an example.

i. Size of a graph. The number of edges, written |E|. In the complete graph K4 the size is 6; in a tree on 10 vertices the size is 9.

ii. Valency of a vertex. The number of edge ends meeting at it, the same quantity as its degree. A pendant vertex has valency 1; every vertex of K5 has valency 4.

iii. In-degree and out-degree. In a directed graph the in-degree of v is how many arrows arrive at v and the out-degree how many leave. If a to b and c to b are the only arcs, then b has in-degree 2 and out-degree 0, while a has out-degree 1 and in-degree 0.

iv. Graph isomorphism. Two graphs are isomorphic when there is a one-to-one correspondence between their vertices that preserves adjacency: the same graph drawn twice. A square drawn as a square and the same four vertices drawn as a diamond are isomorphic; K3 and a path on three vertices are not, since they have different numbers of edges.

v. Eulerian graph. A graph containing a circuit that uses every edge exactly once and returns to its start, which happens exactly when the graph is connected and every vertex has even degree. A triangle is Eulerian; the Konigsberg graph, with four odd-degree vertices, is not.

b. The place of logic in computer science (2 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 1.5. Logic supplies the rules that separate a valid argument from an invalid one, and computing rests on it at every level: circuit design builds directly from AND, OR and NOT (Part Eight); program conditions and loop tests are propositions; database queries are predicate logic; and proving a program correct is proving an implication.

c. Three acceptable logic statements leading to a conclusion (3 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 1.5. A valid argument, laid out as premises and conclusion:

If a program compiles, it has no syntax error.
If it has no syntax error, the parser accepted it.
This program compiles.
∴ The parser accepted it.

Naming the rules earns the marks: the first two premises chain by hypothetical syllogism to give "if it compiles, the parser accepted it", and with the third premise modus ponens gives the conclusion.

d. Arrangements of the letters of SCHOOL (2.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 5.2. Six letters with O repeated twice:

6! / 2! = 720 / 2 = 360

Why the division. The two O's are identical, so each distinct arrangement has been counted 2! = 2 times by 6!, once for each way of ordering them.

a. ii. The alarm (3 marks)

Let F and S be the two sensors and L the alarm. "Either or" here is the inclusive or, since the alarm must certainly sound if both fire:

L = F + S

With only the smoke sensor active, F = 0 and S = 1, so L = 0 + 1 = 1: the alarm sounds. Had exclusive or been used the alarm would fall silent when both sensors detected danger, which is the sentence that secures the mark.

b. i. Kuratowski's theorem (2 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.7. A graph is planar if and only if it contains no subgraph that is a subdivision of K5 or of K3,3. In words: those two graphs are the only obstructions to planarity, and any non-planar graph has a copy of one of them hidden inside it.

b. ii. Why K5 and K3,3 matter (2 marks)

Because by Kuratowski's theorem they are the minimal non-planar graphs: every non-planar graph contains a subdivision of one of them, so the whole question of planarity reduces to looking for these two. They are the reference points against which any drawing is tested.

b. iii. Showing both are non-planar (6 marks)

K5. It has v = 5 vertices and, being complete, e = C(5, 2) = 10 edges. For a simple connected planar graph with v ≥ 3, Euler's formula forces

e ≤ 3v − 6 = 3 × 5 − 6 = 9

but 10 > 9, so the bound fails and K5 is not planar.

K3,3. It has v = 6 and e = 3 × 3 = 9. It is bipartite, so it has no triangle, and the triangle-free bound applies:

e ≤ 2v − 4 = 2 × 6 − 4 = 8

but 9 > 8, so K3,3 is not planar.

Say why the second bound is different. Using e ≤ 3v − 6 on K3,3 gives 12, which 9 satisfies, and the argument would prove nothing. The tighter bound is available precisely because a bipartite graph has no odd cycle and therefore no triangle. That sentence is worth a mark on its own.

Question Six

Answer2025/2026 Q6 · 17.5 marks

a. i. The adjacency matrix, explained (2.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.5. For a graph on n vertices labelled 1 to n, the adjacency matrix is the n by n array whose entry aij is 1 when vertices i and j are joined by an edge and 0 when they are not. For an undirected graph it is symmetric about the main diagonal, since an edge joining i to j equally joins j to i, and the diagonal is 0 unless the graph has loops.

a. ii. Memory management in an adjacency matrix (4 marks)

The cost. The matrix always occupies n2 entries, whatever the number of edges, because every pair has a slot whether or not it is used.

When that is wasteful. A sparse graph, where the edges are far fewer than n2, stores overwhelmingly zeros. A road network with a thousand junctions and three thousand roads still needs a million entries.

Two ways it is managed. Store only half of it, since symmetry makes the upper triangle sufficient for an undirected graph, roughly halving the space. Or store one bit per entry rather than one integer, since every entry is 0 or 1.

The alternative. An adjacency list keeps, for each vertex, only the vertices actually adjacent to it, so it uses space in proportion to the edges rather than to n2. The trade is speed: the matrix answers "are i and j adjacent" in one lookup, the list has to search.

b. ATM PIN codes of 4 to 6 digits (5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 5.1. Ten digits, repeats allowed, so a code of length k can be formed in 10k ways. The three lengths are alternatives, so add them:

104 + 105 + 106 = 10000 + 100000 + 1000000 = 1110000

Both rules appear here. The product rule gives each 10k, one factor of 10 per position; the sum rule joins the three lengths, because a code has exactly one length.

c. i. Three-digit natural numbers (1 mark)

Quoted as printed in section 5.1. The first digit cannot be 0, so it has 9 choices; the other two have 10 each:

9 × 10 × 10 = 900

Check: the three-digit numbers run from 100 to 999, and 999 − 100 + 1 = 900.

c. ii. The parts of the task, and their type (4 marks)

The task of forming a three-digit number has three parts, choosing the hundreds digit, the tens digit and the units digit. They are compulsory and not alternative: every one must be performed to produce a number, which is exactly the condition the product rule requires. Had they been alternatives, only one would be carried out and the sum rule would apply, as it does across the three PIN lengths in part (b) above.

c. iii. Which rule applies (1 mark)

The product rule, since the three digit choices are performed one after another and every combination of them gives a different number.
