Traversals and Expression Trees

Three ways to walk a tree, and each one writes an arithmetic expression in a different notation. The 2024/2025 paper gives 6 marks to the walks and 6 more to the notations.

By the end of this section you will be able to traverse an ordered rooted tree in preorder, inorder and postorder, build the expression tree of an arithmetic expression, read prefix and postfix notation off that tree, and answer both traversal questions on the 2024/2025 paper in full.

The three traversals

Must memorisePreorder, inorder, postorder

Let T be an ordered rooted tree with root r and subtrees T1, T2, …, Tk hanging from r, taken left to right. Each traversal is defined by where the root goes.

Preorder: visit r first, then traverse T1, …, Tk in preorder.

Inorder: traverse T1 in inorder, then visit r, then traverse T2, …, Tk in inorder.

Postorder: traverse T1, …, Tk in postorder, then visit r last.

Where ordered means the children of every vertex have a fixed left-to-right order. Without it none of the three is defined, which is why section 7.1 named ordered rooted trees separately. T1 is the leftmost subtree, and inorder treats it differently from all the others. a single vertex is its own traversal in all three orders: the recursion stops there.

The names say it. "Pre", "in" and "post" describe where the root is visited relative to its subtrees: before them, in among them, or after them. Nothing else differs.

Memory hook: the prefix tells you when the root is visited. Pre-order: root first. Post-order: root last. In-order: root in the middle, after the leftmost subtree only.

TeachHow to do it without losing your place

The reliable method by hand is the tracing method. Draw a curve that starts at the root, hugs the outside of the whole tree anticlockwise, and returns to the root. Every vertex is passed three times: on its left, underneath it, and on its right.

  • Preorder: list each vertex the first time the curve passes it, on the left.
  • Inorder: list each vertex the second time, from below.
  • Postorder: list each vertex the third time, on the right.

One curve gives all three lists, which matters when a question asks for all three of the same tree, as 2024/2025 Q6(a) does.

The check that catches nearly every slip: each list must contain every vertex exactly once, so its length equals the number of vertices. In preorder the root comes first; in postorder the root comes last. If either of those fails, stop and redo it.

Given
The 17-vertex ordered rooted tree drawn below, read off the paper's figure.
Find
The preorder, inorder and postorder lists.
Rule
Preorder visits the root first, inorder after the leftmost subtree, postorder last.

i. Preorder

Root first, then each subtree in turn. Take a, then everything under b, then everything under c.

under b: b, then d, then e's subtree, then f
e's subtree: e, then j, then k's subtree, which is k, n, o
so under b: b, d, e, j, k, n, o, f
under c: c, g, then h's subtree, then i
h's subtree: h, l, then m's subtree, which is m, p, q
so under c: c, g, h, l, m, p, q, i

a, b, d, e, j, k, n, o, f, c, g, h, l, m, p, q, i

ii. Inorder

Leftmost subtree, then the root, then the rest. At a: everything under b, then a, then everything under c.

at b, children d, e, f: inorder(d), b, inorder(e), inorder(f)
at e, children j, k: j, e, inorder(k); at k, children n, o: n, k, o
so inorder(e) = j, e, n, k, o, and under b: d, b, j, e, n, k, o, f
at c, children g, h, i: g, c, inorder(h), i
at h, children l, m: l, h, inorder(m); at m: p, m, q
so inorder(h) = l, h, p, m, q, and under c: g, c, l, h, p, m, q, i

d, b, j, e, n, k, o, f, a, g, c, l, h, p, m, q, i

iii. Postorder

All the subtrees first, root last.

under b: d, then postorder(e), then f, then b
postorder(e) = j, then postorder(k) = n, o, k, then e, giving j, n, o, k, e
so under b: d, j, n, o, k, e, f, b
under c: g, then postorder(h), then i, then c
postorder(h) = l, then p, q, m, then h, giving l, p, q, m, h
so under c: g, l, p, q, m, h, i, c

d, j, n, o, k, e, f, b, g, l, p, q, m, h, i, c, a

Check all three. Each list has 17 entries and each contains every vertex exactly once. Preorder starts with a; postorder ends with a; inorder has a in the middle, right after the whole of b's subtree. All four conditions hold.

Now redo it: traverse just the subtree at k, whose children are n and o. Preorder k, n, o. Inorder n, k, o. Postorder n, o, k.

Expression trees

Must memoriseExpression tree, prefix and postfix

An expression tree represents an arithmetic expression: each leaf is a number, and each internal vertex is an operator whose children are the things it acts on, in order.

Traversing that tree gives the three standard notations:

TraversalNotationAlso calledOperator sits
preorderprefix notationPolish notationbefore its operands
inorderinfix notationordinary algebrabetween its operands
postorderpostfix notationreverse Polishafter its operands
Where the tree records the grouping, so the brackets of the original expression are built into its shape. prefix and postfix need no brackets at all and are never ambiguous, which is exactly why compilers and calculators use them. infix is the notation people write, and it is the only one of the three that needs brackets and precedence rules.

Building the tree: find the operator that is applied last, which is the one least tightly bound. That becomes the root. Its two sides become its subtrees, and the same rule is applied to each of them.

Memory hook: the root is the last operation performed. Work out what you would compute last, put it at the top, and recurse.

The expression question

The question
2024/2025 Q6(b) is quoted in full in section 7.2, where its parts vi and vii are answered. Its parts i, iv and v are answered here: draw the rooted tree of the expression, then write it in prefix and in postfix notation, 2 marks each.

The expression carries two characters that could not be read. As recorded in 7.2, a single superscript follows each of the two closing brackets and neither is legible at any resolution available, including in the PDF's own text layer. They are not guessed. The answer below solves the plain reading, in which those characters are absent, and then says exactly what changes if they turn out to be exponents.
Break it down
Given

Asked

Marks say
2 each. Parts iv and v are read straight off the tree from part i, so the tree is worth getting right first: one drawing earns all 6 marks.
Teaches
This section, 7.3.
Trap
Putting the wrong operator at the root. Inside the first bracket, multiplication binds tighter than subtraction, so the subtraction is done last and is the root of that subtree, not the multiplication. Getting the root wrong makes all three answers wrong together.
Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q6(b) i, iv and v
Given
(3 − 4 · 2) − (5 + (5 − 2)), the plain reading of the printed expression.
Find
The expression tree, then the prefix and postfix forms.
Rule
The last operation performed is the root. Preorder gives prefix, postorder gives postfix.

i. The rooted tree

Find the root. The whole expression is (something) − (something), and that outer subtraction is what you would do last. So the root is −.

Left subtree, 3 − 4 · 2. Multiplication binds tighter than subtraction, so 4 · 2 is computed first and the subtraction last. Its root is −, with children 3 and the subtree 4 · 2.

Right subtree, 5 + (5 − 2). The bracket is computed first, so the addition is last. Its root is +, with children 5 and the subtree 5 − 2.

iv. Prefix notation

Read the tree in preorder: root, then left subtree, then right.

root −, then the left subtree (− 3 · 4 2), then the right subtree (+ 5 − 5 2)

− − 3 · 4 2 + 5 − 5 2

v. Postfix notation

Read the tree in postorder: left subtree, right subtree, then root.

left subtree (3 4 2 · −), then the right subtree (5 5 2 − +), then the root −

3 4 2 · − 5 5 2 − + −

Check by evaluating the postfix form, which costs four lines and proves the tree is right. Read left to right, pushing numbers and applying each operator to the two most recent values:

ReadStack afterwards
3, 4, 23, 4, 2
·3, 8   (4 × 2)
−−5   (3 − 8)
5, 5, 2−5, 5, 5, 2
−−5, 5, 3   (5 − 2)
+−5, 8   (5 + 3)
−−13   (−5 − 8)

Directly: (3 − 8) − (5 + 3) = −5 − 8 = −13. The two agree, so the tree, the postfix form and the arithmetic are all consistent.

If the two unreadable characters are exponents. Say this in one line on the script rather than leaving it unaddressed. Each would become an extra internal vertex, an exponentiation operator, sitting above the bracket it follows: the left subtree would become (3 − 4 · 2) raised to that power, and the inner (5 − 2) likewise. The tree gains two vertices, the root and the overall shape are unchanged, and both notations gain the two operators in the corresponding places. Nothing about the method changes.

Now redo it: give the prefix and postfix forms of (2 + 3) · 4. The root is ·, so prefix is · + 2 3 4 and postfix is 2 3 + 4 ·.

TrapPrefix is not "the expression backwards"

Postfix is not the reverse of prefix, and neither is produced by moving the operators about by eye. Both are read off the tree, and the tree is where the grouping lives. Compare:

InfixPrefixPostfix
3 − 4 · 2− 3 · 4 23 4 2 · −
(3 − 4) · 2· − 3 4 23 4 − 2 ·

Those two infix expressions differ only by a pair of brackets and they have different values, −5 and −2. Their prefix and postfix forms use the same five symbols in different orders, with no brackets at all. That is the property that makes these notations worth having, and it only works if the tree was built correctly.

5. The order of the symbols already records the grouping, so no punctuation is needed to disambiguate it.
