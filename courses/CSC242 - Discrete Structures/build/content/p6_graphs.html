Graphs, Vertices, Edges and Degree

A graph is dots and lines. Almost every mark in this part comes from naming the dots and lines precisely, so the vocabulary is the subject, not the preamble.

By the end of this section you will be able to define a graph, a vertex and an edge, count the order and the size of a graph, compute the degree of a vertex, say what incidence and adjacency mean, name loops, parallel edges, pendant and isolated vertices, and answer the incidence and short-notes questions in full.

Not in the course text
The official 161-page text contains no graph theory at all. It never uses the word vertex, never defines an edge, and never states the Handshaking theorem. Meanwhile 2025/2026 gives graphs roughly 30 of its marks, 2024/2025 gives a whole 17.5-mark question to one graph, and 2020/2021, 2021/2022 and 2023/2024 each build two questions on them. This part is your only source, so nothing here assumes you have seen a graph before.

What a graph is

Must memoriseGraph, vertex, edge

A graph G = (V, E) consists of a set V of vertices and a set E of edges.

A vertex (plural vertices) is a point, drawn as a dot or a small circle, standing for an object: a city, a computer, a person, a state of a program. A vertex is also called a node.

An edge is a line that joins two vertices, standing for a relationship between the objects: a road, a cable, a friendship, a transition.

Where V is the vertex set, and it must not be empty: a graph has at least one vertex. E is the edge set, and it may be empty. A graph with no edges is still a graph. G = (V, E) is the standard way of naming a graph by its two parts, and it is worth writing out in an answer.

Memory hook: dots and lines, objects and relationships. The picture is not the graph. Two very different-looking drawings can be the same graph, because only which dots are joined matters, never where they sit on the page.

Must memoriseOrder and size

The order of a graph is the number of vertices, written |V| or n.

The size of a graph is the number of edges, written |E| or e.

Where |V| and |E| are cardinalities, the same bars used for set size in section 2.1. order counts dots. size counts lines. The two words are not interchangeable, and 2025/2026 Q4(a) pays 2 marks for knowing which is which.

Memory hook: order counts the objects, size counts the connections. Everyday English suggests "size" should mean how big the graph is; in graph theory it means how many edges it has. Learn it against your instinct.

Degree, and the words that go with it

Must memoriseDegree and valency

The degree of a vertex v, written deg(v), is the number of edges meeting v, with a loop counted twice.

Valency is another word for exactly the same thing. Some papers use one word, some the other, and 2025/2026 asks you to tell them apart. They are the same quantity under two names.

Where deg(v) is a whole number, 0 or more. a loop at v counts 2, because both of its ends meet v. parallel edges each count separately: two edges between the same pair contribute 2 to each endpoint.

Memory hook: count the line-ends touching the dot, not the neighbours. A vertex joined to one neighbour by three parallel edges has degree 3, not 1.

Incidence and adjacency are not the same relation, and the exam asks about incidence specifically. Incidence relates a vertex to an edge; adjacency relates a vertex to another vertex. Saying "the vertices are incident" is wrong, and so is "the vertex is adjacent to the edge".

Degree and valency of a node in a graph.

The distinction, stated plainly. They are two names for one quantity: the number of edges meeting the node, a loop counting twice. There is no graph and no node for which the degree and the valency differ. The difference is one of usage, not of value:

Two examples, as the question demands.

  1. In the figure above, vertex b is met by one edge to a and two parallel edges to c. So deg(b) = 3, and equally the valency of b is 3.
  2. Vertex c carries two parallel edges to b and one loop. The loop contributes 2, so deg(c) = 2 + 2 = 4, and the valency of c is 4. Both words give the same 4, and both would give the same 4 for any vertex you choose.

Now redo it: what are the degree and the valency of the pendant vertex d? Both are 1, and both would be 0 for the isolated vertex e.

Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q4(a)(i) and (ii)

(i) Size of a graph

Definition. The size of a graph G = (V, E) is the number of edges it has, written |E|. It is not the number of vertices; that is the order, |V|.

Example 1. The graph in the figure above has edges ab, bc, bc again, the loop at c, and ad. That is five edges, so its size is 5. Its order is 5 as well, since it has vertices a, b, c, d and e, and the coincidence is worth pointing out precisely because it shows the two counts are different questions.

Example 2. A graph on 4 vertices with every pair joined has C(4, 2) = 6 edges, so its size is 6 and its order is 4.

(ii) The valency of a vertex in a graph

Definition. The valency of a vertex is the number of edges incident to it, a loop counting twice. It is the same quantity as the degree, deg(v).

Example 1. In a triangle, every vertex is joined to the other two, so every vertex has valency 2.

Example 2. In a star with one centre joined to four outer vertices, the centre has valency 4 and each outer vertex has valency 1, so each outer vertex is a pendant vertex.

Note on the paper, and on the figure
The same question on two papers, one wanting a worked figure for 5 marks and the other an example for 3.5. The definition is identical; only the second half changes.

The 2020/2021 figure cannot be fully read. It shows four vertices numbered 1 to 4 and six edges labelled e1 to e6. Vertices 1, 2 and 3 and the edges e1 to e5 are clear, including that e1 and e2 are parallel, as are e4 and e5. The sixth edge, e6, is drawn as a long curve on the right passing around vertex 4, and at the resolution we hold it cannot be settled whether it is a loop at vertex 2 or a second path from 2 to 3. Vertex 4 is joined to nothing either way, which is plainly the point the question is testing. The answer below states the reading it uses, tabulates on that reading, and shows what changes under the other. Read the arrowless curve on your own copy before committing.

Worked examplePQ 20/21 Q1(c) · PQ 21/22 Q5(c)
Given
Vertices 1, 2, 3, 4. Edges e1 and e2 both joining 1 and 2; e3 joining 1 and 3; e4 and e5 both joining 2 and 3; e6 a loop at 2 on the reading used here.
Find
The definition of incidence, then every edge at every vertex.
Rule
A vertex is incident to an edge exactly when it is one of that edge's two endpoints.

Part 1: the definition. A vertex v is said to be incident to an edge e when v is one of the two endpoints of e; equivalently, when e touches v in the drawing. The relation runs both ways in speech: we say that e is incident to v, and that v is incident to e. For a loop, the single vertex is incident to the edge, and the edge contributes 2 to that vertex's degree.

Part 2: the table. State the reading first, then tabulate.

Reading used: e6 is a loop at vertex 2.

VertexEdges it is incident toDegree
1e1, e2, e33
2e1, e2, e4, e5, e6 (the loop, twice)6

Check the total. The degrees add to 3 + 6 + 3 + 0 = 12, which is twice the six edges. That is the Handshaking theorem of section 6.3, and it is the fastest way to catch a miscounted table.

Under the other reading, where e6 joins 2 to 3 rather than looping, vertex 2 is incident to e1, e2, e4, e5, e6 with degree 5 and vertex 3 to e3, e4, e5, e6 with degree 4. The degrees then add to 3 + 5 + 4 + 0 = 12, still twice six, so the check passes either way. Only the two middle rows change.

For the 2021/2022 version, which asks only to "Show an example", one edge and its two vertices is enough: draw an edge e joining u and v, and write "u is incident to e and v is incident to e; u and v are adjacent". Add a loop as a second example to show you know it counts twice.

Now redo it: in a triangle with vertices x, y, z and edges f1 = xy, f2 = yz, f3 = zx, which edges is y incident to? f1 and f2, so deg(y) = 2.

1. Define a graph, a vertex and an edge.

2. What is the difference between the order and the size of a graph?

3. How much does a loop add to a vertex's degree, and why?

4. When is a vertex incident to an edge?

5. What is a pendant vertex, and what is an isolated vertex?

Answers

1. A graph G = (V, E) is a set V of vertices, which are points standing for objects, together with a set E of edges, each joining two vertices and standing for a relationship.
2. Order is the number of vertices, |V|. Size is the number of edges, |E|.
3. Two, because both of its ends meet that vertex.
4. When the vertex is one of the edge's two endpoints.
5. A pendant vertex has degree 1; an isolated vertex has degree 0.
6. No. They are two names for the same number.
