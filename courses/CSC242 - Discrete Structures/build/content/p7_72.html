m-ary and Balanced Trees

Two formulas answer almost every tree counting question on these papers, and the chain-letter problem they solve has appeared on three of the five in three different costumes.

By the end of this section you will be able to define m-ary, full m-ary, binary and balanced trees, use the two counting formulas to find the vertices, leaves or internal vertices of a full m-ary tree, prove the height bound on leaves, and answer the balanced-tree, chain-letter, quinary and playoff questions in full.

The families

Must memorisem-ary, full and binary

A rooted tree is m-ary when every internal vertex has at most m children.

It is a full m-ary tree when every internal vertex has exactly m children.

An m-ary tree with m = 2 is a binary tree; with m = 3 a ternary tree; with m = 5 a quinary tree, which is the word the 2024/2025 paper uses.

Where m is a positive whole number, the branching factor. at most m is the m-ary condition; exactly m is the full condition, and the difference between them decides whether the counting formulas below apply at all. a leaf has no children, so the condition is only ever about internal vertices.

Memory hook: m-ary caps the branching, full fixes it. Every full m-ary tree is m-ary; the reverse is not true, and the formulas need full.

Must memoriseBalanced tree

An m-ary tree of height h is balanced when every leaf lies at level h or at level h − 1.

Where h is the height, the deepest level any vertex reaches, from section 7.1. h or h − 1 is the whole condition: the leaves sit on the bottom two levels and nowhere higher. No leaf may dangle near the top while another branch runs deep.

Why balance is worth having. A balanced tree of n vertices has height about logm n, which is as small as it can be. Since searching costs one comparison per level, a balanced tree is what makes a search structure fast: an unbalanced tree can degenerate into a single long chain, at which point searching it is no better than reading a list.

Memory hook: all the leaves on the bottom two shelves. If any leaf is higher than that, the tree is not balanced.

The two counting formulas

Must memoriseCounting a full m-ary tree

In a full m-ary tree with i internal vertices:

n = mi + 1     (total vertices)
l = n − i = (m − 1)i + 1     (leaves)

Where m is the branching factor: every internal vertex has exactly m children. i is the number of internal vertices, the ones that do have children. n is the total number of vertices and l the number of leaves, so n = i + l always.

Where n = mi + 1 comes from. Every vertex except the root is the child of exactly one internal vertex. Each of the i internal vertices supplies exactly m children, so there are mi non-root vertices, and adding the root gives mi + 1.

Then the leaves follow with no new idea: l = n − i = (mi + 1) − i = (m − 1)i + 1.

Rearranged for the exam, because questions usually give you the leaves and want the rest:

i = (l − 1) / (m − 1)     and then     n = mi + 1

Memory hook: every vertex but the root is somebody's child. That one sentence gives n = mi + 1, and everything else in this box is algebra on it.

Must memoriseThe height bound

An m-ary tree of height h has at most mh leaves.

Where h is the height, from section 7.1. mh is m multiplied by itself h times. at most is exact: equality holds precisely when the tree is full and every leaf is at level h.

Why. Level 0 holds the root alone. Each vertex has at most m children, so level 1 holds at most m vertices, level 2 at most m × m = m2, and level k at most mk. Every leaf lies at some level no deeper than h, and the deepest level can hold at most mh of them.

Memory hook: each level multiplies by m. The product rule of section 5.1 applied h times: h independent choices of which child to descend to, m ways each.

Worked examplePQ 20/21 Q3(a) · PQ 21/22 Q4(b)

Build the answer in four layers, each earning its own marks.

1. m-ary

A rooted tree is m-ary when every internal vertex has at most m children. It is full m-ary when every internal vertex has exactly m children. For m = 2 it is called binary, for m = 3 ternary, for m = 5 quinary.

2. Level and height

The level of a vertex is the number of edges from the root to it, the root being at level 0. The height of the tree is the largest level any vertex reaches.

3. Balanced

An m-ary tree of height h is balanced when every leaf is at level h or at level h − 1: all the leaves lie on the bottom two levels. Putting the two words together, a balanced m-ary tree is a rooted tree in which no internal vertex has more than m children and every leaf sits on one of the two deepest levels.

4. The examples, drawn

Refer to the three trees drawn above. The first is a full ternary tree of height 2 whose leaves lie at levels 1 and 2, so it is balanced. The second is a full binary tree of height 2 with leaves at levels 1 and 2, also balanced. The third is a full binary tree of height 3 with one leaf at level 1, which is two levels above the bottom, so it is not balanced even though it is full.

That third example is the one that earns marks, because it shows that full and balanced are independent: a tree can be one without the other.

5. Why it matters, for the last mark

A balanced m-ary tree with n vertices has height roughly logm n, the smallest a tree of that size can have. Search cost is proportional to height, so balance is what keeps a search structure fast; an unbalanced tree can stretch into a chain and lose the advantage entirely.

Now redo it: is a full binary tree with leaves at levels 2, 3 and 3 balanced? Its height is 3, so leaves are allowed at levels 3 and 2. Yes, it is balanced.

Note on the paper
One problem, three papers, 22.5 marks. 2020/2021 tells it as a chain letter and asks both questions in one run-on sentence for 8 marks; the two later papers tell it as convocation invitation cards and split it into (i) and (ii) for 10 and 9.5 marks. That is a subordinate clause with no main clause, so the sentence never finishes. The numbered parts supply the question. Nothing is missing from the data.

The sentence doing the mathematical work is "no one receives more than one card". That is what makes the picture a tree: one parent per person, and no cycles.

Worked examplePQ 20/21 Q3(b) · PQ 21/22 Q3(a) · PQ 23/24 Q5(d)
Given
m = 4 recipients per sender; l = 100 people who read it but did not send it on; nobody receives it twice.
Find
n, the number who have seen it, and i, the number who sent it out.
Formula
For a full m-ary tree, l = (m − 1)i + 1 and n = mi + 1.

Step 1. Model it, and say so. Draw each person as a vertex and each sending as an edge from sender to recipient. Because no one receives more than one letter, every person other than the first has exactly one parent, so the picture is a rooted tree with the first person at the root. Because everyone who sends, sends to exactly four, every internal vertex has exactly four children, so it is a full 4-ary tree.

senders = internal vertices = i
people who read but did not send = leaves = l = 100
people who have seen it = all vertices = n

Step 2. Find the internal vertices from the leaves.

l = (m − 1)i + 1
100 = (4 − 1)i + 1
100 = 3i + 1
99 = 3i
i = 33

Step 3. Find the total.

n = mi + 1 = 4 × 33 + 1 = 132 + 1 = 133

Answers.

(i) 133 people have seen the letter, including the first person.
(ii) 33 people sent it out.

Check, and write the check down. The vertices split into senders and non-senders with nothing left over:

i + l = 33 + 100 = 133 = n   ✓

And count the edges: 33 senders each sending 4 gives 132 letters sent, which is exactly the 132 people other than the first. That is e = v − 1 from section 7.1, so the tree arithmetic closes twice over.

For the 2020/2021 version, which asks both questions in one sentence and pays 8 marks, give the same working and end with both numbers named: 133 have seen it, 33 sent it out.

Now redo it: same problem with each person sending to three others. Then 100 = 2i + 1 gives i = 49.5, which is not a whole number, so no such chain exists: with m = 3 the leaf count is always odd. Saying that is the answer, and it is the same style of impossibility argument as section 6.3.

The numbering skips. The parts run i, then iv, v, vi, vii. There is no ii and no iii. The marks still total 17.5 for the question, so nothing is missing from the paper; only the labels are wrong.

Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q6(b) vi and vii
Given
m = 5 (quinary), h = 3.
Find
The number of leaves, and a proof of the general bound.
Formula
An m-ary tree of height h has at most mh leaves.

vi. The number of leaves

Say what is determined. A quinary tree of height 3 need not have any particular number of leaves; the quantity fixed by m and h is the maximum, reached when the tree is full and every leaf sits at level 3. So:

leaves = mh = 53 = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125

Answer: at most 125 leaves, and exactly 125 for the full quinary tree of height 3.

Check it against the other formula, which is worth a line. The full quinary tree of height 3 has internal vertices at levels 0, 1 and 2, that is

i = 1 + 5 + 25 = 31

and then l = (m − 1)i + 1 = 4 × 31 + 1 = 125. The two formulas agree, as they must, and n = mi + 1 = 5 × 31 + 1 = 156 total vertices.

vii. Prove the bound

To prove: an m-ary tree of height h has at most mh leaves.

The method, in two sentences, before it is used. This is a proof by mathematical induction: show the claim holds at the smallest value of h, then show that whenever it holds at one height it must also hold at the next, and those two facts together carry it to every height. Section 9.2 sets the method out in full, with the reasons it works and several more examples; everything needed for this proof is in the sentence you have just read.

Proof, by induction on the height h.

Base case, h = 0. A tree of height 0 is a single vertex, which is the root and also a leaf. It has 1 leaf, and m0 = 1. So the claim holds.

Inductive step. Suppose the claim holds for every m-ary tree of height h − 1, for some h ≥ 1. Let T be an m-ary tree of height h. Remove the root. What remains is at most m subtrees, one for each child of the root, since T is m-ary. Each subtree has height at most h − 1, so by the inductive hypothesis each has at most mh−1 leaves.

Every leaf of T lies in one of those subtrees, so

leaves of T ≤ m × mh−1 = mh

By induction the claim holds for every h ≥ 0. □

When is the bound attained? Exactly when the root has all m children, every subtree attains its own bound, and no leaf sits above level h; that is, when the tree is full and all leaves are at level h. Adding that sentence shows the bound is sharp rather than merely true.

Applying it at m = 5, h = 3 gives 53 = 125, which is part vi.

Now redo it: what is the greatest number of leaves a binary tree of height 4 can have? mh = 24 = 16.

The playoff tree

As printed2020/2021 · Q4(a)

4. (a) Playoff between two teams consists of at most five games. The first team that wins three games wins the playoff.

(i) Construct a tree diagram for five games. (4marks)

(ii) In how many different ways can the playoff occur? (3½marks)

Note on the paper
"Playoff between two teams" is missing its article; it should read "A playoff". More importantly, part (i) says "Construct a tree diagram for five games", but the playoff can finish in three or four games as well: the tree must show every way the series can end, and its branches are of different lengths. Drawing exactly five levels everywhere would be wrong, because once a team has three wins no further game is played.
Break it down
Given
Two teams. At most five games. The first to three wins takes the playoff.
Asked
(i) Construct the tree diagram. (ii) In how many different ways the playoff can occur.
Model
A binary tree. Each vertex is a state of the series; the two children are "team A wins the next game" and "team B wins it". A branch stops as soon as either team reaches three wins, so the leaves are exactly the possible outcomes.
Marks say
4 for the diagram, 3.5 for the count. The count is the number of leaves, so drawing the tree carefully answers both parts at once.
Teaches
This section, 7.2, with the combinations of 5.2.
Trap
Drawing all 25 = 32 sequences of five games. Most of them are impossible, because the series stops the moment a team reaches three wins. The answer is 20, not 32.
Worked examplePQ 20/21 Q4(a)
Given
Two teams, first to 3 wins, at most 5 games.
Find
The tree of possible series, and the number of ways the playoff can occur.
Rule
Each game branches two ways; a branch stops at 3 wins. The outcomes are the leaves.

Part (i): the tree. Label each vertex with the score so far as (wins for A, wins for B). The root is (0, 0). From any vertex, the left child adds a win for A and the right child a win for B. Stop as soon as either count reaches 3.

Part (ii): how many ways. Count the leaves by how long the series runs. Take team A winning first.

  • A wins in 3 games. Only AAA, so 1 way.
  • A wins in 4 games. The fourth game must be A's third win, so A won exactly 2 of the first 3. Choosing which 2: C(3, 2) = 3.
  • A wins in 5 games. The fifth game is A's third win, so A won exactly 2 of the first 4: C(4, 2) = 6.

A wins in 1 + 3 + 6 = 10 ways.

By symmetry B also wins in 10 ways, and the two cases cannot both happen, so the sum rule of section 5.1 gives

total = 10 + 10 = 20

Answer: the playoff can occur in 20 different ways.

Why the last game must be the decider, which is the step most answers skip: if A had already won three games earlier, the series would have stopped there and the later games would never be played. So in any series of exactly k games, the k-th is the winner's third win.

Now redo it: best of three, first to two wins. A wins in 2 games: 1 way; in 3 games: C(2, 1) = 2 ways. So A wins in 3 ways, B in 3, total 6.
