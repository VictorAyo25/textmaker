Question One

Answer2020/2021 Q1 · 23.5 marks

a. Types of undirected graph, with examples (12 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.2, where the full catalogue is tabled. The single most repeated question on this course, worth 12 marks here, 12 on 2021/2022 and 9 on 2023/2024.

Name, define and illustrate each: simple, multigraph, pseudograph, null, trivial, complete Kn, regular, cycle, path, wheel, bipartite, complete bipartite Km,n, connected, weighted and subgraph. Note this paper says "with the aid of relevant examples" rather than "diagrammatical illustrations", so a named instance (K4, C5, K3,3) counts alongside a sketch.

The distinction the marks turn on is simple against multigraph against pseudograph: no repeats and no loops, repeats allowed, repeats and loops allowed. Ten types with examples beats fifteen names without.

b. Communication links between five buildings (6½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.2. The question names five buildings. A simple link joins two of them, and the link from X to Y is the same link as from Y to X, so order does not matter:

links = C(5, 2) = 5 × 4 / 2 = 10

The diagram is K5: five dots arranged in a pentagon, each joined to the other four, which draws as a pentagon with all five diagonals. Ten lines, counted.

Count the buildings, do not remember the number. The 2021/2022 paper sets this with four buildings and its answer is C(4, 2) = 6.

Worth one extra line: K5 is exactly the graph that section 6.7 proves non-planar, so these ten links cannot be laid out on a flat plan without two of them crossing. That is a genuine engineering observation and it costs one sentence.

c. Incidence, and the edges each vertex is incident to (5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.1.

Definition. A vertex v is incident to an edge e when v is one of the two endpoints of e. Incidence relates a vertex to an edge; adjacency relates a vertex to another vertex.

State the reading of the figure. Vertices 1, 2 and 3 with edges e1 to e5 are clear, and e1, e2 are a parallel pair, as are e4, e5. The sixth edge is drawn as a long curve on the right and cannot be settled at this resolution as a loop at vertex 2 or an edge from 2 to 3. The table below takes it as a loop at vertex 2 and gives the other reading underneath.

VertexIncident edgesDegree
1e1, e2, e33
2e1, e2, e4, e5, e66
3e3, e4, e53
4none0

Why vertex 2's degree is 6 and not 5. A loop counts twice towards the degree, because both of its ends meet the same vertex.

The Handshaking check, which settles that the reading is at least consistent. Six edges give a degree sum of 2 × 6 = 12, and 3 + 6 + 3 + 0 = 12. Under the other reading, with e6 joining 2 and 3, the degrees are 3, 5, 4 and 0, which also sum to 12. Both readings pass, so the check cannot decide between them, and saying that is more honest than pretending it can.

Vertex 4 is the point of the question. It is incident to no edge at all, which is why it was drawn. An isolated vertex has degree 0 and is still a vertex.

a. The travelling missionary from Detroit (10 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.8, where all twelve tours are tabulated. The same figure and the same ten distances as 2021/2022 and 2023/2024.

Twelve tours, since fixing Detroit leaves 4! = 24 orders for the other four cities and each tour equals its own reverse: 24 / 2 = 12.

The minimum is 458 miles:

Detroit, Saginaw, Grand Rapids, Kalamazoo, Toledo, Detroit
98 + 113 + 56 + 133 + 58 = 458

The question says "minimum", so list all twelve. Exhibiting one short tour shows 458 is achievable; only the enumeration shows nothing beats it.

b. Four real-life scenarios for graph models, two examples each (6 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.1. Say what the vertices and the edges are in each; that is the modelling, and it is what is being marked.

1. Transport networks. Vertices are places, edges are routes. Examples: a road map with distances on the edges, exactly as in part (a); an airline map where an edge means a direct flight.

2. Social networks. Vertices are people, edges are relationships. Examples: friendship, where the edge is undirected because it is mutual; following, where it is directed because it is not.

3. Utility and supply networks. Vertices are junctions, edges are cables or pipes. Examples: an electricity grid; a water network where the edge weights are capacities.

4. Scheduling and dependency. Vertices are tasks, edges are "must come before". Examples: a project plan, in which a cycle would mean an impossible schedule; an exam timetable, in which an edge means two papers clash and cannot share a slot.

c. Key terminologies of a rooted tree, with a diagram (7½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.1. Identical to 2021/2022 Q3(c).

Definition first. A rooted tree is a tree with one vertex designated the root; every edge is then read as directed away from it, and every other vertex has exactly one path back to it.

The terms: root, parent, child, siblings, ancestors, descendants, leaf, internal vertex, subtree, level (0 at the root) and height (the largest level).

Then one labelled diagram carrying every term at once, with the levels numbered down the left edge. Eleven labels on one tree beats eleven separate sketches, and it is faster to draw.

Close with the counting facts: v vertices means v − 1 edges, and there is exactly one path between any two vertices.

Question Three

Answer2020/2021 Q3 · 23.5 marks

a. What a balanced m-ary tree means, with examples (7½ marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.2. Three definitions stacked, and the question needs all three.

m-ary: every internal vertex has at most m children. Full: every internal vertex has exactly m children. Balanced, for a tree of height h: every leaf sits at level h or at level h − 1.

Examples. A binary tree with a root, two children and four grandchildren has all four leaves at level 2: full and balanced. Hang two more vertices under one of those leaves and it is no longer balanced, since the height becomes 3 while three leaves remain at level 2. A chain of five vertices is m-ary for every m and as unbalanced as a tree can be.

Why it matters: a balanced tree of height h holds close to mh leaves, so reaching any one of them costs about h steps, while the chain costs one step per vertex. That is the reason the word exists.

b. The chain letter (8 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.2. The first person is the root, each sender has exactly four children, and the 100 who read but did not send are the leaves.

l = (m − 1)i + 1, so 100 = 3i + 1 and i = 33
n = mi + 1 = 4 × 33 + 1 = 133

133 people have seen the letter, including the first, and 33 people sent it out, those being the internal vertices.

Check: 33 + 100 = 133.

The same problem appears as convocation cards on 2021/2022 Q3(a) and 2023/2024 Q5(d). One method, three costumes.

c. Four application areas in computing, one example each (8 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.1. Computing areas, not real-life scenarios: part (b) already took those.

1. Networking and routing. Vertices are routers, edges are links. Example: computing a shortest path across the network.

2. Compilers and program analysis. Vertices are program points, edges are possible jumps. Example: a control-flow graph, used to find code that can never run.

3. Databases and the web. Vertices are pages or records, edges are links or keys. Example: ranking a page by how many others point at it, which is a question about its in-degree.

4. Operating systems. Vertices are processes and resources, edges are "holds" and "waits for". Example: detecting deadlock, which is searching that graph for a cycle.

Question Four

Answer2020/2021 Q4 · 23.5 marks

a. i. A tree diagram for the playoff (4 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.2. Two teams, at most five games, first to three wins takes the playoff.

Read the question carefully. It says "a tree diagram for five games", but the series stops the moment a team reaches three wins, so many branches end at three or four games. The tree must show every way the playoff can finish, not five games on every branch.

How to draw it. Start at the root. Each vertex has two children, one for each team winning the next game, labelled A and B. Stop a branch as soon as one letter has appeared three times along it. The result is a binary tree whose leaves are the completed series, the shortest with three games (AAA and BBB) and the longest with five.

a. ii. In how many ways can the playoff occur? (3½ marks)

Count the leaves, which is easier by cases than by drawing.

A wins in three games: AAA, and there is 1 way.
A wins in four: A's third win is the last game, so exactly one of the first three is a B, which is C(3, 1) = 3 ways.
A wins in five: the last game is A's third win, so exactly two of the first four are B, which is C(4, 2) = 6 ways.

A wins in 1 + 3 + 6 = 10 ways

By symmetry B wins in 10 ways too, so the playoff can occur in

10 + 10 = 20 different ways

The trap here is 25 = 32. That would be the answer if five games were always played, but the series stops early, so the shorter sequences are single outcomes rather than groups of them. Saying that sentence shows you saw the difference.

Question Five

Answer2020/2021 Q5 · 22.5 marks as printed

a. Define and draw the truth table for six terms (6 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 1.1. One mark each, so a definition and a table apiece.

i. Negation. ¬p is true exactly when p is false.   T gives F, F gives T.

ii. Conjunction. p ∧ q is true only when both are.   TT gives T; the other three rows give F.

iii. Exclusive or. p ⊕ q is true when exactly one is.   TT gives F, TF gives T, FT gives T, FF gives F.

iv. Implication. p → q is false only when p is true and q false.   TT gives T, TF gives F, FT gives T, FF gives T.

v. Tautology. A compound proposition true in every row, whatever its letters are. Example: p ∨ ¬p, whose column is T, T.

vi. "Contigency", as printed, which is contingency. A compound proposition that is neither a tautology nor a contradiction: true in some rows and false in others. Example: p ∧ q, whose column is T, F, F, F. Quote the misspelling, then define the real term.

Name the third member of the family, which is free marks: a contradiction is false in every row, such as p ∧ ¬p. Tautology, contradiction and contingency are the three possibilities, and every compound proposition is exactly one of them.

b. How many possible passwords? (5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 5.1. Six to eight characters, each an uppercase letter or a digit, and at least one digit.

Count the alphabet. 26 letters plus 10 digits = 36 symbols, of which 26 are not digits.

c. ii. Which are reflexive, symmetric, transitive? (6½ marks)

RelationReflexiveSymmetricTransitive
R1: a ≤ byesnoyes
R2: a > bnonoyes
R3: a = ±byesyesyes
R4: a = byesyesyes
R5: a = b + 1nonono
R6: a + b ≤ 3noyesno

Give the reason for each "no", since that is what 6.5 marks buys.

R1 is not symmetric: 1 ≤ 2 but 2 is not ≤ 1.
R2 is not reflexive, since no a is greater than itself, and not symmetric for the same reason as R1. It is transitive: a > b and b > c give a > c.
R3 is reflexive since a = a; symmetric since a = −b gives b = −a; and transitive, because a = ±b and b = ±c force a = ±c.
R4 is equality itself, which is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, and is therefore an equivalence relation.
R5 fails all three: a is never a + 1; a = b + 1 does not give b = a + 1; and a = b + 1 with b = c + 1 gives a = c + 2, not c + 1.
R6 is not reflexive, since 2 + 2 = 4 is not ≤ 3. It is symmetric, because a + b = b + a. It is not transitive: (3, 0) and (0, 3) are both in R6 but (3, 3) is not, since 3 + 3 = 6.

The counterexamples are the answer. A single explicit pair settles each "no", and R6's failure of transitivity is the one worth writing out in full, because the relation is symmetric and looks well behaved until it is tested.
