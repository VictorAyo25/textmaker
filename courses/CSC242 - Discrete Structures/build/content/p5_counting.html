Counting sounds like the easiest thing in this book and is the one that loses the most marks. Every question here is answered by deciding two things: are the choices stages or alternatives, and does order matter.
Part Five · 5.1

The Product and Sum Rules

Two rules answer most counting questions on these papers, and choosing between them is the whole skill. Stages multiply. Alternatives add.

By the end of this section you will be able to break a counting task into parts, decide whether those parts are compulsory or alternative, apply the product rule, the sum rule and the subtraction rule, and answer the auditorium chairs, ATM PIN, three-digit-number and password questions in full.

What a counting question is asking

TeachCounting without listing

A counting question asks how many objects of some kind exist. The honest way to answer is to list them all and count the list. That works for six letters and fails for a million PIN codes, so we need rules that give the size of the list without writing it out.

Every such rule comes from the same move: break the task into parts, count each part, then combine. The only question is how to combine, and there are exactly two answers.

If the parts areThenBecause
compulsory: you do part 1 and part 2 and part 3multiplyevery way of doing part 1 pairs with every way of doing part 2
alternative: you do part 1 or part 2 or part 3, never two at onceaddthe possibilities are separate piles and nothing sits in two piles

Those two words, compulsory and alternative, are the ones the 2025/2026 paper uses when it asks you to name the parts of a task. They are worth learning in exactly that language.

The product rule

Must memoriseProduct rule

If a task is carried out in a sequence of stages, and

stage 1 can be done in n1 ways,
stage 2 in n2 ways whatever stage 1 did,
… and stage k in nk ways,

then the whole task can be carried out in

n1 × n2 × … × nk ways.

Where k is the number of stages, one for each decision you must make. ni is the number of choices at stage i. × is ordinary multiplication.

Memory hook: and means times. A shirt and a pair of trousers: 3 shirts and 2 trousers give 6 outfits, not 5. Say the task out loud; if the joining word is "and", multiply.

TeachWhy multiplying is right

Draw the choices as a tree. Each stage adds a level, and each complete path from the root to a tip is one outcome. Three first choices, each splitting into two, gives 3 × 2 = 6 tips.

The condition that stage 2 has n2 ways whatever stage 1 did is what makes every branch split the same number of times. If the second choice depended on the first, the branches would have different widths and the count would not be a single product.

The sum rule

Must memoriseSum rule

If a task can be done in one of k alternative ways, and

alternative 1 can be done in n1 ways, alternative 2 in n2 ways, … alternative k in nk ways,

and no outcome counts under two alternatives at once, then the task can be done in

n1 + n2 + … + nk ways.

Where k is the number of alternatives, one for each separate case. no overlap is a genuine condition, not decoration. If two alternatives can both produce the same outcome, adding counts that outcome twice.

Memory hook: or means plus. Piles you can choose between are added; stages you must pass through are multiplied.

TrapThe word "or" is not proof of a sum

English "or" is a bad guide on its own. In the password question below, each character is "an uppercase letter or a digit", and that or is counted by adding: 26 + 10 = 36 symbols. But choosing eight characters is eight compulsory stages, so those 36s are multiplied. One sentence, both rules, in that order.

The reliable test is not the word. It is this question: am I describing one decision with several options, or several decisions I must all make? Options add. Decisions multiply.

The subtraction rule

Must memoriseSubtraction rule

To count the objects with some property, when the ones without it are easier to count:

(number with the property) = (total) − (number without it)

Where total is the count with the condition ignored entirely, which is nearly always the easy number. number without it is the count of the cases the condition rules out, and it must be counted over the same total, not over some smaller pool. the two groups must cover everything between them and share nothing, which is what makes one subtraction exact.

This is the counting form of the complement from section 2.2: the objects with the property and the objects without it fill the whole collection between them and share nothing.

Memory hook: "at least one" means "not none". Whenever a question says at least one, count everything, count the cases with none, and subtract. Counting "at least one" directly means splitting into exactly one, exactly two, exactly three and so on, which is longer and easier to get wrong.

The auditorium chairs

As printed2021/2022 · Q2(a)

2. (a) The chairs of an auditorium are to be labeled with an uppercase English letter followed by a positive integer not exceeding 100. What is the largest number of chairs that can be labeled differently? (6marks)

Note on the paper
"labeled" is the American spelling; the paper uses it throughout. The mark text is closed up as "(6marks)", which is how this whole paper prints marks. Neither affects the answer. What does affect the answer is "not exceeding 100": that means 100 is allowed, so the integers run 1 to 100 inclusive and there are 100 of them, not 99.
Break it down
Given
A label is an uppercase English letter followed by a positive integer that is at most 100.
Asked
The largest number of chairs that can be labelled differently, which is the number of distinct labels available.
Parts
Two, and both are compulsory: every label has a letter and a number. So this is the product rule.
Marks say
6 marks for one multiplication is generous, so the marks are for the reasoning: name the two stages, count each, justify the counts, then multiply.
Teaches
This section, 5.1.
Trap
Two of them. "Uppercase English letter" is 26, not 52. "Positive integer not exceeding 100" is 100, not 99 and not 101: positive excludes 0, and not exceeding includes 100.
Worked examplePQ 21/22 Q2(a)
Given
label = uppercase letter, then a positive integer at most 100.
Find
How many different labels exist.
Rule
Product rule: the two parts are compulsory.

Stage 1: choose the letter. The uppercase English letters are A to Z, so there are 26 choices.

Stage 2: choose the integer. Positive integers not exceeding 100 are 1, 2, 3, …, 100. Counting them, there are 100 choices.

Stage 2 offers the same 100 numbers whichever letter was picked, so the product rule applies directly:

number of labels = 26 × 100 = 2600

Answer: 2600 chairs can be labelled differently.

Say the last sentence in the paper's own terms: since each chair needs its own label, 2600 is the largest number of chairs that can be labelled differently.

Now redo it: if the label were two uppercase letters followed by a positive integer not exceeding 100, how many? Three compulsory stages now: 26 × 26 × 100 = 67600.

The ATM PIN codes

As printed2025/2026 · Q6(b)

(b) ATM PIN code in Nigeria can contain the digits 0,1,2,…,9 and digits may be repeated In the light of the above, how many 4 to 6 digit pin codes are there? (5 marks)

Note on the paper
Two sentences have been run together with no full stop: "digits may be repeated In the light of the above". Read the capital "In" as the start of the second sentence. The phrase "4 to 6 digit" means codes of length 4, length 5 or length 6, and those three lengths are the alternatives that make this a sum-rule question sitting on top of a product-rule one.
Break it down
Given
Each position holds one of the ten digits 0 to 9, and repeats are allowed.
Asked
How many codes of length 4, 5 or 6 there are, in total.
Parts
Two levels. Within one length the positions are compulsory parts, so multiply. Across the three lengths the cases are alternative parts, so add.
Marks say
5 marks for three powers and one addition. Show each length separately, then total. A bare final number risks losing the method marks.
Teaches
This section, 5.1.
Trap
Answering only for 6 digits, or multiplying the three lengths together. A code is 4 or 5 or 6 digits long, never two lengths at once, so the three counts are added.
Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q6(b)
Given
Digits 0 to 9, so 10 choices per position. Repetition allowed. Lengths 4, 5 and 6.
Find
The total number of such codes.
Rule
Product rule within a length, sum rule across lengths.

Step 1. Count the codes of each length. Because repeats are allowed, every position still has all 10 digits available, no matter what the earlier positions took. So each position is a stage with 10 ways.

LengthProduct ruleCount
4 digits10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10410000
5 digits105100000
6 digits1061000000

Step 2. Combine the three lengths. A code has exactly one length, so no code is counted twice and the sum rule applies:

total = 104 + 105 + 106
= 10000 + 100000 + 1000000
= 1110000

Answer: there are 1110000 PIN codes of 4 to 6 digits. Written with separators for readability, 1 110 000.

Why repetition matters. If digits could not repeat, each position would remove one digit from the pool and the length-4 count would be 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 instead of 104. Section 5.2 counts that case. The words "digits may be repeated" are the paper telling you which of the two applies.

Now redo it: how many codes of 4 to 6 digits start with a nonzero digit? Each length loses its first-position zero, giving 9 × 103 + 9 × 104 + 9 × 105 = 9000 + 90000 + 900000 = 999000.

Three-digit numbers, and naming the rule

As printed2025/2026 · Q6(c)

(c) i. How many three digit natural numbers can be formed using digits 0, 1, · · · , 9? (1 mark)

ii. Identify the number of parts in the task and the type of the parts (compulsory or alternative). (4 marks)

iii. Which rule applies here? (1 mark)

Note on the paper
Parts ii and iii say "the task" and "here" without saying which task. They sit directly under (c) i, and (c) i is the nearest task, so answer them about the three-digit numbers. Part (b) immediately above is also a counting task with a different answer, so the safe examination move is to answer for (c) i and then add one line covering (b) as well. That costs a sentence and cannot be marked wrong. Both answers are given below.
Break it down
Given
The ten digits 0 to 9, and a three-digit natural number to build from them.
Asked
(i) How many such numbers. (ii) Identify the number of parts and their type. (iii) Which rule applies. Only the first is a calculation; the other two ask you to describe your own method.
Marks say
1, 4 and 1. The 4 marks are the largest on this part and they are for the description, not the arithmetic. Name each part, say how many ways it has, and say why it is compulsory.
Teaches
This section, 5.1.
Trap
Answering 103 = 1000. A three-digit number cannot begin with 0, because 012 is the two-digit number 12. The hundreds position has 9 choices, not 10.
Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q6(c)
Given
Digits 0 to 9, forming a three-digit natural number.
Find
The count, the parts, and the rule.
Rule
Product rule, with one restricted position.

i. How many three-digit natural numbers

A three-digit natural number runs from 100 to 999. Build it position by position:

PositionAllowed digitsWays
hundreds1 to 9, since a leading 0 would make it a shorter number9
tens0 to 910
units0 to 910

count = 9 × 10 × 10 = 900

Answer: 900. Check it another way, which is worth doing on a 1-mark part: the numbers 100 to 999 inclusive number 999 − 100 + 1 = 900. The two methods agree.

ii. The number of parts and their type

Three parts, all compulsory. Set out in full, since 4 marks are on offer:

  1. Part 1, choose the hundreds digit: 9 ways. Compulsory, because every three-digit number has a hundreds digit.
  2. Part 2, choose the tens digit: 10 ways. Compulsory.
  3. Part 3, choose the units digit: 10 ways. Compulsory.

They are compulsory rather than alternative because the number is not finished until all three have been done. You do not pick one of the three parts; you do part 1 and part 2 and part 3.

For part (b) above, the structure is different and worth one line: that task has three alternative parts, one per allowed length (4, 5 and 6 digits), and each of those parts is itself built from compulsory sub-parts, one per digit position.

iii. Which rule applies

The product rule, because the parts of (c) i are compulsory: the counts 9, 10 and 10 are multiplied.

And again for (b): the sum rule across the three lengths, sitting on top of the product rule within each length.

Now redo it: how many three-digit natural numbers have no repeated digit? Hundreds 9 ways, tens 9 (zero is back in, but the hundreds digit is gone), units 8. So 9 × 9 × 8 = 648.

The password question

As printed2020/2021 · Q5(b)

(b) Each user on a computer system has a password, which is six to eight characters long, where each character is an uppercase letter or a digit. Each password must contain at least one digit. How many possible passwords are there? (5 marks)

Note on the paper
This is the hardest counting question in the whole set, and it is worth 5 marks out of 22.5. It combines all three rules of this section: add across the three lengths, multiply across the character positions, and subtract to handle "at least one digit". Nothing about it is printed wrongly. The difficulty is real, not a defect.
Break it down
Given
Password length 6, 7 or 8. Each character is an uppercase letter or a digit. At least one character must be a digit.
Asked
How many possible passwords there are in total.
Parts
Three alternative lengths, so add. Within a length, one compulsory part per character position, so multiply. The "at least one" condition is handled by subtracting.
Marks say
5 marks. Roughly: 1 for the 36 symbols, 1 for seeing that "at least one digit" means total minus letters-only, 2 for the three lengths, 1 for the total. Show every line.
Teaches
This section, 5.1: product, sum and subtraction rules together.
Trap
Trying to count "at least one digit" directly by cases (exactly one digit, exactly two, and so on). It can be done, but it is seven cases per length and almost nobody finishes it correctly under exam pressure. Subtract instead.
Worked examplePQ 20/21 Q5(b)
Given
Length 6, 7 or 8; each character from 26 uppercase letters or 10 digits; at least one digit required.
Find
The number of possible passwords.
Rule
Sum over lengths; product over positions; subtraction for "at least one".

Step 1. Count the symbols available per character. A character is an uppercase letter or a digit, and no symbol is both, so the sum rule gives

26 + 10 = 36 symbols.

Step 2. Turn "at least one digit" into a subtraction. Let Pn be the number of valid passwords of length n. Then

Pn = (all strings of length n over the 36 symbols) − (those using no digit at all)
= 36n − 26n

A string with no digit is made only of the 26 letters, which is where 26n comes from. Every string either contains a digit or does not, and never both, so the subtraction is exact.

Step 3. Evaluate each length.

P6 = 366 − 266 = 2176782336 − 308915776 = 1867866560
P7 = 367 − 267 = 78364164096 − 8031810176 = 70332353920
P8 = 368 − 268 = 2821109907456 − 208827064576 = 2612282842880

Step 4. Add the three lengths. A password has exactly one length, so the sum rule applies:

P = P6 + P7 + P8 = 2684483063360

Answer: 2684483063360 possible passwords, or 2 684 483 063 360 written with separators.

If you run out of time, the formula P = (366 − 266) + (367 − 267) + (368 − 268) with the three rules named earns most of the 5 marks on its own. Write the formula first, then evaluate. Never the other way round.

Now redo it: same question but passwords are exactly six characters and must contain at least one letter. Answer: 366 − 106 = 2176782336 − 1000000 = 2175782336.

Recall and check

1. When do you multiply the counts of the parts, and when do you add them?

2. What condition must alternatives satisfy before the sum rule is valid?

3. How many three-digit natural numbers are there, and why is it not 1000?

4. Turn "at least one vowel" into a subtraction.

5. Labels are one uppercase letter then an integer from 1 to 100. How many?

Answers

1. Multiply when the parts are compulsory, that is, when all of them must be done. Add when they are alternatives and exactly one of them happens.
2. No outcome may count under two alternatives, otherwise it is counted twice.
3. 900. The hundreds digit cannot be 0, so it has 9 choices, not 10: 9 × 10 × 10.
4. (total number of strings) − (number of strings with no vowel).
5. 26 × 100 = 2600.
