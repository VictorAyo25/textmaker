Reading and Writing Mathematics

Mathematical English is a dialect. A handful of phrases mean something narrower than they do in ordinary speech, and marks are lost every year by reading them casually.

By the end of this section you will know exactly what "if", "only if", "if and only if", "necessary" and "sufficient" translate to in symbols, what the instruction verbs on an exam paper demand, and how to lay out an answer so a marker can give you every mark you earned.

The five phrases that carry the marks

TeachTwo arrows, before we use them

Two symbols appear all through this section, so they are introduced here and studied properly in Part One.

→ is the implication, also called the conditional. p → q is read "if p then q", and it is a single statement claiming that whenever p holds, q holds too.

↔ is the biconditional. p ↔ q is read "p if and only if q", and it claims the two always hold together: each one implies the other. It points both ways because it works both ways.

Here p and q stand for whole statements. Section 1.1 gives them their exact truth tables; for now these readings are enough.

TeachNecessary and sufficient

Take a statement of the form "if p then q", written p → q. English dresses it up in several ways, and they are not interchangeable.

p is sufficient for q. Having p is enough to guarantee q. Being a dog is sufficient for being an animal.

q is necessary for p. Without q you cannot have p. Being an animal is necessary for being a dog.

So one implication, p → q, says two things at once: p is enough, and q is required. Which side is which is the whole question, and it is the thing the examiner tests.

Memory hook: the two dangerous ones are "p if q", which is q → p, and "p only if q", which is p → q. "If" alone introduces the hypothesis; "only if" introduces the conclusion. They point opposite ways.

Trap"Whenever" and "only if" reverse people every year

The 2024/2025 paper uses both, deliberately.

"I go to the beach whenever it is a sunny summer day." The "whenever" clause is the hypothesis. Let p be "it is a sunny summer day" and q "I go to the beach". This is p → q, not q → p. The sunny day triggers the beach trip, not the other way round.

"When I stay up late, it is necessary that I sleep until noon." Here "necessary" marks the conclusion. Let p be "I stay up late" and q "I sleep until noon". This is p → q.

Method that never fails: rewrite the sentence as a plain "if ... then ..." before you touch symbols. Do that in the margin, then translate. Two extra seconds, and the whole question stops being about English.

Worked exampleMethod

Translate each into symbols, with p = "it is raining", q = "the match is cancelled".

(a) "The match is cancelled if it is raining."
Rewrite: if it is raining, then the match is cancelled. So p → q.

(b) "The match is cancelled only if it is raining."
"Only if" marks the conclusion, so rain is required for cancellation. Rewrite: if the match is cancelled, then it is raining. So q → p. Note that this is the reverse of (a), from a sentence that differs by one word.

(c) "Rain is necessary and sufficient for cancellation."
Both directions at once: p ↔ q.

(d) "The match is cancelled whenever it rains."
"Whenever" marks the hypothesis: p → q, the same as (a).

Now redo it: translate "passing the exam is necessary for graduating". Answer below the fold of your hand: let g be graduating and e be passing, then g → e.

What the instruction verbs demand

TeachRead the verb before you start writing

Marks are allocated to the verb, not to the topic. A perfect definition earns nothing when the question said "prove". This table is drawn from the verbs the five papers actually use.

VerbWhat earns the marks
DefineOne precise sentence. No example needed unless asked.
StateThe claim itself, in full, with its conditions. Not a worked instance of it.
ExplainThe idea in your own words, plus why it is so. Usually an example is expected.
Differentiate betweenA stated point of contrast, both sides, on the same axis. A list of two definitions side by side does not differentiate.
Show that / ProveAn argument that settles every case, not a check of one or two.
Determine whetherA verdict plus the reason. A bare yes or no scores almost nothing.
Construct / DrawThe object itself, labelled.
Compute / Calculate / FindThe number, with the working that produced it.
With the aid of examplesThe example is compulsory and carries marks of its own.
Trap"Differentiate" is not "define twice"

The 2025/2026 paper opens with ten marks of "differentiate between the following", five pairs at two marks each. The commonest way to lose half of those ten marks is to write two definitions and stop.

A contrast needs a shared axis. Compare this pair of answers on the two ways of describing a set, from F.1:

Scores poorly: "The roster method lists the elements. Set-builder notation uses a rule."

Scores fully: "Both name exactly the same collection, and either can describe any set that the other can. They differ in what they make easy. The roster method writes the members out, so it is direct but only practical for a small finite set: {2, 4, 6, 8}. Set-builder states the property that decides membership, so it handles infinite sets and sets defined by a condition: {x | x is even and 0 < x < 10}. The roster shows you what is in; set-builder shows you why."

Same facts. The second one answers the verb, because every sentence contrasts the two on a stated axis: what they name, what they make easy, what they show.

How to lay out an answer

Must memoriseThe four-line answer

For anything with a number in it, write these four things, in this order, every time.

1. Given. The values the question handed you, listed.
2. Find. What is being asked, in your own words.
3. Formula or rule. Stated before it is used, with what each letter means.
4. Substitute, work, and answer. With units or a unit of counting.

This is worth real marks even when your arithmetic goes wrong, because a marker can see the method and award it. An unexplained number cannot be given method marks, however correct it is.

Memory hook: Given, Find, Formula, Substitute. Every worked example in this book opens that way, so you will have copied the habit a hundred times before the exam.

TeachThree words this course uses precisely

Definition. A naming. It cannot be true or false, only used or misused. "n! means the product of every whole number from n down to 1" is a definition.

Theorem. A claim that has been proved. It is true, and something else depends on it. "There is no largest prime number" is a theorem: it is not a naming, it asserts something, and it has been established beyond doubt.

Proof. An argument that settles a claim for every case it covers. Checking three examples is not a proof, it is evidence. Part Nine builds proofs properly.

An exam that says "prove" and receives three examples gets the marks for three examples, which is usually zero.

Recall and check

1. Translate "q is necessary for p" into symbols.

2. Translate "p only if q" into symbols.

3. A question says "determine whether 51 is prime". You write "No". How many of the 2 marks should you expect?

4. What is the difference between a definition and a theorem?

Answers

1. p → q. The necessary thing is the conclusion.
2. p → q. "Only if" also marks the conclusion.
3. About one, at best. "Determine whether" wants the verdict and the reason: exhibit what settles it, for instance "no, because 51 = 3 × 17".
4. A definition names something and cannot be true or false. A theorem makes a claim that has been proved true.
