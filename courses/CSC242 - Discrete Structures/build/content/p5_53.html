Part Five · 5.3

Inclusion, Exclusion and the Pigeonhole Principle

When two groups overlap, adding their sizes counts the overlap twice. Subtracting it once is the whole of inclusion and exclusion, and it is worth seven marks on the newest paper.

By the end of this section you will be able to state and apply the inclusion and exclusion principle for two and three sets, read the overlap off a Venn diagram, state the pigeonhole principle in both its forms, and answer the Ibibio and Efik question and the class-size question in full.

Why adding is wrong

TeachThe double count

The sum rule of section 5.1 came with a condition: no outcome may count under two alternatives. Sets of people almost always break that condition, because a person can be in both.

Suppose 30 students take Maths and 25 take Physics. Adding gives 55. But if 10 take both, those 10 were counted once in the 30 and again in the 25, so 55 overstates the class by exactly 10. The true total is 45.

That is the entire idea. Add the parts, then take the overlap back out once, because it went in twice.

Must memoriseInclusion and exclusion, two sets

Section 2.2 introduced this as the counting formula, where it belonged with the set operations. Here it is again, this time with the rearrangements the exam actually asks for.

For any two finite sets A and B,

n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)

Where n(A) is the cardinality of A, the number of things in it, as defined in section 2.1. A ∪ B is the union: everything in A or in B or in both. A ∩ B is the intersection: the things in both, which is the overlap that was counted twice.

The rearrangements you will actually use. Exam questions give you three of the four quantities and ask for the fourth, so learn all three forms, not just the first:

n(A ∩ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∪ B)     [find the overlap]
n(A only) = n(A) − n(A ∩ B)     [strip the overlap out of A]
n(B only) = n(B) − n(A ∩ B)

Memory hook: in, in, out. Put A in, put B in, take the middle out. And a completed answer always satisfies A only + both + B only = n(A ∪ B).

The principle of inclusion and exclusion

The claim. The size of a union is found by adding the sizes of the sets and then removing every overlap that the addition counted more than once. For two sets that means subtracting the single overlap; for more sets the corrections alternate in sign.

In symbols.
n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)
n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) − n(A ∩ B) − n(A ∩ C) − n(B ∩ C) + n(A ∩ B ∩ C)

Conditions. The sets must be finite, or the counts do not exist to be added. Nothing else is required: the sets may overlap in any way at all, which is precisely the point of the principle.

The limiting case. When the sets are disjoint, every overlap term is 0 and the principle collapses to the sum rule of section 5.1: n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B). So the sum rule is not a separate law but the special case of this one where nothing is shared.

Must memoriseInclusion and exclusion, three sets

For three finite sets A, B and C,

n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) − n(A ∩ B) − n(A ∩ C) − n(B ∩ C) + n(A ∩ B ∩ C)

Where n(A), n(B), n(C) are the sizes of the three sets on their own, each counted with no regard to the others. n(A ∩ B) and the other two pair terms are the sizes of each two-way overlap, subtracted because each was counted in two of the singles. n(A ∩ B ∩ C) is the size of the three-way overlap, added back because the singles put it in three times and the pairs took it out three times.

Read the pattern rather than memorising the letters: add the singles, subtract the pairs, add the triple. Signs alternate, and the number of sets in each term goes up by one.

The final term is added back because the elements in all three sets were added three times by the singles and then subtracted three times by the pairs, leaving them counted zero times. One addition restores them.

Memory hook: plus singles, minus pairs, plus triple. Odd-sized groups are added, even-sized groups are subtracted.

The Ibibio and Efik question

As printed2025/2026 · Q3(a)

(a) In a group of 100 students, 72 students can speak Ibibio and 43 students can speak Ibibio. Based on these data, answer the following questions:

(i) Find the number of students who can speak both Ibibio and Efik. (3 marks)

(ii) Find the number of students who can speak Ibibio only. (2 marks)

(iii) Find the number of students who can speak Efik only. (2 marks)

Note on the paper
This question contradicts itself, and it is the most important defect on the paper. The opening sentence names the same language twice: "72 students can speak Ibibio and 43 students can speak Ibibio". Parts (i) to (iii) then ask about Ibibio and Efik, so the second figure must have been meant as Efik. Read literally, the data mention Efik nowhere and 7 marks are unanswerable.

What to do in the hall. Do not stall and do not silently rewrite the question. Write one line: "The second figure is taken as 43 students who can speak Efik, since parts (i) to (iii) ask about Efik and the question is otherwise unanswerable." Then answer on that reading. A stated assumption that is obviously the intended one is normal mathematical practice and protects every mark; a silent one does not.
Break it down
Given
100 students in the group. 72 speak Ibibio. 43 speak Efik, on the stated reading.
Asked
(i) both languages, (ii) Ibibio only, (iii) Efik only. Three different regions of the same diagram.
Hidden assumption
That every one of the 100 speaks at least one of the two, so n(A ∪ B) = 100. The question does not say so, but without it the three parts have no unique answer. State this too.
Marks say
3, 2 and 2. Part (i) is worth more because the other two follow from it in one subtraction each. Get (i) right and the rest are free.
Teaches
This section, 5.3.
Trap
Answering (ii) with 72. That is everyone who speaks Ibibio, including those who also speak Efik. "Only" means the overlap must come out.
Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q3(a)
Given
n(U) = 100, n(I) = 72 speak Ibibio, n(E) = 43 speak Efik.
Find
n(I ∩ E), then n(I only) and n(E only).
Formula
n(I ∪ E) = n(I) + n(E) − n(I ∩ E).

Step 0. State the two readings you are working under.

  1. The second figure of 43 refers to Efik, not Ibibio.
  2. Every student speaks at least one of the two languages, so n(I ∪ E) = 100.

Assumption 2 is forced by the arithmetic as well as by common sense: 72 + 43 = 115, which is 15 more than the group, so at least 15 students must be in both. If some students spoke neither, the overlap would be even larger, and the question gives no way to know how many.

i. Students who speak both

Rearrange the two-set formula to make the overlap the subject:

n(I ∩ E) = n(I) + n(E) − n(I ∪ E)
= 72 + 43 − 100
= 115 − 100
= 15

Answer: 15 students speak both Ibibio and Efik.

ii. Students who speak Ibibio only

The 72 Ibibio speakers include the 15 who also speak Efik. Strip them out:

n(I only) = n(I) − n(I ∩ E) = 72 − 15 = 57

iii. Students who speak Efik only

n(E only) = n(E) − n(I ∩ E) = 43 − 15 = 28

Check, and write the check down. The three regions must fill the group exactly:

57 + 15 + 28 = 100   ✓

Drawing this diagram is worth doing even when the question does not ask for it. It takes twenty seconds and it catches the "only" trap automatically.

Now redo it: in a group of 80, 50 speak Yoruba and 45 speak Hausa, everyone speaking at least one. Both: 50 + 45 − 80 = 15. Yoruba only: 35. Hausa only: 30. Check: 35 + 15 + 30 = 80.

The class-size question

As printed2021/2022 · Q5(b)

(b) In a discrete structures' class, every student is a major in either Computer Science or Maths or both. There of 25 students who major in Computer, while 13 in Maths. If 8 students major in both Computing Science & Maths, how many students are in the entire class? (4marks)

Note on the paper
"There of 25 students" is a typing slip for "There are 25 students", and the subject is called "Computer Science" in one clause, "Computer" in the next and "Computing Science" in the third. All three name the same set. The sentence that saves the question is the first one: every student majors in Computer Science or Maths or both, which states outright the assumption that the Ibibio question left you to supply. So here n(A ∪ B) is the class, and it is what you are asked for.
Break it down
Given
n(C) = 25 major in Computer Science, n(M) = 13 major in Maths, n(C ∩ M) = 8 major in both, and nobody majors in neither.
Asked
How many students are in the entire class, which is n(C ∪ M).
Marks say
4 marks for one formula. Write the principle, then substitute, then evaluate, then answer in a sentence. A bare 30 earns less.
Teaches
This section, 5.3.
Trap
Answering 25 + 13 = 38, forgetting the 8 who were counted in both figures.
Worked examplePQ 21/22 Q5(b)
Given
n(C) = 25, n(M) = 13, n(C ∩ M) = 8, every student in at least one.
Find
n(C ∪ M), the size of the class.
Formula
n(C ∪ M) = n(C) + n(M) − n(C ∩ M).

Because every student majors in one subject or the other or both, the class is the union, so the union is what to compute:

n(C ∪ M) = 25 + 13 − 8
= 38 − 8
= 30

Answer: there are 30 students in the entire class.

Break it into regions as a check: Computer Science only is 25 − 8 = 17, Maths only is 13 − 8 = 5, and both is 8. Then 17 + 5 + 8 = 30, which agrees.

One ambiguity worth a line on the script. The sentence "There of 25 students who major in Computer" could be misread as saying the class has 25 students in total. It cannot mean that: 8 of them major in both, and 13 major in Maths, so Maths-only alone would need 5 more students than a 25-strong class has room for once the 25 Computer Science majors are placed. The reading above is the only consistent one.

Now redo it: if instead 30 major in Computer Science, 20 in Maths and 12 in both, the class has 30 + 20 − 12 = 38 students.

The pigeonhole principle

The pigeonhole principle

The claim. If more objects are placed into boxes than there are boxes, then some box must hold more than one object. Nothing about the objects, the boxes or the way they are placed can prevent it.

In symbols.
If k + 1 or more objects go into k boxes, some box holds at least 2.
If N objects go into k boxes, some box holds at least ⌈N / k⌉.

Conditions. The number of boxes must be finite, and every object must go into some box. It says nothing about which box is crowded, and it cannot: that is a feature, since it lets you prove something exists without being able to find it.

The limiting case. When N = k the principle promises only ⌈k / k⌉ = 1, which is no information at all, and correctly so: k objects can sit one to a box with nothing shared. The principle bites from N = k + 1 onward, and that single extra object is the whole of it.

Must memorisePigeonhole principle

If k + 1 or more objects are placed into k boxes, then at least one box contains two or more objects.

Where k is the number of boxes, sometimes called pigeonholes. objects are whatever is being sorted into them: people, numbers, letters, socks.

The generalised form, which is the one that answers real questions: if N objects are placed into k boxes, then at least one box contains at least

⌈N / k⌉ objects,

where ⌈x⌉ is the ceiling of x: the smallest whole number that is not less than x. So ⌈7 / 3⌉ = ⌈2.33 ⌉ = 3.

Memory hook: more pigeons than holes means sharing. The principle never tells you which box is crowded, only that one of them is. That is exactly why it is useful: it proves something exists without finding it.

TeachUsing it

Every pigeonhole argument has the same three steps, and the marks are in step 2.

  1. Name the objects. What is being placed?
  2. Name the boxes. This is the creative step. The boxes are usually the possible values of something, not physical containers.
  3. Compare the counts and apply the principle.

Example. Among any 13 people, at least two were born in the same month. Objects: the 13 people. Boxes: the 12 months. Since 13 > 12, two people share a box, that is, a birth month.

Example, generalised form. Among 100 people, at least ⌈100 / 12⌉ = ⌈8.33⌉ = 9 share a birth month.

Example with an unobvious box. In any group of 8 people, two have the same number of friends within the group. Objects: 8 people. Boxes: the possible friend counts, 0 to 7, which is 8 boxes, so the counting alone is not enough. The extra step: nobody can have 0 friends and somebody else have 7 at the same time, because a person with 7 friends is friends with everyone, including the supposed loner. So only 7 of the 8 boxes can be occupied, and 8 people into 7 boxes forces a repeat.

Where this stands on the papers: none of the five papers we hold has set a pigeonhole question outright. It is in the official course text, it takes two minutes to learn, and the 2023/2024 paper does ask a closely related impossibility question about five cities that is settled by a counting argument in Part Six. Learn the statement and the three steps; that is the whole of the return on it.
