Part Six · 6.4

Directed Graphs, In-degree and Out-degree

Put an arrowhead on every edge and the degree splits in two: arrows coming in, and arrows going out. Two papers set the same tabulation question on a hand-drawn figure.

By the end of this section you will be able to define a directed graph, compute in-degree and out-degree, state the directed form of the Handshaking theorem, tabulate a digraph vertex by vertex, and answer both tabulation questions and the short note on in-degree and out-degree.

Directed graphs

Must memoriseDigraph, arc, in-degree, out-degree

A directed graph, or digraph, is a graph in which every edge has a direction. A directed edge is called an arc, and it is written as an ordered pair (u, v), drawn as an arrow from u to v.

For a vertex v:

the in-degree deg−(v) is the number of arcs pointing into v;
the out-degree deg+(v) is the number of arcs pointing out of v.

Where (u, v) is an ordered pair, so (u, v) and (v, u) are different arcs. This is the whole difference from section 6.1, where an edge joining u and v was one thing however you named it. deg−(v) counts arrowheads landing on v. deg+(v) counts arrow tails leaving v. a loop at v adds 1 to each, since it both leaves v and arrives at v.

Memory hook: minus is in, plus is out. The superscript minus looks like an arrow flattened as it arrives; the plus is the vertex sending traffic outward. And a mnemonic that never fails: the in-degree counts the arrowheads.

Must memoriseHandshaking for digraphs

In any directed graph,

Σ deg−(v) = Σ deg+(v) = |E|

Where |E| is the number of arcs. the two sums are each equal to |E|, not to 2|E|: every arc has exactly one tail and exactly one head, so it is counted once in each sum rather than twice in one.

How this differs from section 6.3, which is the point most often lost: in an undirected graph the degrees sum to 2|E|; in a digraph the in-degrees sum to |E| and the out-degrees sum to |E|, and adding the two sums together recovers the 2|E|.

Memory hook: one tail and one head per arc. Both column totals of your table must equal the arc count. That is a free check, and it catches a misread arrowhead immediately.

The short note

Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q4(a)(iii)

The question is quoted in full in section 6.1: "(iii) In-degree and Out-degree of a vertex", 2 marks, wanting short notes with relevant examples.

Definition. In a directed graph, the in-degree of a vertex v, written deg−(v), is the number of arcs whose arrowhead lands on v. The out-degree, deg+(v), is the number of arcs whose tail leaves v. A loop at v adds 1 to each.

Example 1. In the figure above, vertex p has one arc arriving (from s) and two leaving (to q and to r), so deg−(p) = 1 and deg+(p) = 2.

Example 2. On a web link graph, a page's in-degree is the number of pages linking to it and its out-degree the number it links out to. Search engines rank on the first and not the second, which is a one-line way of showing you understand the difference matters.

The line worth adding: Σ deg−(v) = Σ deg+(v) = |E|, because every arc has exactly one head and one tail.

Worked exampleMethod · how to tabulate any digraph

The method, in four steps. It works on any figure, including the one on your own paper.

  1. List the vertices first, reading the labels off the drawing and including any vertex with no arcs. Write the count.
  2. Walk the arcs one bundle at a time, writing each as an ordered pair (tail, head). Where several curves join the same pair, count the curves, not the pairs. Write the count.
  3. Build a row per vertex with three columns: arcs out, arcs in, and the two degrees. Fill it from the list in step 2, not from the drawing, so each arc is used exactly once.
  4. Total both degree columns. Each total must equal the arc count from step 2. If they do not, an arc was missed or double counted.

Worked on a clearly drawn example, so the method is visible without an unreadable figure in the way. This figure is mine, not the paper's.

Step 2. Arcs as ordered pairs: (a, b), (b, a), (b, c), (c, d), (d, c), (a, c). Total arcs = 6.

The check. Both totals are 6, which is the arc count from step 2. That is Σ deg+(v) = Σ deg−(v) = |E|, and it is the only verification available in the exam hall. Do it every time.

Now redo it: add an arc (e, a) to the figure. Then |E| = 7, deg+(e) = 1, deg−(a) = 2, and both totals become 7.

TrapNever present an unreadable figure as read

Both of these questions are drawn by hand and scanned. If an arrowhead is genuinely ambiguous on your copy, the professional move is the same one this manual has used: state the reading you are working under, then answer under it. One sentence protects the whole table.

What loses marks is silence. A table with no stated reading and a misread arc looks like a candidate who cannot count, rather than one working from a poor photocopy.

Recall and check

1. What makes a digraph different from an undirected graph?

2. Define in-degree and out-degree.

3. What do the in-degrees sum to, and what do the degrees of an undirected graph sum to?

4. How much does a loop add to each of the two degrees in a digraph?

5. A digraph has 13 arcs. What must each of your two column totals be?

Answers

1. Every edge has a direction, so an arc is an ordered pair and (u, v) differs from (v, u).
2. In-degree counts arcs arriving at the vertex; out-degree counts arcs leaving it.
3. In-degrees sum to |E|. Undirected degrees sum to 2|E|.
4. One to each, since it both leaves and arrives at that vertex.
5. 13 each. If either differs, an arc has been missed or counted twice.
