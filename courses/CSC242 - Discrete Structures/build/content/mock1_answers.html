AnswerMock One Q1 · 17.5 marks

a. Five differences, two examples each (10 marks)

Two marks each, so each item wants a contrasting sentence and then two examples. Writing two definitions side by side and leaving the reader to spot the difference is the standard way to lose half of these marks: see F.2.

i. Tautology and contradiction. A tautology is a compound proposition that is true in every row of its truth table, whereas a contradiction is false in every row. Examples of tautologies: p ∨ ¬p, and (p ∧ q) → p. Examples of contradictions: p ∧ ¬p, and ¬(p ∨ ¬p).

ii. Subset and proper subset. A ⊆ B allows the two sets to be equal, whereas A ⊂ B requires A ⊆ B and A ≠ B, so B must have at least one member A lacks. Examples: {1, 2} ⊆ {1, 2} is true while {1, 2} ⊂ {1, 2} is false; {1} ⊂ {1, 2} and {1} ⊆ {1, 2} are both true.

iii. Injective and surjective. An injective function sends no two different inputs to the same output, whereas a surjective function reaches every element of the codomain. The first is a condition on the domain side, the second on the codomain side, which is why a function can have one without the other. Examples: with A = {1, 2} and B = {a, b, c}, the map 1 → a, 2 → b is injective but not surjective, since c is never reached; with A = {1, 2, 3} and B = {a, b}, the map 1 → a, 2 → a, 3 → b is surjective but not injective, since 1 and 2 share an image.

iv. Euler circuit and Hamilton circuit. An Euler circuit uses every edge exactly once and returns to its start, whereas a Hamilton circuit visits every vertex exactly once and returns to its start. One is about edges and the other about vertices, and neither implies the other. Examples: two triangles joined at a single vertex have an Euler circuit, since every degree is even, but no Hamilton circuit, because the shared vertex would have to be visited twice; K4 has a Hamilton circuit but no Euler circuit, since all four of its vertices have the odd degree 3.

v. Permutation and combination. A permutation counts arrangements, so order matters, whereas a combination counts selections, so order does not; the two differ by the r! orderings of each chosen set. Examples: choosing a president, a secretary and a treasurer from 5 people is P(5, 3) = 60; choosing a committee of 3 from the same 5 people is C(5, 3) = 10, and 60 / 10 = 3! = 6.

b. De Morgan's laws for sets, one verified (2.5 marks)

The laws. For any sets A and B inside a universal set U,

(A ∪ B)′ = A′ ∩ B′   and   (A ∩ B)′ = A′ ∪ B′

In words: the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements. The operation flips and the complement moves inward.

Verifying the first. With U = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}, A = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} and B = {4, 5, 6, 7}:

A ∪ B = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7}, so (A ∪ B)′ = {8}
A′ = {6, 7, 8} and B′ = {1, 2, 3, 8}, so A′ ∩ B′ = {8}

The two sides agree, which is what was to be shown. Compute both sides separately and only then compare them. Working from one side towards the other is how a verification turns into a circular argument.

c. The five results (5 marks)

A ∪ B = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7}
A ∩ B = {4, 5}
A − B = {1, 2, 3}
B − A = {6, 7}
A ∩ B has 2 members, so it has 22 = 4 subsets, namely {}, {4}, {5} and {4, 5}

Two checks worth the ink. A − B and B − A are different, which is the whole reason the two are asked for separately; and |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B| = 5 + 4 − 2 = 7, which is what the first line lists.

AnswerMock One Q2 · 17.5 marks

a. The three-subject class of 120 (7 marks)

Fill the diagram from the middle outward. That single habit answers every question of this kind, and it is the reason the middle figure, 10, is given first.

Working the regions out. Write M, P and C for the three sets. The centre is given as 10. Each two-subject region is the stated overlap less the centre:

M and P only: 25 − 10 = 15
P and C only: 20 − 10 = 10
M and C only: 22 − 10 = 12

Each single-subject region is the whole set less the three regions already placed inside it:

M only: 60 − 15 − 12 − 10 = 23
P only: 55 − 15 − 10 − 10 = 20
C only: 50 − 12 − 10 − 10 = 18

(i) At least one. By inclusion and exclusion for three sets,

n(M ∪ P ∪ C) = 60 + 55 + 50 − 25 − 20 − 22 + 10 = 108

(ii) None. 120 − 108 = 12

(iii) Exactly one. 23 + 20 + 18 = 61

Check: exactly two is 15 + 10 + 12 = 37, exactly three is 10, and 61 + 37 + 10 = 108, which is part (i) again by a different route. The diagram is worth marks in its own right here, so draw it even when the arithmetic alone would do.

b. a R b when a − b is even (5.5 marks)

Listing R first makes the three checks a matter of looking. On {1, 2, 3, 4}, a − b is even exactly when a and b are both odd or both even, so

R = {(1,1), (1,3), (3,1), (3,3), (2,2), (2,4), (4,2), (4,4)}

Reflexive. For every a, a − a = 0, which is even, so (a, a) is in R for all four elements. Yes.

Symmetric. If a − b is even then b − a = −(a − b) is even too, so (b, a) is in R whenever (a, b) is. Yes.

Transitive. If a − b and b − c are both even, then a − c = (a − b) + (b − c) is a sum of two even numbers and therefore even. Yes.

So R is an equivalence relation, and the partition it induces is

{1, 3} and {2, 4}, the odd members and the even members

Naming the classes is the line that shows you know what an equivalence relation is for: it cuts the set into blocks that share a property, and every element lands in exactly one block.

c. The four properties of S, with reasons (5 marks)

S = {(1,1), (1,2), (2,3), (3,4)} on {1, 2, 3, 4}. A property fails the moment one witness fails, and the witness is what earns the mark: a bare "no" scores nothing.

PropertyHolds?The witness
ReflexiveNo(2, 2) is not in S, and reflexivity needs (a, a) for every a
SymmetricNo(1, 2) is in S but (2, 1) is not
AntisymmetricYesthe only pair appearing with its reverse is (1, 1), where the two entries are equal, so nothing violates it
TransitiveNo(1, 2) and (2, 3) are in S but (1, 3) is not

The trap in this part is assuming that failing symmetry means passing antisymmetry, or the reverse. They are independent: S here is neither symmetric nor reflexive yet is antisymmetric, and a relation can be both symmetric and antisymmetric at once, which happens exactly when it contains nothing but loops.

AnswerMock One Q3 · 17.5 marks

a. Arrangements of STATISTICS (3 marks)

Count the letters before anything else. STATISTICS has 10 letters: S three times, T three times, I twice, and A and C once each. Check: 3 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 10.

10! / (3! × 3! × 2!) = 3628800 / (6 × 6 × 2) = 3628800 / 72 = 50400

Why the division. The 10! counts the three S's as though they were distinguishable, so each genuinely distinct arrangement has been counted 3! times over for the S's, 3! times over for the T's and 2! times over for the I's. Dividing by the product removes exactly that overcount.

b. A committee of 5 from 7 men and 6 women (6 marks)

Thirteen people in all. Order does not matter in a committee, so every part is a combination.

(i) No restriction.

C(13, 5) = 13! / (5! × 8!) = 1287

(ii) Exactly 3 women. Three of the 6 women and, to fill the committee, two of the 7 men. The two choices are both compulsory, so multiply:

C(6, 3) × C(7, 2) = 20 × 21 = 420

(iii) At least 4 men. With only 5 seats, "at least 4 men" means exactly 4 or exactly 5. The two cases are alternatives, so add:

C(7, 4) × C(6, 1) = 35 × 6 = 210
C(7, 5) × C(6, 0) = 21 × 1 = 21
total = 210 + 21 = 231

Both rules appear here, and saying which is which is worth a mark: the product rule joins choices that are all made, the sum rule joins cases only one of which happens. Note also that the complement trick used in the 2025/2026 paper does not help in (iii), because "fewer than 4 men" is itself four cases.

c. Five characters, beginning with a letter (3.5 marks)

The alphabet has 26 + 10 = 36 characters. The first position is restricted to the 26 letters; the other four are unrestricted and repetition is allowed, so each has 36 choices. All five choices are made, so the product rule applies:

26 × 364 = 26 × 1679616 = 43670016

Deal with the restricted position first. Doing the free positions first and trying to correct afterwards is where this question goes wrong.

d. The divisors of n (5 marks)

d divides n exactly when n % d is 0, so the whole program is one loop over the candidates from 1 to n with that one test inside it.

In Python, the language to reach for this semester. The loop and the one test n % d == 0 are the whole answer.

divisors.py
# Mock One Question Three (d): every positive divisor of n, on one line.

n = int(input("Enter a positive integer: "))
if n < 1:
    print("n must be positive.")
else:
    divisors = [d for d in range(1, n + 1) if n % d == 0]   # d divides n exactly
    print("Divisors of " + str(n) + ": " + " ".join(map(str, divisors)))
What the Python version printed for n = 28
Enter a positive integer: Divisors of 28: 1 2 4 7 14 28

And the same program in C++.

divisors.cpp
// Mock One Question Three (d): every positive divisor of n, on one line.
#include <iostream>
using namespace std;

int main() {
    int n;
    cout << "Enter a positive integer: ";   // i. prompt
    cin >> n;

    if (n < 1) {                            // ii. guard the precondition
        cout << "n must be positive." << endl;
        return 0;
    }

    cout << "Divisors of " << n << ":";      // iii. head the line
    for (int d = 1; d <= n; d++) {          // iv. test every candidate
        if (n % d == 0) cout << " " << d;     // v. d goes into n exactly
    }
    cout << endl;                            // vi. close the line
    return 0;
}
What it printed for n = 28
Enter a positive integer: Divisors of 28: 1 2 4 7 14 28

So the answer to state is 1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28. The prompt and the answer share a line because the 28 you type is not echoed to the output by the program; that is what the captured run above shows.

What earns the marks on paper. The loop bound d ≤ n and the test n % d == 0 are the two things being examined. Writing d < n loses the divisor n itself, and starting at d = 0 divides by zero. If you have time, add the line that 28 = 1 + 2 + 4 + 7 + 14, so 28 is a perfect number: it costs one sentence and shows you read your own output.

AnswerMock One Q4 · 17.5 marks

a. Five short notes with an example each (10 marks)

Two marks each: one for the definition, one for the example. A note without an example is half an answer here.

i. Complete graph. A simple graph in which every pair of distinct vertices is joined by exactly one edge, written Kn on n vertices; it has C(n, 2) = n(n − 1)/2 edges. Example: K4, a square with both diagonals drawn, has 4 × 3 / 2 = 6 edges.

ii. Bipartite graph. A graph whose vertices split into two sets such that every edge joins a vertex of one set to a vertex of the other, and no edge lies inside either set. Example: K3,3, three houses each joined to three utilities, which has 3 × 3 = 9 edges and no triangle at all.

iii. Regular graph. A graph in which every vertex has the same degree; if that common degree is k it is called k-regular. Example: a cycle on five vertices is 2-regular, and K4 is 3-regular.

iv. Subgraph. A graph H whose vertices are among the vertices of G and whose edges are among the edges of G, with both endpoints of each kept edge also kept. Example: a single triangle inside K4 is a subgraph of it, obtained by deleting one vertex and the three edges at it.

v. Eulerian graph. A connected graph containing a circuit that uses every edge exactly once and returns to its start, which happens exactly when every vertex has even degree. Example: a triangle is Eulerian, each vertex having degree 2; the four-vertex graph of the Konigsberg bridges is not, all four of its vertices having odd degree.

b. Drawing G from its adjacency matrix (4 marks)

Read the matrix above the diagonal only. The matrix is symmetric because the graph is undirected, so the upper triangle holds every edge once and reading the whole matrix double-counts them. The 1's above the diagonal sit at (1,2), (1,3), (2,3), (2,4), (3,4), (3,5) and (4,5), which is 7 edges.

The degree sequence is read off as the row sums:

deg(v1) = 2, deg(v2) = 3, deg(v3) = 4, deg(v4) = 3, deg(v5) = 2, so the sequence written in decreasing order is (4, 3, 3, 2, 2)

Verifying the handshaking theorem. The sum of the degrees is 4 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 14, and twice the number of edges is 2 × 7 = 14. They agree, as the theorem requires, because each edge contributes exactly 1 to each of its two endpoints. Note too that the graph has exactly two vertices of odd degree, and two is even, as the corollary to the theorem demands.

c. The full binary tree with 8 leaves (3.5 marks)

The formula. A full m-ary tree with i internal vertices has n = m·i + 1 vertices in all and l = (m − 1)·i + 1 leaves. Here m = 2 and l = 8.

8 = (2 − 1)·i + 1 = i + 1, so i = 7 internal vertices
n = 2 × 7 + 1 = 15 vertices altogether
edges = n − 1 = 14, since every tree on n vertices has n − 1 edges

Check by adding up: 7 internal + 8 leaves = 15 vertices, which is the n the first formula gave. Two formulas agreeing is a real check; quoting one of them twice is not.

What the table establishes. The last column is T in all eight rows, so the proposition is a tautology. That is exactly what makes hypothetical syllogism a valid rule of inference: whenever both premises p → q and q → r hold, p → r cannot fail. Say that sentence. A table with no conclusion under it answers only half of "say what the table establishes".

Where the F's sit is the interesting part. The conjunction is F in rows 2, 3, 4 and 6, and in every one of those rows the implication is vacuously T whatever p → r does. Only the four rows where both premises hold are doing any work, and in all four p → r is T.

b. Simplifying A·B + A·B′ + A′·B (4 marks)

Steps 4 to 6 are the ones people miss. A + A′·B looks as though nothing can be done with it, because the usual distributive law of ordinary algebra does not run in that direction. AnswerMock One Q6 · 17.5 marks

a. Induction for the sum of squares (6 marks)

Write the claim down and name it before starting, so that the reader knows what P(n) refers to on every later line.

P(n): 12 + 22 + … + n2 = n(n + 1)(2n + 1) / 6

Step 1, the basis. At n = 1 the left side is 12 = 1 and the right side is 1 × 2 × 3 / 6 = 6 / 6 = 1. They agree, so P(1) is true.

Step 2, the inductive hypothesis. Assume P(k) holds for some particular positive integer k, that is

12 + 22 + … + k2 = k(k + 1)(2k + 1) / 6

Step 3, the inductive step. Add the next square to both sides and simplify the right:

12 + … + k2 + (k + 1)2 = k(k + 1)(2k + 1) / 6 + (k + 1)2
= (k + 1)[k(2k + 1) + 6(k + 1)] / 6   (taking out (k + 1)/6)
= (k + 1)(2k2 + k + 6k + 6) / 6
= (k + 1)(2k2 + 7k + 6) / 6
= (k + 1)(k + 2)(2k + 3) / 6

and the claim P(k + 1) reads

(k + 1)(k + 2)(2(k + 1) + 1) / 6 = (k + 1)(k + 2)(2k + 3) / 6

which is the same expression. So P(k) implies P(k + 1).

Conclusion. P(1) is true and P(k) implies P(k + 1) for every k, so by the principle of mathematical induction P(n) is true for every positive integer n.

The step that is worth two marks on its own is factorising 2k2 + 7k + 6 as (k + 2)(2k + 3). Multiply it back out to check: 2k2 + 3k + 4k + 6, which is 2k2 + 7k + 6. Without that factorisation the two sides look different and the proof appears to fail when it has in fact succeeded.

A numerical sanity check costs one line and is worth writing at the end: at n = 4 the sum is 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 = 30, and the formula gives 4 × 5 × 9 / 6 = 180 / 6 = 30.

b. If n² is even then n is even, by contraposition (3 marks)

State the contrapositive first. The claim "if n2 is even then n is even" has contrapositive

if n is odd then n2 is odd

and an implication and its contrapositive are logically equivalent, so proving the one proves the other.

Proof. Let n be odd. Then n = 2k + 1 for some integer k, by the definition of odd. Squaring,

n2 = (2k + 1)2 = 4k2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k2 + 2k) + 1

and 2k2 + 2k is an integer, so n2 has the form 2m + 1 and is odd. The contrapositive holds, and therefore so does the original claim.

Why contraposition and not a direct proof. A direct proof would start from "n2 is even", that is n2 = 2m, and would have to extract information about n from a fact about n2, which needs a square root. The contrapositive starts from a statement about n itself, which can be written down in the form 2k + 1 at once. Choose the direction whose hypothesis is the easier one to write in symbols.

c. Solving an = 6an−1 − 8an−2 (5 marks)

This is linear, homogeneous, of order 2 and with constant coefficients, so the characteristic equation applies. Replace an by rn and divide through by rn−2:

r2 = 6r − 8, that is r2 − 6r + 8 = 0
(r − 2)(r − 4) = 0, so r = 2 or r = 4

Two distinct roots, so the general solution is

an = A · 2n + B · 4n

Fitting the two given terms.

n = 0: A + B = 3
n = 1: 2A + 4B = 10, that is A + 2B = 5
subtracting, B = 2, and then A = 3 − 2 = 1

an = 2n + 2 · 4n

Check against the recurrence itself, which is the check the examiner can see you doing. From the recurrence, a2 = 6 × 10 − 8 × 3 = 60 − 24 = 36. From the formula, 22 + 2 × 42 = 4 + 32 = 36. They agree.

d. Two balls without replacement (3.5 marks)

There are 5 + 7 = 12 balls. "Without replacement" is the signal that the second probability is conditional on the first draw, so the denominator falls from 12 to 11.

(i) Both red.

P(both red) = (5/12) × (4/11) = 20 / 132 = 5 / 33

(ii) At least one blue. "At least one" is the signal for the complement, and the opposite of "at least one blue" is "no blue at all", which is exactly "both red":

P(at least one blue) = 1 − 5 / 33 = 28 / 33

Do not compute (ii) by cases. One blue then one red, one red then one blue, and both blue is three fractions to get right where the complement is one subtraction. In decimals the answers are about 0.152 and 0.848, and those two adding to 1 is the check.
