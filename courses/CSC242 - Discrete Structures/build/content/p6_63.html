Part Six · 6.3

The Handshaking Theorem

One line of arithmetic that every graph must obey. It is asked for by name on three papers, and it silently decides a fourth: the whole of 2024/2025 Question Five turns on it.

By the end of this section you will be able to state and prove the Handshaking theorem, use it to find a missing degree count, use its corollary to prove that a described graph cannot exist, and answer the 17.5-mark Question Five of 2024/2025 in full.

The Handshaking theorem

The claim. In any undirected graph, the sum of the degrees of all the vertices equals twice the number of edges. Counting degrees counts every edge exactly twice, once from each end.

In symbols.
Σ deg(v) = 2|E|, the sum running over every vertex v in V.

The proof, which is one sentence. Every edge has two endpoints, so each edge contributes exactly 1 to the degree of each of its two ends, that is, 2 to the total. Adding over all |E| edges gives 2|E|. A loop is no exception: both of its ends are at the same vertex, which is precisely why a loop is defined to add 2 to that vertex's degree.

Conditions. The graph must be finite and undirected. Loops and parallel edges are allowed, provided a loop counts 2. For a directed graph the corresponding statement is different and is given in section 6.4.

The corollary that does the real work. The number of vertices of odd degree is always even. Since the total 2|E| is even, and the even-degree vertices contribute an even amount, the odd-degree vertices must also contribute an even amount between them, which is only possible if there is an even number of them.

The limiting case. With no edges at all the theorem reads 0 = 0, true and empty. It is at its most useful in the opposite direction: given a degree sum that is odd, no graph with those degrees exists at all, and that impossibility is what 2023/2024 Q5(b) is testing.

Must memoriseHandshaking, and what to do with it

Σ deg(v) = 2|E|

Where Σ is summation, introduced in section F.1: add the quantity that follows over everything listed under it. deg(v) is the degree of vertex v from section 6.1, a loop counting twice. |E| is the number of edges, the size of the graph. 2|E| is even always, whatever the graph, and that single fact is the source of every impossibility proof in this section.

The three ways it is used, and the exam uses all three:

  1. Find the edges from the degrees. |E| = (Σ deg(v)) / 2.
  2. Find a missing degree. If all degrees but one are known and |E| is known, the last one is forced.
  3. Prove something cannot exist. If the degrees a question describes add to an odd number, no such graph exists, and saying so is the answer.

Memory hook: every handshake is counted twice, once by each hand. That is where the name comes from: at a party, the total number of hands shaken is twice the number of handshakes.

Worked examplePQ 21/22 Q4(a) · PQ 23/24 Q5(a)

For 3 marks (2023/2024)

Statement. In any undirected graph, the sum of the degrees of all the vertices is equal to twice the number of edges: Σ deg(v) = 2|E|.

Reason. Each edge has two endpoints and so contributes 1 to the degree of each, that is, 2 to the total. Summing over all edges gives 2|E|.

Example. A triangle has 3 vertices each of degree 2 and 3 edges: 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 = 2 × 3.

For 5.5 marks (2021/2022), add these

The corollary. The number of vertices of odd degree is even.

Why the corollary follows. Split the sum into the even-degree vertices and the odd-degree ones. The even-degree part is even. The whole sum is 2|E|, also even. So the odd-degree part is even too; but a sum of odd numbers is even only when there is an even count of them.

A use. No graph can have exactly three vertices of degree 5 and nothing else, because 5 + 5 + 5 = 15 is odd.

Where the name comes from, worth one line: if each vertex is a person and each edge a handshake, the degree of a person is the number of hands they shook, and the total counts every handshake from both sides.

Now redo it: a graph has 6 vertices each of degree 3. How many edges? Σ deg = 18, so |E| = 18 / 2 = 9.

Proving a graph cannot exist

As printed2023/2024 · Q5(b)

(b) An Airplane pilot has been scheduled for a trip to five (5) different cities. He can travel directly between two cities through the same route.

(i) Can he visit exactly three (3) cities from each of the cities on the flight plan? Compute your answer. (3 marks)

(ii) Support your answer with a diagrammatical illustration. (2 marks)

Note on the paper
The second sentence is doing the work of "every pair of cities is joined by at most one route, and a route may be flown in either direction". That is the paper's way of saying the graph is simple and undirected. Read part (i) as: can a simple undirected graph on 5 vertices have every vertex of degree exactly 3? The word "Compute" is the clue that an arithmetic argument, not a drawing, is what earns the 3 marks.
Break it down
Given
5 cities. Routes are undirected and there is at most one between any pair.
Asked
(i) Can every city connect to exactly 3 others, and compute the answer. (ii) Support it with a diagram.
Marks say
3 and 2. The 3 marks are for the degree-sum computation. Answering only "no" earns almost nothing; the arithmetic is the answer.
Teaches
This section, 6.3.
Trap
Trying to draw it, failing, and concluding it is impossible. Failing to draw something proves nothing about whether it exists. The degree sum proves it.
Worked examplePQ 23/24 Q5(b)
Given
n = 5 vertices (cities), each required to have degree exactly 3, graph simple and undirected.
Find
Whether such a graph exists.
Formula
Σ deg(v) = 2|E|, so the degree sum must be even.

Step 1. Compute the degree sum. If each of the 5 cities connects to exactly 3 others, then every vertex has degree 3, so

Σ deg(v) = 5 × 3 = 15

Step 2. Apply the theorem. By the Handshaking theorem this sum must equal 2|E|, so

2|E| = 15,   giving   |E| = 7.5

Step 3. Conclude. The number of edges must be a whole number, and 7.5 is not. Equivalently, 15 is odd and 2|E| is always even. No such flight plan exists: the pilot cannot visit exactly three cities from each of five cities.

The corollary says the same thing faster, and is worth adding: every one of the 5 vertices would have odd degree, giving 5 odd-degree vertices, but the number of odd-degree vertices is always even.

Part (ii): the illustration. A drawing cannot show something impossible, so show why it fails and what does work instead.

Now redo it: can six cities each connect to exactly three others? Σ deg = 18, even, so |E| = 9 and there is no obstruction. It can be done, for instance as two triangles joined by a perfect matching.

The 2024/2025 Question Five

As printed2024/2025 · Question Five

A graph designed by a 400-level Graphics student consists of 24 vertices and 30 edges. The graph has five vertices of degree 4, seven pendant vertices, and seven vertices of degree 2. All other vertices have a degree of either 3 or 4. After completing the design, the student seeks your assistance as a computer science student to help solve the following questions for further review:

a. How many vertices of degree 4 are there in the given graph? 4 marks

b. What is the total number of vertices of degree 4 in the corresponding complete graph? 1 mark

c. Provide a proof to determine whether the Handshaking Theorem holds for this scenario. 3 marks

d. Write a C++ code snippet to compute the remaining number of vertices that have either degree 3 or 4. 3 marks

e. Demonstrate how the Handshaking Theorem can be applied in Data Communication. 2 marks

f. Prove that if G is a graph with n vertices and n edges, and no vertex has a degree of 0 or 1, then the degree of every vertex in G is 2. 3 marks

g. Explain what it means for two graphs G and H to be isomorphic. 1.5 marks

Note on the paper
This question looks self-contradictory and is not. It tells you there are "five vertices of degree 4" and then asks in part (a) how many vertices of degree 4 there are. The answer is not five, and a candidate who writes five has fallen into the best trap on any of these papers.

The sentence that resolves it is "All other vertices have a degree of either 3 or 4". More degree-4 vertices are hiding in that remainder, and part (a) is asking for the total. Read the preamble as a partial inventory, not a complete one.

Part (d) is a C++ question and is answered in section 11.3, where the code can be compiled and run. Part (g) is answered in section 6.6, where isomorphism is taught. Everything else is answered below.
Break it down
Given
|V| = 24, |E| = 30. Five vertices of degree 4, seven pendant vertices (degree 1 by definition, section 6.1), seven of degree 2. All remaining vertices have degree 3 or 4.
Asked
Seven parts. The whole 17.5-mark question rests on one degree sum, so compute that first and almost everything follows.
Marks say
4 for part (a) alone, which tells you part (a) is not a one-line read-off. If your answer to a 4-mark question is a number copied from the question, re-read the question.
Teaches
This section, 6.3, with (d) in 11.3 and (g) in 6.6.

Answering (a) with 5. Also forgetting that pendant means degree 1: the question never says "degree 1" anywhere, and if you do not know the word you cannot start.
Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q5(a), (b), (c), (e), (f)
Given

Find
(a) the total number of degree-4 vertices; (b) the same count in the corresponding complete graph; (c) a proof that Handshaking holds; (e) an application; (f) a proof about n vertices and n edges.

a. How many vertices of degree 4 are there

Step 1. The total degree. By the Handshaking theorem,

Σ deg(v) = 2|E| = 2 × 30 = 60

Check the whole inventory, and write the check down: 9 × 4 + 1 × 3 + 7 × 2 + 7 × 1 = 36 + 3 + 14 + 7 = 60, which is 2 × 30. The vertices count too: 9 + 1 + 7 + 7 = 24. Both agree.

b. Degree-4 vertices in the corresponding complete graph

State the reading, because "corresponding" is not defined. The complete graph corresponding to this one is the complete graph on the same 24 vertices, K24.

In Kn every vertex is joined to every other, so every vertex has degree n − 1. Here

degree of every vertex of K24 = 24 − 1 = 23

Since 23 ≠ 4, no vertex of K24 has degree 4, so the answer is 0.

Add the alternative reading in one line, since it costs nothing and the phrase is genuinely ambiguous: the only complete graph in which every vertex has degree 4 is K5, which has 5 such vertices. If "corresponding" was meant to point at the complete graph of degree 4, the answer is 5. State whichever you use.

c. Proof that the Handshaking theorem holds here

"Provide a proof to determine whether" means: check it, and show the check.

Left side, the degree sum. Using the full inventory from part (a):

Σ deg(v) = (9 × 4) + (1 × 3) + (7 × 2) + (7 × 1)
= 36 + 3 + 14 + 7
= 60

Right side, twice the edges.

2|E| = 2 × 30 = 60

Conclusion. The two sides agree, so Σ deg(v) = 2|E| and the Handshaking theorem holds for this graph. Add the reason it had to: the theorem holds for every finite undirected graph, so agreement is not a coincidence but a confirmation that the degree inventory in part (a) is consistent. Had the two sides differed, the fault would have been in the inventory, not in the theorem.

e. The Handshaking theorem in Data Communication

Model a network as a graph: each node (router, switch, host) is a vertex, and each physical link between two nodes is an edge. The degree of a node is the number of its ports that are in use.

Application 1: counting cables from port counts. The theorem says the total number of used ports across the network is twice the number of links. So a network of 40 nodes with 6 used ports each needs (40 × 6) / 2 = 120 cables, and a bill of materials that says anything else is wrong.

Application 2: validating a proposed topology. If a design specifies an odd total of used ports, that design cannot be built: no graph has an odd degree sum. This catches a specification error before any cable is bought, which is the practical point.

Application 3: the odd-degree corollary in redundancy planning. In a topology where every node must have an odd number of links, the number of nodes must be even. That constrains what a resilient ring or mesh design can look like.

f. If G has n vertices and n edges and no degree is 0 or 1

To prove: every vertex of G has degree exactly 2.

Proof. By the Handshaking theorem,

Σ deg(v) = 2|E| = 2n

No vertex has degree 0 or 1, so every vertex has degree at least 2. There are n vertices, so

Σ deg(v) ≥ 2 + 2 + … + 2 (n times) = 2n

But the sum equals 2n. So the inequality is an equality.

Now suppose some vertex u had degree 3 or more. Then the other n − 1 vertices still contribute at least 2 each, so

Σ deg(v) ≥ 3 + 2(n − 1) = 2n + 1 > 2n,

contradicting the theorem. Hence no vertex has degree 3 or more, and since none has degree 0 or 1, every vertex has degree exactly 2. □

What such a graph looks like, worth a closing line: a graph in which every vertex has degree 2 is a disjoint union of cycles. With n vertices and n edges and no isolated or pendant vertices, G is a cycle, or several cycles side by side.

Now redo it: a graph has 10 vertices and 12 edges, with four pendant vertices and four of degree 2. What degrees do the remaining two vertices share? Degree sum 24, accounted 4 × 1 + 4 × 2 = 12 over 8 vertices, leaving 2 vertices sharing 12, so both have degree 6.
