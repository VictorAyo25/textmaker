Part Five · 5.4

The Binomial Theorem

Expanding a bracket raised to a power is a counting problem in disguise, and the numbers that come out are the combinations you met in section 5.2.

By the end of this section you will be able to read the binomial coefficient notation, state and apply the binomial theorem, build and use Pascal's triangle, pick out a single term without expanding the whole thing, and explain why the coefficients of a row add to a power of two.

TeachWhere this stands on the papers

Say this plainly before spending time here. None of the five papers we hold sets a binomial theorem question. It is a full module of the official course text, so it is examinable and cheap to learn, but it should be the last thing you revise, not the first.

It earns its place for a second reason: it is the clearest proof that combinations are not just a formula for committees. The coefficients of an expansion are counts of selections, and seeing that makes section 5.2 harder to forget.

The binomial coefficient

Must memoriseBinomial coefficient

The binomial coefficient is the combination count of section 5.2 under a second name and a second notation. For whole numbers n and r with 0 ≤ r ≤ n,

C(n, r) = n! / (r! (n − r)!), also written ( n over r ) and read "n choose r".

Where n is the power the bracket is raised to. r selects which term of the expansion you are looking at, counting from 0. "n choose r" is the spoken form. When a question says "choose", it means this.

Two identities worth knowing on sight.

C(n, r) = C(n, n − r)     [choosing r to keep is choosing n − r to leave]
C(n, 0) = C(n, n) = 1     [one way to take none, one way to take all]

Memory hook: the coefficient counts the ways. The number in front of a term is the number of ways of picking that many copies of one letter from the brackets.

The theorem

Must memoriseBinomial theorem

For any numbers x and y and any whole number n that is not negative,

(x + y)n = C(n, 0) xn + C(n, 1) xn−1 y + C(n, 2) xn−2 y2 + … + C(n, n) yn

The general term, the one to quote when a question asks for a single term rather than the whole expansion, is

term r = C(n, r) xn−r yr,   for r = 0, 1, 2, …, n.

Where r counts from 0, so the expansion has n + 1 terms, not n. the exponents always add to n: as the power of x falls, the power of y rises by the same step.

Memory hook: the powers see-saw and the exponents always sum to n. If a line of your working breaks that rule, it is wrong, and you can see it without checking anything else.

TeachWhy the coefficients are combinations

Write (x + y)3 as three brackets side by side and do not collect anything yet:

(x + y)(x + y)(x + y)

Multiplying out means going through the three brackets and taking either x or y from each. Every choice gives one product of three letters, and every such product is a term.

How many of those products come out as x2y? Exactly as many as there are ways of picking which one of the three brackets supplied the y, and that is C(3, 1) = 3. So the x2y term is 3x2y, and it is a combination count, not a coincidence.

(x + y)3 = x3 + 3x2y + 3xy2 + y3

This also explains the second identity above. Choosing which brackets give y is the same job as choosing which give x, so C(3, 1) = C(3, 2) and the coefficients read the same forwards and backwards.

Pascal's triangle

TeachGetting the coefficients without factorials

Write C(n, r) in rows, n going down and r across. Each row starts and ends with 1, and every other entry is the sum of the two directly above it. That triangle is the fastest way to get the coefficients for small n, which is all any exam will ask for.

The rule behind the triangle, called Pascal's identity, is

C(n, r) = C(n − 1, r − 1) + C(n − 1, r)

and it says something sensible about choosing. Fix one particular object. Any selection of r either takes that object, leaving r − 1 to pick from the other n − 1, or it does not, leaving all r to pick from the other n − 1. Those two cases are alternatives with no overlap, so the sum rule of section 5.1 adds them.

Must memoriseThe row sum

The coefficients of row n add to 2n:

C(n, 0) + C(n, 1) + … + C(n, n) = 2n

Where n is the row number, counting from 0 at the apex of the triangle. C(n, r) is the entry r places along that row, also counting from 0. 2n is 2 multiplied by itself n times, so row 4 adds to 16 and row 5 to 32.

Why, in one line. Put x = 1 and y = 1 in the binomial theorem. The left side becomes (1 + 1)n = 2n and every power on the right becomes 1, leaving just the coefficients.

And what it means. C(n, r) counts the subsets of size r of a set with n elements, so adding over every r counts all the subsets. That is 2n, which is exactly the size of the power set from section 2.3. Two sections of this book, arrived at from opposite directions, meeting on the same number.

Memory hook: every row doubles. 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. If your coefficients do not add to the next power of two, one of them is wrong.

Using it

Worked exampleFull expansion

Expand (x + 2)4.

Given
The bracket x + 2 raised to the power 4, so n = 4 and the second term is y = 2.
Find
The expansion in full.
Formula

Step 1. Read the coefficients off row 4: 1, 4, 6, 4, 1.

Check it, and this check costs one line. Put x = 1 on both sides. The left is (1 + 2)4 = 34 = 81, and the right is 1 + 8 + 24 + 32 + 16 = 81. They agree, so no coefficient was dropped.

Now redo it: expand (x + 1)5. Row 5 is 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1 and every power of 1 is 1, so it is x5 + 5x4 + 10x3 + 10x2 + 5x + 1. Check at x = 1: the sum is 32 = 25.

Worked exampleOne term only

Find the coefficient of x3 in (2x + 1)5.

Expanding all six terms would work and would waste four minutes. Use the general term instead and solve for r.

Given
n = 5, first term 2x, second term 1.
Find
The number multiplying x3.
Formula
term r = C(5, r) (2x)5−r 1r.

Step 1. Find which r gives x3. The power of x in term r is 5 − r, so

5 − r = 3,   so   r = 2

Step 2. Evaluate that one term.

term 2 = C(5, 2) (2x)3 12
= 10 × 8x3 × 1
= 80x3

Answer: the coefficient of x3 is 80.

The mistake to avoid is taking the coefficient to be C(5, 2) = 10 and forgetting that the 2 in 2x is also raised to the power 3. Whenever the bracket has a number attached to a letter, that number carries its own power.

Now redo it: the coefficient of x2 in (x + 3)4. Here 4 − r = 2 so r = 2, and the term is C(4, 2) x2 32 = 6 × 9 x2 = 54x2.

Recall and check

1. State the binomial theorem.

2. How many terms has the expansion of (x + y)7?

3. Write row 4 of Pascal's triangle and say what its entries add to.

4. Expand (x + 2)4.

5. Why is C(n, r) = C(n, n − r)?

Answers

1. (x + y)n is the sum over r of C(n, r) xn−r yr, for r from 0 to n.
2. Eight. The terms run r = 0 to r = 7, so there are n + 1 of them.
3. 1, 4, 6, 4, 1, adding to 16 = 24.
4. x4 + 8x3 + 24x2 + 32x + 16.
5. Choosing which r objects to take is the same act as choosing which n − r to leave behind, so the two counts are equal.
