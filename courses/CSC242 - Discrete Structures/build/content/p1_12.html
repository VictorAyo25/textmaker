Implication, Converse, Inverse, Contrapositive

One implication has three relatives. Exactly one of them says the same thing as the original, and every paper in our set asks you which.

By the end of this section you will be able to form the converse, inverse and contrapositive of any conditional statement, in English or in symbols, and prove which one preserves truth value.

The three relatives

TeachBuilt from one implication

Start with a conditional statement p → q. There are exactly three ways to rearrange it, and each has a name.

The converse swaps the two parts: q → p.

The inverse negates both parts, keeping the order: ¬p → ¬q.

The contrapositive does both, swap and negate: ¬q → ¬p.

Watch what happens to a concrete case. Let p be "it is a dog" and q "it is an animal", so p → q reads "if it is a dog then it is an animal", which is true.

Originalp → qIf it is a dog, it is an animal.yes
Converseq → pIf it is an animal, it is a dog.no, a cat is a counterexample
Inverse¬p → ¬qIf it is not a dog, it is not an animal.no, the same cat

Must memoriseThe one that matters

For any conditional statement p → q:

converse = q → p
inverse = ¬p → ¬q
contrapositive = ¬q → ¬p

Only the contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original. The converse and the inverse are equivalent to each other, and neither follows from the original.

Memory hook: Converse Changes order. Inverse Inverts (negates) both. Contrapositive does both, and doing both is the only safe operation. In the dog example: knowing it is not an animal really does tell you it is not a dog.

TeachProving it rather than asserting it

The 2024/2025 paper does not ask you to state this, it asks you to prove it, for six marks. A truth table is the proof.

Read the columns, not the rows. The column for p → q is T, F, T, T. The column for the contrapositive ¬q → ¬p is T, F, T, T: identical, so they are equivalent.

The converse column is T, T, F, T and the inverse column is T, T, F, T: identical to each other, and different from the original in rows 2 and 3. So the converse and inverse are equivalent to each other and neither is equivalent to p → q. That single table proves every claim in this section at once.

The real question

As printed2024/2025 · Q1(c)

c. Consider the conditional statement:

"The home team wins whenever it is raining."

Let p represent: "It is raining."

Let q represent: "The home team wins."

i.       Using this, find the following: the contrapositive, the converse, and the inverse of the statement. 3 marks

ii.       Prove that only the contrapositive of a conditional statement p → q has the same truth value as the original conditional statement. Use the statement from c(i) in your explanation. 6 marks

Note on the paper
The paper hands you the letters, which is a gift: p is the rain, not the win. Read that assignment twice before writing, because the sentence puts the win first and the rain second, while the letters run the other way.
Break it down
Given
The sentence "The home team wins whenever it is raining.", with p = it is raining and q = the home team wins.
Asked
Part i: find three rearrangements, in English. Part ii: prove a general claim, then illustrate it with this sentence. "Prove" means a truth table or a full argument, not an assertion.
Marks say
3 marks for three statements is one each, so short English sentences suffice. 6 marks for the proof is the bulk of the question: that is a full table with the columns compared and a conclusion written out.
Teaches
This section, 1.2, and 1.1 for the table method.
Trap
"Whenever" marks the hypothesis, so the statement is p → q (rain implies win), not q → p. Getting that backwards inverts all three answers in part i and costs the lot.
Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q1(c)
Given
p = "It is raining", q = "The home team wins", and the sentence "The home team wins whenever it is raining."
Find
The contrapositive, converse and inverse, then a proof that only the contrapositive preserves truth value.
Rule
converse q → p, inverse ¬p → ¬q, contrapositive ¬q → ¬p.

Step 1. Get the direction right. "The home team wins whenever it is raining" rewrites as "if it is raining, then the home team wins". So the statement is p → q.

Step 2 (part i). Apply each definition.

NameSymbolsIn English
Contrapositive¬q → ¬pIf the home team does not win, then it is not raining.
Converseq → pIf the home team wins, then it is raining.
Inverse¬p → ¬qIf it is not raining, then the home team does not win.

Step 3 (part ii). Prove it with a truth table. Build every column and compare them against p → q.

pq¬p¬qp → q¬q → ¬pq → p¬p → ¬q
TTFFTTTT
TFFTFFTT
FTTFTTFF
FFTTTTTT

The p → q column reads T, F, T, T. The contrapositive column reads T, F, T, T. They agree in every row, so p → q ≡ ¬q → ¬p. The converse reads T, T, F, T and disagrees with the original in rows 2 and 3, and the inverse matches the converse rather than the original.

Step 4. Say it in words, using their sentence. Suppose it rains and the home team loses. That single situation makes the original false, because the promise was broken. It also makes the contrapositive false: the team did not win, yet it was raining. The two statements fail together and hold together, which is what equivalence means.

Now the converse. Suppose the home team wins on a dry day. The original is untouched, since it only ever promised something about rainy days, so it stays true. But the converse, "if the home team wins then it is raining", is false. One statement true, the other false, so they are not equivalent. The inverse fails on that same dry, winning day.

Conclusion. Of the three rearrangements only the contrapositive always carries the same truth value as the original.

The rest of that question

As printed2024/2025 · Q1(a)

a.       State the converse, contrapositive, and inverse of each of these conditional statements.

i. If it snows tonight, then I will stay at home.

ii. I go to the beach whenever it is a sunny summer day.

Break it down
Given
Three English sentences, each hiding an implication in a different disguise: a plain "if ... then", a "whenever", and a "necessary that".
Asked
State three rearrangements of each, so nine sentences in all. English is fine; symbols are not required but help you avoid errors.
Marks say
4.5 marks over nine statements is half a mark each. Write them as short sentences and move on; there are no marks for commentary here.
Teaches
This section for the three forms, F.2 for decoding "whenever" and "necessary".
Trap
All three sentences put the pieces in a different order from p → q. Identify p and q explicitly, in the margin, before writing a single answer.
Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q1(a)

(i) "If it snows tonight, then I will stay at home."
Already in standard form. p = it snows tonight, q = I will stay at home.

Converse: If I stay at home tonight, then it will snow.
Inverse: If it does not snow tonight, then I will not stay at home.
Contrapositive: If I do not stay at home tonight, then it will not snow.

(ii) "I go to the beach whenever it is a sunny summer day."
"Whenever" marks the hypothesis. p = it is a sunny summer day, q = I go to the beach. Rewritten: if it is a sunny summer day, then I go to the beach.

Converse: If I go to the beach, then it is a sunny summer day.
Inverse: If it is not a sunny summer day, then I do not go to the beach.
Contrapositive: If I do not go to the beach, then it is not a sunny summer day.

(iii) "When I stay up late, it is necessary that I sleep until noon."
"When" marks the hypothesis and "it is necessary that" marks the conclusion. p = I stay up late, q = I sleep until noon. Rewritten: if I stay up late, then I sleep until noon.

Converse: If I sleep until noon, then I stayed up late.
Inverse: If I do not stay up late, then I do not sleep until noon.
Contrapositive: If I do not sleep until noon, then I did not stay up late.

Now redo it: nine sentences, blank paper, four minutes. If any of yours reverses the roles of p and q, go back to F.2 and re-read the phrase table.

TrapNegating carelessly

The negation of "I will stay at home" is "I will not stay at home". It is not "I will go out", however natural that sounds. Staying at home and going out might not exhaust the possibilities, and logic only knows what you wrote down.

Similarly, the negation of "x > 3" is "x ≤ 3", not "x < 3". Dropping the equality case is the commonest numeric slip on this topic.

Recall and check

1. Which one of the three relatives is equivalent to the original?

2. Write the contrapositive of "if n is even then n² is even".

3. The converse is equivalent to which other statement?

4. What is the negation of "x ≥ 5"?

Answers

1. The contrapositive.
2. If n² is not even, then n is not even.
3. The inverse. Both are T, T, F, T.
4. x < 5.
