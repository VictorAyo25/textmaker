Part Six · 6.6

Isomorphism, Euler and Hamilton

Three questions about the same drawing: is this the same graph as that one, can I walk every edge once, and can I visit every vertex once. Only the middle one has an easy test, and knowing that is half the marks.

By the end of this section you will be able to define graph isomorphism and use invariants to disprove it, define Euler paths and circuits and apply Euler's test, define Hamilton paths and circuits and say why no simple test exists, and answer both short notes and the isomorphism part of 2024/2025 Question Five.

Isomorphism

Must memoriseGraph isomorphism

Two graphs G and H are isomorphic, written G ≅ H, when there is a bijection f from the vertices of G to the vertices of H such that

u and v are adjacent in G exactly when f(u) and f(v) are adjacent in H.

Where f is a bijection, that is, one-to-one and onto, as defined in section 4.2. So G and H must have the same number of vertices before anything else can be checked. "exactly when" is two conditions in one: f must carry edges to edges and non-edges to non-edges. ≅ is read "is isomorphic to".

In plain words: the two graphs are the same graph with the vertices renamed and the picture redrawn. Nothing about position, length or crossing matters; only which vertices are joined.

Memory hook: same structure, different labels. An isomorphism is a relabelling that preserves every join and every non-join.

Must memoriseInvariants: how to disprove isomorphism

An invariant is a property that isomorphic graphs must share. If two graphs differ on any invariant, they are not isomorphic, and naming the invariant is a complete proof.

  • the number of vertices;
  • the number of edges;
  • the degree sequence, that is, the list of all degrees in order;
  • the number of components, and whether the graph is connected;
  • the length of the shortest cycle, and whether any cycle exists at all;
  • whether the graph is bipartite.

Section 6.7 adds one more to this list once it has the vocabulary for it.

The asymmetry that the exam rewards. Invariants can only ever disprove. Two graphs agreeing on every invariant in the list above may still fail to be isomorphic, so to prove isomorphism you must exhibit the bijection itself and check it. Say that sentence in any answer about isomorphism; it is the difference between knowing the definition and understanding it.

Memory hook: invariants say no, only a mapping says yes. Count vertices, count edges, sort the degrees. If any of the three differ, stop and write the answer.

Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q5(g) · PQ 25/26 Q4(a)(iv)

The two questions are quoted in full in sections 6.3 and 6.1: 2024/2025 Q5(g) asks you to "Explain what it means for two graphs G and H to be isomorphic" for 1.5 marks, and 2025/2026 Q4(a)(iv) asks for a short note on "Graph isomorphism" with a relevant example for 2 marks. One answer serves both; give the example either way.

The explanation. Two graphs G and H are isomorphic when there is a one-to-one and onto function f from the vertices of G to the vertices of H such that two vertices u and v are adjacent in G if and only if f(u) and f(v) are adjacent in H. Informally, H is G with its vertices renamed and its picture redrawn: the two are the same graph in every respect except the labels and the geometry.

What must match, and what need not. The number of vertices, the number of edges and the degree of every corresponding vertex must match. The positions of the vertices on the page, the lengths of the edges and whether the edges cross need not: none of those is part of what a graph is.

The example. The square a, b, c, d and the diamond w, x, y, z above are isomorphic under a to w, b to x, c to y, d to z. Check it edge by edge: ab goes to wx, bc to xy, cd to yz, da to zw, and all four are edges of H. The two drawings look different and are the same graph.

The extra line for the full 1.5 marks: to disprove isomorphism it is enough to find one invariant on which the graphs differ, such as the degree sequence; but to prove it, the bijection must actually be exhibited, because agreeing on every standard invariant is not sufficient.

Now redo it: are a 4-cycle and a path on 4 vertices isomorphic? No. Both have 4 vertices, but the cycle has 4 edges and degree sequence 2, 2, 2, 2, while the path has 3 edges and degree sequence 1, 2, 2, 1. Either invariant settles it.

Euler paths and circuits

Must memoriseEuler path and Euler circuit

An Euler path is a walk that uses every edge of the graph exactly once. An Euler circuit is an Euler path that finishes where it started.

A graph that has an Euler circuit is called an Eulerian graph.

Where every edge exactly once is the condition. Vertices may be revisited as often as you like; it is the edges that are rationed. circuit means the walk returns to its starting vertex; a path need not.

Memory hook: Euler is about edges, Hamilton is about vertices. Both names begin with a letter that helps: Euler for edges. Get that one association right and neither definition can be confused again.

Euler's theorem on circuits and paths

The claim. Whether a graph can be traced without lifting the pen or repeating an edge depends only on how many of its vertices have odd degree, and on nothing else.

In symbols.
A connected graph has an Euler circuit exactly when every vertex has even degree.
A connected graph has an Euler path but no circuit exactly when it has exactly two vertices of odd degree, and any such path must start at one of them and finish at the other.

Conditions. The graph must be connected, apart from isolated vertices, which no walk needs to reach. Loops and parallel edges are allowed. Note that "exactly one odd vertex" never arises: by the Handshaking corollary of section 6.3, the number of odd-degree vertices is always even.

Why the degree condition is the right one. Every time a walk passes through a vertex it uses one edge to arrive and one to leave, so it consumes edges there in pairs. A vertex of odd degree therefore cannot be passed through cleanly: the walk must either start or finish there. With no odd vertices the walk can close up into a circuit; with two it runs between them; with four or more it cannot be a single walk at all.

The limiting case. A graph with no edges satisfies the condition vacuously and has an empty Euler circuit, which is true and of no use. The theorem earns its keep in the negative direction: the seven bridges of Königsberg form a graph with four odd vertices, so no walk crosses every bridge once, and that impossibility is the result the whole subject grew from.

Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q4(a)(v)

The question is quoted in full in section 6.1: "(v) Eulerian graph", 2 marks, wanting a short note with a relevant example.

Definition. A connected graph is Eulerian when it contains an Euler circuit, that is, a closed walk using every edge of the graph exactly once. By Euler's theorem a connected graph is Eulerian exactly when every one of its vertices has even degree.

Example 1, Eulerian. The 4-cycle drawn above: every vertex has degree 2, which is even, so the circuit a to b to c to d to a uses all four edges once and returns to the start.

Example 2, not Eulerian. The seven bridges of Königsberg, the problem Euler solved in 1736 and the origin of the whole subject. Four land masses joined by seven bridges give a graph whose four vertices all have odd degree (3, 3, 3 and 5), so no walk crosses every bridge exactly once. The townspeople had been trying; Euler proved it could not be done.

The distinction worth adding for the second mark: a graph with exactly two odd vertices has an Euler path but no circuit, and is therefore not Eulerian. Only the all-even case earns the name.

Hamilton paths and circuits

Must memoriseHamilton path and circuit

A Hamilton path is a walk that visits every vertex of the graph exactly once. A Hamilton circuit is a Hamilton path that returns to its starting vertex.

Where every vertex exactly once is the condition, so here it is the vertices that are rationed and edges may be left unused entirely. the starting vertex of a circuit is visited twice in the sense that the walk returns to it, which is not counted as a repeat visit.

There is no easy test, and saying so is part of the answer. Euler circuits are settled by counting degrees. For Hamilton circuits no such simple criterion is known, and deciding whether one exists is one of the classic computationally hard problems. Only sufficient conditions exist, such as Dirac's: a simple graph on n ≥ 3 vertices in which every vertex has degree at least n/2 has a Hamilton circuit. That is a one-way test, so a graph failing it may still have one.

Memory hook: Euler counts degrees, Hamilton counts on luck. If an exam question asks you to find a Hamilton circuit, you are expected to find it by inspection; if it asks whether one exists in general, the answer involves saying there is no efficient test.

Worked exampleMethod · deciding both for one graph
Given
The four-vertex graph of section 6.5: vertices 1, 2, 3, 4 with edges 1-2, 1-3, 2-3 and 3-4.
Find
Whether it has an Euler circuit, an Euler path, and a Hamilton path or circuit.
Rule
Euler by counting odd degrees; Hamilton by inspection.

Step 1. The degrees, read off the adjacency matrix row sums in section 6.5: deg(1) = 2, deg(2) = 2, deg(3) = 3, deg(4) = 1.

Step 2. Count the odd ones. Vertices 3 and 4 have odd degree, and the other two are even. That is exactly two odd vertices.

Step 3. Apply Euler's theorem. Exactly two odd vertices means no Euler circuit, but an Euler path exists, and it must run between the two odd vertices, that is, between 3 and 4. One such path:

4 to 3 to 1 to 2 to 3

Check it: the edges used are 34, 31, 12, 23. That is all four edges, each once. Vertex 3 is visited twice, which an Euler path is allowed to do.

Step 4. Hamilton, by inspection. A Hamilton path must touch each of 1, 2, 3, 4 exactly once. Try 4 to 3 to 1 to 2: every vertex once, so a Hamilton path exists.

A Hamilton circuit would have to return from 2 to 4, and there is no edge 2-4. In fact vertex 4 has degree 1, and a circuit must both enter and leave every vertex, needing degree at least 2. So no Hamilton circuit exists, and the pendant vertex is the whole reason.

That last observation generalises and is worth remembering: a graph with any vertex of degree 1 can never have a Hamilton circuit.

Now redo it: add the edge 2-4. Degrees become 2, 3, 3, 2, so still exactly two odd vertices and still an Euler path but no circuit. Now 1 to 2 to 4 to 3 to 1 is a Hamilton circuit, so the answers to the two questions moved independently.

Answers

1. A bijection between the vertex sets under which two vertices are adjacent in one graph exactly when their images are adjacent in the other.
2. Vertex count, edge count, degree sequence (also connectedness, shortest cycle, bipartiteness). They can disprove isomorphism but never prove it; only exhibiting the bijection proves it.
3. A connected graph has an Euler circuit exactly when every degree is even, and an Euler path but no circuit exactly when exactly two degrees are odd.
4. A connected graph that has an Euler circuit, equivalently one whose every vertex has even degree.
5. Euler uses every edge once and may repeat vertices; Hamilton visits every vertex once and may skip edges.
6. A circuit must enter and leave every vertex it visits, which needs degree at least 2.
