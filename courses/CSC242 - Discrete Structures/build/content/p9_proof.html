Part Nine · 9.1

Proof Techniques

A proof is not a feeling that something is true. It is a chain of steps from things already agreed to the thing claimed, where every link can be checked by someone who does not trust you. This section gives you the six chains this course uses, and the vocabulary the examiner uses to ask for them.

By the end of this section you will be able to say what a theorem, lemma, corollary, proposition, conjecture and axiom each are; write a trivial, vacuous, direct, contrapositive, contradiction or cases proof and choose between them; use the definitions of even, odd, rational and divides fluently; and lay a proof out so a marker can follow it line by line.

TeachWhat is being asked for

When a question says prove, show that, establish or demonstrate, it wants an argument that holds for every case covered by the claim, not one example. One example proves nothing except an existence claim.

The mirror image matters just as much. To disprove a claim about all cases you need exactly one counterexample, and no more. Section 3.2 already used this: a single pair (1, 2) with (2, 1) missing kills symmetry outright.

The claim saysTo prove itTo disprove it
For all x, P(x)an argument about an arbitrary xone counterexample
There exists x with P(x)one example (a witness)an argument about an arbitrary x

That table is just the quantifier negation of section 1.4 read as advice: the negation of "for all" is "there exists ... not", so disproving a universal claim is proving an existential one, and finding a witness is the whole job.

The vocabulary of provable things

Must memoriseTheorem, lemma, corollary and the rest

These words are not interchangeable, and a question that asks you to "differentiate" between them wants the distinction, not a definition of each in isolation.

WordWhat it isThe distinguishing feature
Axioma statement taken as true without proofthe starting point; nothing is behind it
Theorema statement shown to be trueimportant in its own right
Propositiona statement shown to be truetrue, proved, but of lesser importance
Lemmaa statement proved in order to prove something elsea helper; it exists to serve a later proof
Corollarya statement that follows at once from a theoremcomes after, and costs almost no extra work
Conjecturea statement believed true but not yet provedno proof exists yet; it may still be false

Memory hook: a lemma comes before, a corollary comes after, and a conjecture has not arrived at all. The theorem is the thing in the middle that everyone remembers.

Worth one line in an answer. A conjecture that is proved becomes a theorem; a conjecture that is disproved becomes nothing at all. The word records the state of our knowledge, not a property of the statement.

The definitions every proof in this course leans on

Must memoriseEven, odd, rational, divides

Almost every proof set on this course is unlocked by writing one of these four definitions down. If you are stuck, the move is nearly always "replace the word by its definition".

WordDefinition
m is evenm = 2k for some integer k
n is oddn = 2k + 1 for some integer k
n is rationaln = p / q for some integers p and q with q ≠ 0
a divides b, written a | bb = ka for some integer k
Where k, p, q are integers, and the phrase "for some integer k" is doing real work: it says such a k exists, and the proof's next move is always to name it and use it. a | b is read "a divides b", and it points the opposite way to a fraction: a | b means b is the multiple, a is the divisor. The bar is not a division sign and the statement is not a number, it is either true or false. ≠ means "is not equal to"; q ≠ 0 because division by zero has no value.

An integer is either even or odd, never both and never neither. That sentence is what makes a proof by cases on parity exhaustive, and it is worth stating explicitly when you use it.

Memory hook: write the definition, then do algebra. Nine proofs in ten on this course are that and nothing more.

The six strategies

Must memoriseSix ways to prove p → q

Every strategy below proves the same shape of statement, the conditional p → q of section 1.2. They differ in what you assume at the start.

Where p is the hypothesis, also called the premise or antecedent: what you are given. q is the conclusion, also called the consequent: what you must reach. ¬q is the negation of q, from section 1.1. r ∧ ¬r is a contradiction: some statement r and its own negation, both derived. Section 1.3 showed that is false in every row of its table, which is exactly why deriving one destroys the assumption that produced it.

Memory hook: direct goes forwards, contrapositive goes backwards, contradiction goes nowhere and shows the alternative is impossible.

TeachWhy contrapositive and contradiction are allowed

Neither is a trick. Both rest on a logical equivalence proved back in section 1.3, and quoting that equivalence by name is worth a mark.

Contrapositive. p → q ≡ ¬q → ¬p. The two have identical truth tables, so proving either one proves the other. Section 1.2 built that table and 2024/2025 Q1(c)(ii) asked you to prove it; the proof lives there, and this section is where you spend it.

Contradiction. If assuming p ∧ ¬q leads to something false, then p ∧ ¬q is itself false, so its negation ¬p ∨ q is true, and that is exactly p → q written the other way (section 1.3, the implication law).

Contrapositive is a special case of contradiction, which is why the two are so often confused. If you assume ¬q and reach ¬p while p was also assumed, you have p ∧ ¬p, a contradiction. The practical difference is in the writing: a contrapositive proof is short and clean, a contradiction proof carries an extra assumption all the way through. Prefer the contrapositive when it is available.

Direct proofs, worked

Worked exampleMethod · three direct proofs

1. The sum of two odd integers is even.

Proof. Let m and n be arbitrary odd integers. By the definition of odd there are integers a and b with

m = 2a + 1   and   n = 2b + 1

Then

m + n = (2a + 1) + (2b + 1)
        = 2a + 2b + 2
        = 2(a + b + 1)

Since a + b + 1 is an integer, m + n is twice an integer, so m + n is even. □

2. If n is even then n2 is even.

Proof. Let n be an arbitrary integer and suppose n is even. Then n = 2k for some integer k, so

n2 = (2k)2 = 4k2 = 2(2k2)

and 2k2 is an integer, so n2 is even. □

3. Divisibility is transitive: if a | b and b | c then a | c.

Proof. Let a, b, c be integers and assume a | b and b | c. By the definition of divides there are integers k and j with

b = ka   and   c = jb

Substituting the first into the second, c = j(ka) = (jk)a. Since jk is an integer, a | c. □

Notice the shape all three share. Assume, replace each word by its definition, do ordinary algebra, then read the definition backwards to name what you have reached. That is the entire method, and the last sentence, "since ... is an integer", is the one candidates leave out.

TrapTwo integers, two letters

Writing m = 2a + 1 and n = 2a + 1 with the same letter says m and n are the same number. The proof then only covers the case m = n and is worth almost nothing. Two independent objects get two independent letters, every time.

The same slip in reverse: proving something for "an arbitrary even integer n = 2k" and then later writing k = 3 to check. Checking is fine, but say you are checking. A proof that fixes its arbitrary letter to a number has stopped being a proof.

Contrapositive, worked

Worked exampleMethod · contrapositive

1. If mn is even, then m is even or n is even.

Why not direct? Assuming mn = 2k tells you almost nothing about m and n separately. But the negation of the conclusion is clean: "m is not even and n is not even" means both are odd, and odd numbers have a formula. That is the signal to flip.

The contrapositive. If m is odd and n is odd, then mn is odd. Note that negating "m is even or n is even" gives "m is odd and n is odd", by De Morgan's law of section 1.3. Getting that "and" is where the mark is.

Proof. Suppose m and n are both odd, so m = 2a + 1 and n = 2b + 1 for some integers a and b. Then

mn = (2a + 1)(2b + 1)
      = 4ab + 2a + 2b + 1
      = 2(2ab + a + b) + 1

and 2ab + a + b is an integer, so mn is odd. The contrapositive holds, therefore the original statement holds. □

2. If x3 − 7x2 + x − 7 = 0, then x ≠ 10.

The contrapositive. If x = 10, then x3 − 7x2 + x − 7 ≠ 0.

Proof. Substitute x = 10:

103 − 7 × 102 + 10 − 7 = 1000 − 700 + 10 − 7 = 303

and 303 ≠ 0. □

Worth an extra line. The expression factorises as x2(x − 7) + (x − 7) = (x2 + 1)(x − 7), and x2 + 1 is never zero for real x, so the only real solution is x = 7. Saying so shows you understood the question rather than only answered it.

3. If 5a + 25b = 156, then a or b is not an integer.

The contrapositive. If a and b are both integers, then 5a + 25b ≠ 156.

Proof. Suppose a and b are integers. Then

5a + 25b = 5(a + 5b)

and a + 5b is an integer, so 5a + 25b is a multiple of 5. But 156 = 5 × 31 + 1 leaves remainder 1, so 156 is not a multiple of 5. Hence 5a + 25b ≠ 156. □

Now redo it: show that if 6a + 9b = 100 then a or b is not an integer. Same move: 6a + 9b = 3(2a + 3b) is a multiple of 3, and 100 = 3 × 33 + 1 is not.

Contradiction, worked

Worked exampleMethod · contradiction

1. There is no largest integer.

Why contradiction? The claim is a denial: it says a thing does not exist. There is nothing to build forwards from, so assume the thing does exist and watch it break.

Proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that there is a largest integer, and call it N. Then N + 1 is also an integer, and N + 1 > N, so N is not the largest after all. That contradicts the assumption, which must therefore be false. There is no largest integer. □

2. √2 is irrational. This is the classic, and it is worth knowing by heart because every part of it is a technique you have already met.

Proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that √2 is rational. Then by the definition of rational there are integers p and q with q ≠ 0 and

√2 = p / q

and we may take the fraction in lowest terms, so that p and q have no common factor other than 1. Squaring and multiplying by q2:

2 = p2 / q2,   so   p2 = 2q2

So p2 is even. By the contrapositive of worked example 2 above, if p were odd then p2 would be odd, so p is even, say p = 2k. Substituting:

(2k)2 = 2q2,   so   4k2 = 2q2,   so   q2 = 2k2

By the same argument q is even. But then p and q are both even and share the factor 2, contradicting "lowest terms". The assumption was false, so √2 is irrational. □

Where each technique appeared. The definition of rational opened it, a contrapositive supplied the step from p2 even to p even, and the contradiction closed it. Proofs at this level are almost always built from the smaller ones.

Cases, worked

Worked exampleMethod · proof by cases

For every integer n, the number n3 − n is even.

Why cases? Nothing is given about n at all, so there is no definition to unfold. But every integer is even or odd, and each of those does come with a formula. Two cases, and they are exhaustive.

The rule that makes it legal, which is worth quoting:

(p1 ∨ p2 ∨ … ∨ pn) → q ≡ (p1 → q) ∧ (p2 → q) ∧ … ∧ (pn → q)

which is another equivalence from section 1.3: proving each case separately is exactly proving the whole.

Case 1: n is even, so n = 2k for some integer k.

n3 − n = (2k)3 − 2k = 8k3 − 2k = 2(4k3 − k)

and 4k3 − k is an integer, so n3 − n is even.

Case 2: n is odd, so n = 2k + 1 for some integer k. Expanding the cube by the binomial theorem of section 5.4:

(2k + 1)3 = 8k3 + 12k2 + 6k + 1

so

n3 − n = 8k3 + 12k2 + 6k + 1 − (2k + 1)
          = 8k3 + 12k2 + 4k
          = 2(4k3 + 6k2 + 2k)

and 4k3 + 6k2 + 2k is an integer, so n3 − n is even.

Both cases are covered and both give the same conclusion, so n3 − n is even for every integer n. □

A one-line check before you trust the algebra: n = 3 gives 27 − 3 = 24, and n = 4 gives 64 − 4 = 60. Both even. A check is not a proof, but a failed check saves you from writing out a proof of something false.

The slicker argument, worth adding after the marks are safe: n3 − n = n(n − 1)(n + 1) is a product of three consecutive integers, and among any two consecutive integers one is even. One line, no cases.

TrapTwo arithmetic slips in a printed proof

The official course text works this same example and drops two errors into the algebra. They are worth knowing because a reader who copies them into an exam loses the marks, and because spotting them is exactly the skill a proof question is testing.

First, in the even case it writes 8k3 − 2k = 2(4k2 − k). The bracket should be 4k3 − k: dividing 8k3 by 2 gives 4k3, and the cube does not become a square.

Second, in the odd case it expands (2k + 1)3 as 8k3 + 6k2 + 6k + 1. The correct expansion is 8k3 + 12k2 + 6k + 1; the squared term is 3 × (2k)2 × 1 = 12k2, not 6k2. The same line also prints the cube as a square, writing (2k + 1)2 where it means (2k + 1)3.

Why the conclusion survives anyway, which is the interesting part: both wrong brackets still come out as 2 × (an integer), so the final claim "n3 − n is even" is still true. A proof can reach a true conclusion through false steps, and that is precisely why every line has to be checked rather than the answer alone.

Trivial and vacuous proofs

TeachThe two one-line proofs

Both come straight off the conditional's truth table in section 1.2, which is false in one row only, when p is true and q is false.

Trivial proof: q is already true, so no row can make p → q false, whatever p says.

If it is raining, then 1 = 1.
Proof: 1 = 1 is true, so the conditional is true regardless of the weather. □

Vacuous proof: p is false, so the one false row is never reached.

If the door is both open and closed, then 2 + 2 = 5.
Proof: a door cannot be both open and closed, so the hypothesis is a contradiction and is false. The conditional is therefore true. □

These are not jokes, and they matter in computing. A loop body that never runs satisfies every claim about what it does; a specification that says "for all x in the empty list, ..." is vacuously true. Section 1.4 made the same point about a universal quantifier over an empty domain.

In an exam, a trivial or vacuous proof is normally the answer to "explain why this is true", not to "prove". If you find yourself writing one for a serious-looking question, re-read it: you have probably misidentified p or q.

Choosing a strategy, and one worked mistake

TeachThe decision order
  1. Write the claim as p → q. Name p and name q in plain words. Half of all failed proof attempts are a claim never written down.
  2. Try direct. Replace every word in p by its definition and see how close the algebra takes you to q.
  3. If p gives you nothing, negate q. If ¬q has a usable formula where p did not, do the contrapositive. "Or" in the conclusion is a strong signal, because its negation is an "and".
  4. If the claim denies existence, use contradiction. Words like "no", "never", "cannot", "irrational", "infinitely many" all point here.
  5. If p splits, use cases. Odd or even, positive or negative or zero, and any hypothesis containing "or".
  6. Say which one you used, in the first line. "We prove the contrapositive" costs six words and tells the marker how to read everything that follows.
TrapWhen the convention changes the answer

Consider the claim "there is a natural number n for which n2 + 3n + 2 is prime".

Section 2.1 defined a prime as a number with exactly two positive divisors, itself and 1. A whole number above 1 that is not prime is composite: it can be written as a product of two smaller whole numbers, each at least 2.

If ℕ starts at 1, the claim is false, and there is a one-line proof: n2 + 3n + 2 = (n + 1)(n + 2), a product of two integers both at least 2, so it is composite for every n ≥ 1. Check: n = 1 gives 6, n = 2 gives 12, n = 3 gives 20.

If ℕ starts at 0, which is the convention this manual declared in Foundations, the claim is true: n = 0 gives 0 + 0 + 2 = 2, and 2 is prime. One witness settles an existence claim, and the whole argument collapses.

The Foundations trap box said "say which convention you are using". This is the case that shows why: the convention is not a matter of taste here, it decides the truth value. State it in the first line of the answer and you cannot be marked wrong either way.

Also note the better proof. The factorisation (n + 1)(n + 2) does in one line what a proof by cases on the parity of n does in fifteen. When a polynomial factorises, factorise it.
