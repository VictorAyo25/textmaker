Predicate Logic and Quantifiers

Propositional logic cannot say "every student has a laptop" without inventing a letter for each student. Predicate logic can, and that is the whole reason it exists.

By the end of this section you will be able to say exactly how predicate logic differs from propositional logic, use both quantifiers correctly, translate an English sentence with "some", "all", "no" or "every" into symbols, negate a quantified statement, and decide the truth of a quantified statement over the real numbers.

The limit of propositional logic

TeachWhy we need something more

In Part One so far, p was an entire sentence with a fixed truth value. That is fine for "it is raining", but it cannot see inside a sentence.

Consider "every student in this class has a laptop". With propositional letters you would need one letter per student, and you would still have no way to say "every". Worse, "x + 1 = 3" cannot be a proposition at all, because its truth depends on x.

Predicate logic fixes both problems by splitting a sentence into a subject and a property, and then adding words for "all" and "some".

Must memorisePredicate versus propositional logic

A predicate is a statement containing one or more variables, which becomes a proposition once the variables are given values. We write P(x), read "P of x".

If P(x) is "x > 3", then P(5) is "5 > 3", which is true, and P(1) is "1 > 3", which is false. P(x) on its own is neither.

Memory hook: propositional logic treats a sentence as a sealed box. Predicate logic opens the box and finds a subject and a property inside.

The two quantifiers

Must memoriseUniversal and existential

The universal quantifier ∀, read "for all" or "for every". ∀x P(x) is true exactly when P(x) is true for every x in the domain.

The existential quantifier ∃, read "there exists" or "for some". ∃x P(x) is true exactly when P(x) is true for at least one x in the domain.

The domain (or universe of discourse) is the collection the variable ranges over. It must always be stated, because the same statement can flip truth value when the domain changes.

Memory hook: ∀ is an upside-down A for All. ∃ is a back-to-front E for Exists. The asymmetry in the last two rows is worth learning on its own: one example kills a "for all" and one example makes a "there exists".

Trap∀ takes an arrow, ∃ takes an and

This is the single most common error in translation, and it appears on every paper that asks for one.

"Every student in the class has a laptop" is ∀x (S(x) → L(x)), with an arrow. Writing ∀x (S(x) ∧ L(x)) instead would claim that everything in the universe is a student and has a laptop, which is far stronger and almost certainly false.

"Some student has a laptop" is ∃x (S(x) ∧ L(x)), with an and. Writing ∃x (S(x) → L(x)) instead is nearly useless: it is satisfied by any non-student at all, since a false hypothesis makes the implication true.

If the domain is already restricted to students, the qualifier disappears and you write ∀x L(x) or ∃x L(x). Say which domain you are using.

Negating a quantified statement

De Morgan's laws for quantifiers

The claim. A negation moved across a quantifier flips it. Saying "not everything has the property" is the same as saying "something lacks it", and saying "nothing has the property" is the same as saying "everything lacks it".

In symbols.
¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x)
¬∃x P(x) ≡ ∀x ¬P(x)

Conditions. None. They hold for any predicate over any domain.

The limiting case. Over a finite domain {a1, …, an}, ∀ is a chain of conjunctions and ∃ a chain of disjunctions, so these two laws collapse into the propositional De Morgan laws of section 1.3 applied n times. They are not a second pair of laws but the same pair, stretched over a domain. Over an empty domain they still hold, and the check is worth doing once: ∀x P(x) is true there (nothing fails) and ∃x P(x) is false (nothing succeeds), so both sides of the first law come out false.

Consequence. Any negation can be pushed inwards until it rests on a bare predicate, flipping every quantifier it passes. This is how "no student has a cat, a dog and a ferret" gets written two different but equivalent ways, which 2024/2025 Q2(a)(iv) exploits.

TeachIn plain English

"Not all swans are white" means "some swan is not white". You have not claimed all swans are non-white, only that at least one is.

"No swan is green" means "every swan is not green". Here the negation does apply to all of them.

Notice that "no" is a negated existential: "no x has property P" is ¬∃x P(x), which becomes ∀x ¬P(x). Whenever an exam sentence starts with "No", write it as a negated "there exists" first, then convert if you want.

The translation question

As printed2024/2025 · Q2(a)

a.       Let C(x) be the statement "x has a cat," let D(x) be the statement "x has a dog," and let F (x) be the statement "x has a ferret." Express each of these statements in terms of C(x), D(x), F (x), quantifiers, and logical connectives. Let the domain consist of all students in your class.

i.       A student in your class has a cat, a dog, and a ferret.

ii.       All students in your class have a cat, a dog, or a ferret.

iii.       Some student in your class has a cat and a ferret, but not a dog.

iv.       No student in your class has a cat, a dog, and a ferret.

v.       For each of the three animals, cats, dogs, and ferrets, there is a student in your class who has this animal as a pet. 7.5 marks

Note on the paper
The domain is given as "all students in your class", which is a real gift: it means you do not need a student predicate S(x), and the ∀/→ pairing collapses. Students who invent S(x) anyway are not wrong, but they do more work for the same marks. Note also the paper's spacing, "F (x)" with a space, which is just typesetting.
Break it down
Given
Three predicates C, D, F, and a domain restricted to students in the class.
Asked
Express five English sentences in symbols. Nothing is to be proved or evaluated; the marks are purely for correct translation.
Marks say
7.5 over five items is 1.5 each, so each answer is one line of symbols. There is no credit for prose here.
Teaches
This section, 1.4.
Trap
Item (v) is not three separate statements joined by "and" about one student. It says each animal has some owner, and the owners may be three different people. That distinction is the whole mark.
Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q2(a)

Throughout, the domain is the students in the class, so a bare ∀x or ∃x already means "every student" or "some student".

(i) "A student in your class has a cat, a dog, and a ferret."
"A student" here means at least one, so it is existential, and the three pets belong to the same student, so they join with "and".

∃x (C(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ F(x))

(ii) "All students in your class have a cat, a dog, or a ferret."
Universal, and "or" is inclusive: having two of them is fine.

∀x (C(x) ∨ D(x) ∨ F(x))

(iii) "Some student in your class has a cat and a ferret, but not a dog."
Existential. "But" is simply "and" with a change of tone, and "not a dog" negates D.

∃x (C(x) ∧ F(x) ∧ ¬D(x))

(iv) "No student in your class has a cat, a dog, and a ferret."
"No" is a negated existential, so start there and then convert.

¬∃x (C(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ F(x))

which by the quantifier law equals ∀x ¬(C(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ F(x)), and by De Morgan equals ∀x (¬C(x) ∨ ¬D(x) ∨ ¬F(x)).

All three are correct and any one earns the mark. Quoting the first and then showing the conversion demonstrates more, and costs one line.

Notice this is exactly the negation of (i), which is worth saying in your answer.

(v) "For each of the three animals, cats, dogs, and ferrets, there is a student in your class who has this animal as a pet."
Three separate existence claims, one per animal, and they need not be the same student. So it is a conjunction of three existentials, each with its own variable.

∃x C(x) ∧ ∃y D(y) ∧ ∃z F(z)

Using three different letters is not fussiness. Writing ∃x (C(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ F(x)) instead would say one student owns all three, which is statement (i), a different and much stronger claim. That contrast is exactly what the examiner set (i) and (v) to test.

Now redo it: cover the answers and translate all five again. Then translate a sixth of your own: "exactly one student has a ferret", which is harder than it looks.

Deciding the truth of a quantified statement

As printed2024/2025 · Q4(a)

a.       Determine the truth value of each of these statements if the domain consists of all real numbers.

i.       ∃x(x³ = −1)     ii.       ∃x(x⁴ < x²)     iii.       ∀x((−x)² = x²)     iv.       ∀x(2x > x) 4 marks

Break it down
Given
Four quantified statements, domain the real numbers.
Asked
Determine the truth value. That means a verdict plus the reason: a witness for a true existential, a counterexample for a false universal, an argument otherwise.
Marks say
1 mark each, so one line each. But a bare "true" is worth little; name the number.
Teaches
This section, and the asymmetry table above.
Trap
Part (iv) looks obviously true and is false. The domain is all reals, which includes zero and the negatives.
Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q4(a)

(i) ∃x(x³ = −1). An existential, so one witness settles it. Take x = −1: then x³ = −1. TRUE.

(ii) ∃x(x⁴ < x²). Another existential. Try a fraction: x = ½ gives x² = 0.25 and x⁴ = 0.0625, and 0.0625 < 0.25. TRUE.

Worth understanding rather than guessing: x⁴ < x² means x²(x² − 1) < 0, which needs x² < 1 and x ≠ 0. So it holds exactly for the non-zero numbers strictly between −1 and 1.

(iii) ∀x((−x)² = x²). A universal, so it needs an argument covering everything, not an example. (−x)² = (−1)² × x² = 1 × x² = x², for every real x. TRUE.

(iv) ∀x(2x > x). A universal, so one counterexample kills it. Take x = 0: then 2x = 0 and x = 0, and 0 > 0 is false. FALSE.

Take x = −3 for a second counterexample: 2x = −6, and −6 > −3 is false. In fact 2x > x only for the positive reals, and the domain here is all of them.

Now redo it: decide ∀x(x² ≥ x) over the reals, then over the integers. The answers differ, which is the point of always stating the domain.

Translating a sentence with structure

As printed2024/2025 · Q2(c)

c.       Express the statement "If a person is female and is a parent, then this person is someone's mother" as a logical expression involving predicates, and quantifiers with a domain consisting of all people, and logical connectives. 4 marks

Break it down
Given
One English sentence and a domain of all people.
Asked
Express it in symbols. You must invent the predicates yourself, which is part of the marks: define them before you use them.
Marks say
4 marks for one sentence is generous, so they are paying for the setup: naming predicates, choosing quantifiers, nesting correctly.
Teaches
This section. The nesting of two quantifiers is the new element.
Trap
"Someone's mother" hides a second quantifier. It means there exists a person of whom she is the mother, so the answer nests ∃ inside ∀.
Worked examplePQ 24/25 Q2(c)

Step 1. Name the predicates. Over the domain of all people, let

F(x) = "x is female"
P(x) = "x is a parent"
M(x, y) = "x is the mother of y"

M takes two arguments because motherhood is a relation between two people, not a property of one. Part Three studies such relations properly.

Step 2. Read the shape of the sentence. It says: for every person, if (female and parent) then (mother of someone).

Step 3. Write it.

∀x ((F(x) ∧ P(x)) → ∃y M(x, y))

Step 4. Check each piece. The outer quantifier is universal because the claim is about every person, and it takes an arrow, as universals do. The hypothesis is a conjunction, because both conditions must hold. The conclusion is existential, because being someone's mother only requires one such someone. The y is bound inside, so it cannot be confused with the x.

An equally correct variant pulls the existential out to the front: ∀x ∃y ((F(x) ∧ P(x)) → M(x, y)). Both are accepted.

Now redo it: express "every student has an adviser" and then "some adviser advises every student". They differ only in the order of the two quantifiers, and they mean very different things.

TrapOrder of nested quantifiers changes the meaning

∀x ∃y L(x, y) says: for each x there is some y, and it may be a different y each time. "Everyone has a mother."

∃y ∀x L(x, y) says: there is one y that works for every x. "Someone is the mother of everyone."

Same symbols, opposite claims, and the first is true while the second is absurd. Two quantifiers of the same kind can be swapped freely; two of different kinds never can.

Recall and check

1. What does ∀ pair with, an arrow or an and? And ∃?

2. Write "no student passed" using ∃, then using ∀.

3. How do you disprove ∀x P(x)?

4. Is ∀x ∃y L(x,y) the same as ∃y ∀x L(x,y)?

Answers

1. ∀ pairs with →, ∃ pairs with ∧.
2. ¬∃x P(x), equivalently ∀x ¬P(x), where P(x) is "x passed".
3. Produce a single counterexample: one x with P(x) false.
4. No. The first lets y depend on x; the second demands one y for all x.
