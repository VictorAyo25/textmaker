Part Six · 6.8

Shortest Routes and the Travelling Salesman

The same five-city map has appeared on three papers, worth 10 marks every time, always with the same distances and always asking the same thing. It is the most reliable 10 marks in this book.

By the end of this section you will be able to state the travelling salesman problem, count how many tours a graph has, apply the nearest-neighbour method and say honestly what it does and does not guarantee, and produce the optimal Detroit tour with a proof that it is optimal.

Must memoriseWeighted graphs and the travelling salesman problem

A weighted graph is a graph in which every edge carries a number w(u, v), its weight: a distance, a cost, a time. The weight of a route is the sum of the weights of the edges it uses.

The travelling salesman problem asks: starting from one vertex, visit every other vertex exactly once and return to the start, with the smallest possible total weight.

Where w(u, v) is the weight on the edge joining u and v, read straight off the labels on the drawing. a tour is a Hamilton circuit, as defined in section 6.6: every vertex once, back to the start. smallest total weight is what makes it an optimisation problem rather than an existence one. On a complete graph a tour always exists; the work is finding the cheapest.

Memory hook: every city once, home at the end, cheapest possible. Drop any one of those three and it is a different problem with a different answer.

Must memoriseHow many tours are there

On a complete graph with n vertices, the number of distinct tours starting and ending at a fixed vertex is

(n − 1)! / 2

Where (n − 1)! counts the orders in which the other n − 1 cities can be visited, by the permutation rule of section 5.2. the division by 2 is because a tour and the same tour driven backwards use the same edges and cost the same, so they are one route, not two.

For the five-city map: (5 − 1)! / 2 = 24 / 2 = 12 distinct tours. That is few enough to list them all, which is why this question is answerable exactly in an exam.

Why the problem is famous, in one line worth writing: the count grows factorially, so 10 cities give 181440 tours and 20 cities give more than 1016. No method is known that finds the exact optimum quickly for large n, which is why heuristics like nearest neighbour exist at all.

Memory hook: fix the start, permute the rest, halve for the reverse.

Note on the paper, and on the figure
Three papers, one question, 30 marks in total, and the same figure each time. The wording differs only in "A Christian missionary" against "a travelling missionary" and in whether the marks are printed closed up.

Why the 2023/2024 quote above carries no mark text. On that paper the diagram sits between the question and its marks, and "(10 marks)" is printed on its own line below the figure. Splicing it onto the end of the sentence would join words the paper does not join, so the quote stops where the paper's sentence stops and the marks are reported here instead. They are 10, the same as the other two versions.

The figure is the same on all three and it is readable. It is a complete graph on five cities, so all C(5, 2) = 10 edges are drawn and labelled. The labels were read at 500 to 700 dpi and are listed in the worked answer below. Unlike the two digraphs of section 6.4, nothing here is in doubt.

What "in which order" means for your answer. The question asks for an order, so the answer is a sequence of city names, not just a number. Give the route, give its total, and give the argument that no route is shorter. All three are needed for 10 marks.
Break it down
Given
Five cities, all pairs joined, with the ten distances labelled on the figure. The tour must start and finish at Detroit.
Asked
In which order the cities should be visited to travel the minimum distance. An order, a total, and a reason it is minimal.
Marks say
10, the largest single part on any of these papers. Budget: 1 for tabulating the distances, 1 for the tour count, 4 for the enumeration or the method, 2 for the route, 2 for showing it is optimal.
Teaches
This section, 6.8, with Hamilton circuits from 6.6 and permutations from 5.2.
Trap
Giving the nearest-neighbour route and calling it the minimum without checking. Nearest neighbour is a heuristic: it is fast, it is often good, and it is not guaranteed to be optimal. With only 12 tours you can check them all, so there is no excuse for guessing here.
Worked examplePQ 20/21 Q2(a) · PQ 21/22 Q4(c) · PQ 23/24 Q4(b)
Given
Five cities forming a complete weighted graph, start and finish at Detroit. The ten distances are tabulated in step 1.
Find
The order of visits giving the minimum total distance, and that minimum.
Rule
A tour is a Hamilton circuit; there are (n − 1)!/2 = 12 of them; the minimum is found by evaluating all 12.

Step 2. Count the tours. Five cities with Detroit fixed as the start gives

(5 − 1)! / 2 = 4! / 2 = 24 / 2 = 12 distinct tours.

Twelve is small enough to evaluate exhaustively, and doing so is what turns "here is a short route" into "here is the shortest route".

Why it is optimal, which is the part worth two marks: all twelve possible tours have been evaluated and none is shorter than 458. This is not an estimate. The worst tour costs 728, so the choice of order matters by over 270, which is a good closing observation.

Note the route may be written either way round. Detroit, Toledo, Kalamazoo, Grand Rapids, Saginaw, Detroit is the same tour driven backwards and costs the same 458. Either is a correct answer; that is exactly what the division by 2 in step 2 was accounting for.

The nearest-neighbour method, and its honest status. If time is short, the heuristic is: from the current city, always go to the nearest unvisited one, and return home at the end.

On this map the heuristic happens to find the optimum, because the route it builds is the optimal tour driven backwards. Say "happens to". Nearest neighbour carries no guarantee: it commits to a cheap first step that can force an expensive last one, and on other maps it does exactly that. If you use it, use it to find a candidate and then justify the candidate by comparison, as above. A 10-mark answer that says "nearest neighbour gives 458, therefore 458 is the minimum" has asserted the thing it was asked to establish.

Now redo it: how many tours would six cities have? (6 − 1)!/2 = 120/2 = 60, five times as many for one extra city. That growth is the whole difficulty of the problem.

TrapA heuristic is not a proof

The three papers all say "the minimum distance". A minimum is a claim about every alternative, so it can only be established by ruling the alternatives out. With 12 tours, list them. With 60 you would explain the method and evaluate a well-chosen few, stating plainly that the result is a good route rather than a proven optimum.

Writing that distinction down costs one sentence and is the difference between an answer that is correct and one that is merely right.

Recall and check

1. State the travelling salesman problem.

2. How many distinct tours has a complete graph on n vertices, and why the division by 2?

3. How many tours has the five-city map?

4. Give the optimal Detroit route and its total.

5. What does the nearest-neighbour method guarantee?

Answers

1. Starting from one vertex of a weighted graph, visit every other vertex exactly once and return to the start, at minimum total weight.
2. (n − 1)!/2. Fixing the start leaves (n − 1)! orders for the rest, and each tour is counted twice because driving it backwards costs the same.
3. 4!/2 = 12.
4. Detroit, Saginaw, Grand Rapids, Kalamazoo, Toledo, Detroit, totalling 458.
5. Nothing. It is a heuristic that often gives a good route and is not guaranteed to give the shortest.
