Reference · R.2

Exam Day Strategy

On a paper where you answer four questions out of six, the choosing is worth more than any single formula. This section is about the three hours: which four to take, how long each part may have, and what to write when a question is defective or you are stuck.

By the end of this section you will know the paper's shape and how it has changed across sessions, a defensible time budget per mark, a method for choosing four questions in ten minutes, what each command word is asking for, and what to do about the printing errors these papers actually contain.

Must memoriseThe time budget

Three hours is 180 minutes for 70 marks. Reserve time at both ends rather than discovering you have none.

Memory hook: two minutes a mark, ten at each end.

Choosing the four

TeachA ten-minute method

These questions are not single problems. Each is a bundle of five to eight small parts, often from unrelated topics, and the parts carry their own marks. That changes the choosing completely: you are not looking for the question you can solve, you are looking for the question whose parts you can bank.

  1. Read all six. Do not start writing. Six questions at ninety seconds each is nine minutes, and it is the highest-value nine minutes of the paper.
  2. Beside each part, write a tick, a cross or a question mark. Tick means "I can do this now". Question mark means "probably, with thought". Cross means "no".
  3. Add up the ticked marks per question. That number, not your feeling about the topic, is what to choose on.
  4. Take the four highest. A question with 12 ticked marks and 5.5 crossed beats one you like the look of with 8 ticked.
  5. Start with your strongest. It banks marks early and settles you. Leave the weakest until last, when you know exactly how much time is left for it.
  6. Number every part exactly as the paper numbers it. If the paper labels a part "b i.", so do you. A marker who cannot find a part cannot award it.

Where the marks hide. On the 2025/2026 paper the parts run 10, 3, 2, 2.5, 5, 2, 2, 4, 4.5, 7, 5, 5.5, and so on. A five-part question you can half-do is worth more than a two-part question you cannot start. Never abandon a question because one part defeats you.

Where "with an example each" is not decoration. When a part says it, roughly half that part's marks are in the examples, and an answer with five perfect definitions and no examples loses them. "differentiate" is the one most often misread. Foundations F.2 covered it: two definitions side by side is not a difference until you write the sentence that contrasts them. "in detail" or "clearly" attached to any verb means the marks are large and the expected answer is long.

Memory hook: answer the verb, not the topic.

Writing an answer that collects everything it earned

TeachSix habits, none of which needs extra knowledge
  • State the formula before you substitute. The formula is usually a mark on its own, and it survives an arithmetic slip further down. Every worked example in this book opens with Given, Find and Formula for exactly that reason.
  • Name the rule or law you are using. "by De Morgan", "by the Handshaking theorem", "by the product rule". It costs three words and turns an assertion into an argument.
  • Draw the diagram even when it is not asked for. A graph question with a sketch is easier to mark and easier to get right, and no marker has ever deducted for a correct extra diagram.
  • Write the units and the object. Not "9" but "9 vertices of degree 4". A bare number can be right and unrecognisable.
  • Check the sanity of every answer. A degree sum must be even. A probability must lie between 0 and 1. A count of arrangements must be a whole number. Each of those has caught a real error in this book's own working.
  • If you run out of time, write the method in ordered lines. "1. Sum of degrees is 2|E| = 60. 2. Known vertices account for 41. 3. Solve the pair." That is most of the reasoning marks with none of the arithmetic.
TrapThese papers contain real printing errors

Every paper in this book has at least one defect, and this manual quotes them exactly as printed rather than tidying them, so you meet them here first rather than in the hall. Two have made a part unanswerable as written: 2025/2026 Q3(a) names the same language twice, and 2024/2025 Q4(b) asks which relations are reflexive in both part i and part v.

What to do, in one line each:

  1. Do not stop. A defect is not permission to leave the part blank, and it is never in your favour to do so.
  2. Say what you think is meant, in one sentence. "Taking the second figure to refer to Efik, since otherwise the two counts are the same group."
  3. Answer that reading in full. The marks are for the method, and the method does not change.
  4. If a second reading is plausible and cheap, give it too. One extra line covering the alternative cannot lose marks and may win them.

The same applies to a figure you cannot read. 2023/2024 Q5(c) has two arrowheads that do not resolve even at high magnification. State the reading you are tabulating, tabulate it correctly, and say that the arrowheads on your own copy should be checked. Section 6.4 does exactly that, and it is the honest answer, not a hedge.

TeachThe last ten minutes

Do these in order. They are cheap and they recover marks that are already earned.

  1. Count your questions. Four, not three, not five. A fifth answer normally earns nothing and cost you time on the four that counted.
  2. Check every part is numbered as the paper numbers it, and that no part has been silently skipped.
  3. Re-add every arithmetic answer. Not re-derive: re-add. Most errors are in the last step.
  4. Check the sanity list: degree sums even, probabilities between 0 and 1, counts whole, a tree's edges one fewer than its vertices.
  5. Make sure every "and give an example" has an example.
  6. Read your first answer again. It was written when you were least settled and is where a whole part is most often missing.
Recall and check

1. How many questions do you answer, out of how many, and for how many marks each?

2. How many minutes does a part worth 4.5 marks deserve?

3. What do you write beside each part while choosing?

4. What does "differentiate" want that two definitions do not give?

5. What should you do when a printed question is defective?

6. Name three sanity checks worth running in the last ten minutes.

Answers

1. Four out of six, 17.5 marks each, 70 in total, in three hours.
2. About 9 minutes, at a shade over two minutes per mark.
3. A tick, a cross or a question mark, then total the ticked marks per question and take the four highest.
4. The contrast itself: a sentence with "whereas" in it.
5. Say in one sentence what you take it to mean, then answer that reading in full, and give the alternative if it is cheap.
6. Degree sums are even; probabilities lie between 0 and 1; counts of arrangements are whole numbers; a tree has one fewer edge than vertices.
