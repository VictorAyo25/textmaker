Part Six · 6.7

Planarity, Euler’s Formula and Kuratowski

Can this graph be drawn on paper with no edges crossing? Two graphs cannot, every other obstruction is built from those two, and the newest paper gives 10 marks to proving it.

By the end of this section you will be able to define a planar graph, state and use Euler's formula for planar graphs, derive and apply the two edge bounds, state Kuratowski's theorem, say why K5 and K3,3 matter, and prove both non-planar in full.

Planar graphs

Must memorisePlanar graph and faces

A graph is planar when it can be drawn in the plane with no two edges crossing except at their shared endpoints. Such a drawing is called a plane drawing of the graph.

A plane drawing cuts the plane into faces, or regions. The unbounded region outside the whole drawing is a face too, and forgetting it is the standard error.

Where planar is a property of the graph: it asks whether some crossing-free drawing exists. plane is a property of a particular drawing, one that happens to have no crossings. a face is a region enclosed by edges, counting the infinite outer region as one.

Planarity is an invariant, in the sense of section 6.6: isomorphic graphs are either both planar or both non-planar, since a relabelling cannot create or remove a crossing. So it joins the list of properties that can disprove isomorphism.

Why a drawing with crossings proves nothing. K4 is usually drawn as a square with both diagonals, which cross; but it can be redrawn as a triangle with one vertex inside, which does not. So a crossing you happen to have drawn never shows a graph is non-planar. Only a counting argument does, which is exactly what this section supplies.

Memory hook: planar means it can be drawn flat, not that you drew it flat. And never forget the outside face.

Euler's formula for planar graphs

The claim. Every plane drawing of the same connected planar graph cuts the plane into the same number of regions, and that number is fixed by the vertex and edge counts alone. Vertices minus edges plus faces is always two, whatever the graph and however it is drawn.

In symbols.
v − e + f = 2

Conditions. The graph must be connected and planar, and f must be counted from a plane drawing, that is, one with no crossings, including the unbounded outer face. For a graph with k components the formula becomes v − e + f = 1 + k.

The limiting case. For a tree, which section 7.1 defines as a connected graph with no cycles, there are no enclosed regions at all, so f = 1, and the formula gives v − e + 1 = 2, that is, e = v − 1. The edge count of a tree is not a separate fact to memorise but this formula at its smallest possible f.

Must memoriseThe two edge bounds

For a simple connected planar graph with v ≥ 3 vertices,

e ≤ 3v − 6

and if in addition the graph has no triangles, for example if it is bipartite,

e ≤ 2v − 4

Where v is the number of vertices and e the number of edges. 3v − 6 is the general bound, coming from the fact that every face of a simple graph is bounded by at least 3 edges. 2v − 4 is the triangle-free bound, coming from the fact that without triangles every face needs at least 4 edges.

Where they come from, which is worth knowing because the derivation is short and it is what makes the bounds usable rather than magic. Count the pairs (face, edge on its boundary) in two ways. Every edge borders exactly two faces, so that count is 2e. Every face is bounded by at least 3 edges in a simple graph, so that count is at least 3f. Hence

2e ≥ 3f,   so   f ≤ 2e/3.

Substituting into Euler's formula f = 2 − v + e gives

2 − v + e ≤ 2e/3
6 − 3v + 3e ≤ 2e
e ≤ 3v − 6

With no triangles every face needs at least 4 edges, so 2e ≥ 4f and the same substitution gives e ≤ 2v − 4.

How to use them. These are one-way tests. If a graph breaks the bound it is definitely not planar. If it satisfies the bound it may or may not be planar, and the bound has told you nothing.

Memory hook: too many edges for too few vertices means crossings are forced. Three v minus six in general; two v minus four when there are no triangles to help.

The two graphs that matter

Kuratowski's theorem

The claim. There are only two ways a graph can fail to be planar. A graph is planar if and only if it contains no subgraph that is a subdivision of K5 or of K3,3. Every non-planar graph, however large, hides one of those two inside it.

In symbols.
G is planar ⇔ G contains no subdivision of K5 and no subdivision of K3,3.

Conditions. A subdivision of a graph is what you get by adding extra vertices in the middle of its edges, which never changes whether the graph can be drawn flat. The theorem holds for all finite simple graphs. Note the "if and only if": unlike the edge bounds, this is a complete characterisation, not a one-way test.

The limiting case, and why it is remarkable. Any graph on 4 or fewer vertices is planar, because K5 needs 5 vertices and K3,3 needs 6, so neither can be hidden inside anything smaller. K4 is therefore the largest complete graph that can be drawn flat, and K5 is the first that cannot. The force of the theorem is that the list stops at two: an infinite family of obstructions could have existed and does not.

The 10-mark planarity question

Note on the paper
Part iii has a comma where a full stop belongs ("using Euler's formula, Recall that"), and the subscripts are printed inline as "K5" and "K3,3" rather than as K5 and K3,3.

One thing in it is mathematically loose and you should handle it carefully. The paper calls e ≤ 3v − 6 and e ≤ 2v − 4 "Euler's formula". They are not. Euler's formula is v − e + f = 2; these two are corollaries of it, derived by combining it with a count of the edges bounding each face. The safe answer states Euler's formula properly, notes that the two inequalities follow from it, and then uses them exactly as the question directs. That earns the marks the question offers and shows you know the difference, without arguing with the examiner.

Note also that the bound e ≤ 2v − 4 is not a general planar bound: it needs the graph to have no triangles. K3,3 is bipartite so it has none, which is exactly why the paper offers that bound for it.

Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q5(b)
Given
K5, the complete graph on 5 vertices, and K3,3, the complete bipartite graph with 3 vertices on each side. The bounds e ≤ 3v − 6 and e ≤ 2v − 4.
Find
A statement of Kuratowski's theorem, the reason these two graphs matter, and a proof that each is non-planar.
Formula
Euler's formula v − e + f = 2, and the two bounds derived from it.

i. State and explain Kuratowski's theorem

Statement. A graph is planar if and only if it contains no subgraph that is a subdivision of K5 or of K3,3.

Explanation. A subdivision of a graph is obtained by inserting extra vertices along its edges, which cannot affect whether the graph can be drawn without crossings, since the inserted vertices simply sit on the curves already drawn. The theorem therefore says that these two graphs are the only obstructions to planarity: any graph that cannot be drawn flat contains a copy of one of them, possibly with extra vertices along its edges, and any graph containing neither can be drawn flat.

The line that shows you understand it: this is an "if and only if", so unlike the edge bounds it decides the question in both directions. The bounds can only ever say "not planar"; Kuratowski also says "planar".

ii. Why K5 and K3,3 are important

Three reasons, one per half mark and a spare:

  1. They are the two smallest non-planar graphs, and by Kuratowski's theorem they are the only obstructions to planarity. Every non-planar graph in existence contains a subdivision of one of them, so understanding two small graphs settles a question about infinitely many large ones.
  2. They mark the exact boundary. K4 is planar and K5 is not, so K4 is the largest complete graph that can be drawn flat. K2,3 is planar and K3,3 is not. The two graphs are where planarity stops.
  3. They matter in practice. K3,3 is the classic "three utilities to three houses" puzzle, which has no solution for exactly this reason. Planarity decides whether a circuit can be laid out on a single-layer board without wires crossing, so these two graphs are the test for when a second layer becomes unavoidable.

iii. Show that K5 is non-planar

State the tool first. Euler's formula for a connected planar graph is v − e + f = 2. Combining it with the fact that every face of a simple graph is bounded by at least three edges gives the bound the question supplies: for any simple connected planar graph with v ≥ 3,

e ≤ 3v − 6

Step 1. Count v and e for K5. It has 5 vertices, and being complete it joins every pair, so by section 6.2

v = 5,   e = C(5, 2) = 5 × 4 / 2 = 10

Step 2. Evaluate the bound.

3v − 6 = 3 × 5 − 6 = 15 − 6 = 9

Step 3. Compare and conclude.

e = 10 > 9 = 3v − 6

Every simple connected planar graph on at least 3 vertices satisfies e ≤ 3v − 6. K5 does not. Therefore K5 is non-planar. □

iii, continued. Show that K3,3 is non-planar

Step 1. Count v and e for K3,3. Three vertices on each side gives 3 + 3 = 6 vertices, and every vertex of one side joins every vertex of the other, so by the product rule of section 5.1

v = 6,   e = 3 × 3 = 9

Step 2. Check that the tighter bound applies. This is the step most answers skip and it is worth a mark. K3,3 is bipartite, and a bipartite graph has no odd cycles, so in particular it has no triangles. Every face of a plane drawing of it would therefore be bounded by at least four edges, which is what gives the bound

e ≤ 2v − 4

Step 3. Evaluate and compare.

2v − 4 = 2 × 6 − 4 = 12 − 4 = 8
e = 9 > 8 = 2v − 4

Therefore K3,3 is non-planar. □

Why the general bound would have failed here, worth one line because it shows why the question supplied two different inequalities: for K3,3, 3v − 6 = 12, and e = 9 is comfortably under 12. The general bound is satisfied, so it proves nothing. Only the triangle-free bound is sharp enough, and it applies only because the graph is bipartite.

Worked exampleMethod · Euler's formula on a plane drawing
Given
K4 drawn as a triangle with one vertex inside, joined to all three corners. No edges cross.
Find
The number of faces, and a check of Euler's formula.
Formula
v − e + f = 2.

Count. v = 4 and e = 6.

Solve for f.

4 − 6 + f = 2,   so   f = 4

Verify by looking at the drawing. The inner vertex splits the triangle into three small regions, and there is the unbounded region outside: 3 + 1 = 4. The formula and the picture agree, and the outer face is the one that is easy to miss.

The bound as a sanity check: 3v − 6 = 6 and e = 6, so K4 sits exactly on the bound. A graph on the bound is called maximal planar: it is planar, and adding any further edge would make it non-planar.

Answers

3. e ≤ 3v − 6 for a simple connected planar graph with v ≥ 3, and e ≤ 2v − 4 when it also has no triangles.
4. A graph is planar if and only if it contains no subdivision of K5 or K3,3.
5. v = 5 and e = 10, but 3v − 6 = 9, and 10 > 9.
6. Because 3v − 6 = 12 and e = 9 satisfies it. The bound is not sharp enough; the triangle-free bound of 8 is.
