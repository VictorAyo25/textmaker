Answer2024/2025 Q1 · 17.5 marks

a. Converse, contrapositive and inverse of three conditionals (4.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 1.2. Identify p and q first; that is where the marks are lost, not in the three rewrites.

i. "If it snows tonight, then I will stay at home." p = it snows tonight, q = I will stay at home.

Converse: If I stay at home tonight, then it will snow.
Contrapositive: If I do not stay at home, then it will not snow.
Inverse: If it does not snow tonight, then I will not stay at home.

ii. "I go to the beach whenever it is a sunny summer day." The whenever clause is the hypothesis, so p = it is a sunny summer day, q = I go to the beach. Writing it the other way round loses every mark that follows.

Converse: If I go to the beach, then it is a sunny summer day.
Contrapositive: If I do not go to the beach, then it is not a sunny summer day.
Inverse: If it is not a sunny summer day, then I do not go to the beach.

iii. "When I stay up late, it is necessary that I sleep until noon." "It is necessary that q" makes q the conclusion, so p = I stay up late, q = I sleep until noon.

Converse: If I sleep until noon, then I stayed up late.
Contrapositive: If I do not sleep until noon, then I did not stay up late.
Inverse: If I do not stay up late, then I do not sleep until noon.

iv. ((p → q) → r) → s

Four letters, so the table has 24 = 16 rows. Rather than fill sixteen rows blindly, work out where it is false and say so.

Write X for (p → q) → r. The whole expression X → s is false only when X is true and s is false. c. i. "The home team wins whenever it is raining" (3 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 1.2. With p = "It is raining" and q = "The home team wins", the statement is p → q.

Contrapositive: If the home team does not win, then it is not raining.
Converse: If the home team wins, then it is raining.
Inverse: If it is not raining, then the home team does not win.

c. ii. Prove only the contrapositive agrees (6 marks)

To prove: of the converse, the inverse and the contrapositive, only the contrapositive has the same truth value as p → q in every case.

Proof, by exhaustive truth table. There are only four assignments, so this covers every case and is a complete proof, not an illustration.

Read the columns. The contrapositive column is identical to the original in all four rows, so p → q ≡ ¬q → ¬p. The converse and the inverse each differ from the original in two rows, the second and the third, so neither is equivalent to it. □

Using the statement from c(i), as the question asks. Take the case where it is not raining and the home team wins, which is row 3. "The home team wins whenever it is raining" is true: nothing was promised about dry days. Its contrapositive, "if the home team does not win it is not raining", is also true, vacuously. But its converse, "if the home team wins it is raining", is false, since the team won in the dry; and its inverse, "if it is not raining the home team does not win", is false for the same reason. One concrete case, and it exhibits the difference the table proves in general.

A second line worth adding: the converse and the inverse have identical columns, so they are equivalent to each other. They are contrapositives of one another.

a. Five quantified translations (7.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 1.4. C(x) = x has a cat, D(x) = x has a dog, F(x) = x has a ferret, domain = the students in the class. Because the domain is already the students, no S(x) is needed and no arrow appears.

i. ∃x (C(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ F(x))
ii. ∀x (C(x) ∨ D(x) ∨ F(x))
iii. ∃x (C(x) ∧ F(x) ∧ ¬D(x))
iv. ¬∃x (C(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ F(x)), equivalently ∀x ¬(C(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ F(x))
v. ∃x C(x) ∧ ∃y D(y) ∧ ∃z F(z)

Part v is the one that separates candidates. "For each of the three animals there is a student who has it" allows a different student for each animal, so it needs three separate quantifiers. Writing ∃x (C(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ F(x)) would say one student has all three, which is part i, not part v.

Part iv is the negation of part i, which is worth saying: it makes the pair easy to check and shows the structure was seen rather than guessed.

b. i. When f ∘ g = g ∘ f (2 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 4.3. With f(x) = ax + b and g(x) = cx + d:

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(cx + d) = a(cx + d) + b = acx + ad + b
(g ∘ f)(x) = g(ax + b) = c(ax + b) + d = acx + bc + d

The x terms already agree, so the two are equal for every x exactly when the constants agree:

ad + b = bc + d

That single equation is both necessary and sufficient, which is what the question asked for.

b. ii. If f and f ∘ g are one-to-one, is g? (2 marks)

Yes, and here is the proof.

Proof. Suppose g(x1) = g(x2). Applying f to both sides, f(g(x1)) = f(g(x2)), that is (f ∘ g)(x1) = (f ∘ g)(x2). Since f ∘ g is one-to-one, x1 = x2. Therefore g is one-to-one. □

Note what was not used: the hypothesis that f is one-to-one never appears. The result holds from f ∘ g alone, and saying so shows the argument was followed rather than recited.

b. iii. f ∘ g and g ∘ f for x2 + 1 and x + 2 (2 marks)

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(x + 2) = (x + 2)2 + 1 = x2 + 4x + 5
(g ∘ f)(x) = g(x2 + 1) = (x2 + 1) + 2 = x2 + 3

They are not equal. One value settles it: at x = 1 the first gives 10 and the second gives 4. This identical question is set again on the 2023/2024 paper.

c. "Female and a parent implies someone's mother" (4 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 1.4. Over a domain of all people, with F(x) = x is female, P(x) = x is a parent, and M(x, y) = x is the mother of y:

∀x ((F(x) ∧ P(x)) → ∃y M(x, y))

Three things to point at. The domain is all people, so the universal quantifier takes an arrow. "Someone's mother" is an existence claim about a second person, so it needs its own ∃y inside. And the ∃y sits inside the implication, because a different child may serve each mother.

Answer2024/2025 Q3 · 17.5 marks

a. Why (A × B) × (C × D) and A × (B × C) × D differ (3.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 2.3.

The point is the shape of the elements, not their number. A typical element of the first is ((a, b), (c, d)): an ordered pair whose two entries are themselves ordered pairs. A typical element of the second is (a, (b, c), d): an ordered triple whose middle entry is a pair.

A pair of pairs is not a triple, so no element of one set is an element of the other, and the two sets are not equal. The Cartesian product is not associative.

What is true. The two sets have the same size, n(A)n(B)n(C)n(D) either way, and there is an obvious one-to-one correspondence between them. Equal size is not equality, and saying both halves is what a 3.5-mark answer looks like.

That enumeration produces the eight subsets in exactly the order the paper prints them, including the empty set as { }.

c. The first De Morgan law by set-builder notation (3 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 2.2.

Note on the printed line first. The paper prints the law with the overbar sitting over B alone and with a dash where an equals sign belongs. The law being asked for is that the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements, and that is what is established here.

To prove: (A ∩ B)′ = A′ ∪ B′.

Proof, by a chain of equivalences on an arbitrary element.

x ∈ (A ∩ B)′
≡ ¬(x ∈ A ∩ B)     [definition of complement]
≡ ¬(x ∈ A ∧ x ∈ B)     [definition of intersection]
≡ ¬(x ∈ A) ∨ ¬(x ∈ B)     [De Morgan for propositions, section 1.3]
≡ x ∈ A′ ∨ x ∈ B′     [definition of complement, twice]
≡ x ∈ A′ ∪ B′     [definition of union]

Since an arbitrary x belongs to one side exactly when it belongs to the other, the two sets are equal. □

Name the step that does the work. The whole proof is the propositional De Morgan law with set membership substituted for the propositions, and saying so is the point of the question.

d. i. Bit string of the complement (2.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 2.3. With U = {1, ..., 10}, the set {1, 3, 5, 7, 9} is 1010101010. The complement flips every bit:

0101010101, which is {2, 4, 6, 8, 10}

The complement is a bitwise NOT, which is why bit strings are used for sets in the first place.

d. ii. Union and intersection by bit strings (2.5 marks)

{1, 2, 3, 4, 5} is 1111100000 and {1, 3, 5, 7, 9} is 1010101010.

Union is bitwise OR, 1 where either has a 1: 1111101010, which is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9}
Intersection is bitwise AND, 1 only where both have a 1: 1010100000, which is {1, 3, 5}

Check by eye: the intersection is the odd numbers up to 5, which is exactly what the two sets share.

a. Truth values over the real numbers (4 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 1.4. Each answer needs a witness if it is true and a counterexample if it is false; the verdict alone is worth little.

i. ∃x(x3 = −1). TRUE. Witness x = −1, since (−1)3 = −1.

ii. ∃x(x4 < x2). TRUE. Witness x = 1/2: x4 = 1/16 and x2 = 1/4, and 1/16 < 1/4. In general it holds for every x strictly between −1 and 1 other than 0.

iii. ∀x((−x)2 = x2). TRUE. For any real x, (−x)2 = (−1)2x2 = x2.

iv. ∀x(2x > x). FALSE. Counterexample x = 0, which gives 0 > 0, false. Also x = −1, which gives −2 > −1, false. The claim is only true for positive x, and this is the part where the domain "all real numbers" is doing the work.

Question Five

Answer2024/2025 Q5 · 17.5 marks

Quoted whole, as printed, in section 6.3. The scenario: 24 vertices, 30 edges, five vertices of degree 4, seven pendant vertices, seven of degree 2, and every other vertex of degree 3 or 4.

a. How many vertices of degree 4? (4 marks)

Not five. The question says five vertices are of degree 4 and then says all other vertices have degree 3 or 4, so more of them are hiding in the remainder. Work it out.

degree sum = 2|E| = 2 × 30 = 60     [Handshaking]
named vertices = 5 + 7 + 7 = 19, leaving 24 − 19 = 5
their degree = 4 × 5 + 1 × 7 + 2 × 7 = 20 + 7 + 14 = 41
degree left for the last five = 60 − 41 = 19

Let four of them have degree 4 and three of them degree 3, with four + three = 5 and 4(four) + 3(three) = 19. Substituting, 4(four) + 3(5 − four) = 19, so four = 4 and three = 1.

So the graph has 5 + 4 = 9 vertices of degree 4.

Check: the full degree list is five 4s, seven 1s, seven 2s, four 4s and one 3, summing to 20 + 7 + 14 + 16 + 3 = 60, and the vertices count 5 + 7 + 7 + 4 + 1 = 24. Both agree.

b. Degree-4 vertices in the corresponding complete graph (1 mark)

The corresponding complete graph on the same 24 vertices is K24, in which every vertex is joined to all 23 others, so every degree is 23.

Therefore the number of vertices of degree 4 in it is 0. State the reading in the same breath: "taking the corresponding complete graph to be K24". A one-mark part rewards the observation, not a calculation.

c. Does the Handshaking theorem hold here? (3 marks)

Statement. In any undirected graph, Σ deg(v) = 2|E|: the degrees sum to twice the number of edges, because each edge contributes exactly one to the degree of each of its two ends.

Applied. Here 2|E| = 60, and the degree list found in part (a) sums to 60. The two agree, so the theorem holds for this graph.

Proof of the theorem itself. Count the pairs (v, e) where vertex v is an end of edge e. Counting by vertices gives Σ deg(v); counting by edges gives 2|E|, since every edge has exactly two ends. One quantity counted two ways must give the same total. □

A consequence worth adding: the degree sum is always even, so a graph with an odd degree sum cannot exist. That is the fact part (a) relied on.

d. C++ snippet for the remaining vertices (3 marks)

Answered with a compiled and run listing in section 11.2. The snippet computes 24 − 19 = 5 vertices left, 60 − 41 = 19 degree left to share, then solves the pair of equations in one line to print four of degree 4 and one of degree 3.

e. Handshaking in data communication (2 marks)

In a communication network model the vertices are devices and the edges are links. The theorem says the total number of link ends, that is the sum over devices of how many links each has, is exactly twice the number of links.

Two uses. Capacity planning: if a network is to have 50 links, the port counts across all devices must total 100, which sizes the hardware before anything is bought. And validation: if a device inventory reports an odd total number of ports, the inventory is wrong, because no network can have an odd total. That second use is a real error check and is the better half of the answer.

f. n vertices, n edges, no degree 0 or 1 (3 marks)

To prove: if G has n vertices and n edges and no vertex has degree 0 or 1, then every vertex has degree exactly 2.

Proof. By Handshaking, Σ deg(v) = 2|E| = 2n. No vertex has degree 0 or 1, so every one of the n vertices has degree at least 2, and therefore

Σ deg(v) ≥ 2n

But the sum equals 2n. If any single vertex had degree 3 or more, the sum would exceed 2n, since the other n − 1 vertices already contribute at least 2 each. That is impossible, so no vertex exceeds degree 2. Combined with every degree being at least 2, every degree is exactly 2. □

What such a graph looks like, worth one extra line: every vertex of degree 2 and n edges means G is a disjoint union of cycles.

g. What it means for G and H to be isomorphic (1.5 marks)

G and H are isomorphic when there is a one-to-one correspondence between their vertex sets that preserves adjacency: two vertices are joined in G exactly when their partners are joined in H. In plain words, they are the same graph drawn differently, with the vertices renamed.

The consequence a marker looks for: isomorphic graphs must agree on every structural property, so the same number of vertices, the same number of edges and the same list of degrees. Any disagreement in those proves they are not isomorphic.

a. Three traversals of the given tree (6 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.3, where the tree is redrawn and each traversal is walked step by step. The tree has 17 vertices: a is the root with children b and c; b has d, e, f; e has j, k; k has n, o; c has g, h, i; h has l, m; m has p, q.

i. Preorder visits the root, then each subtree in turn:

a, b, d, e, j, k, n, o, f, c, g, h, l, m, p, q, i

ii. Inorder visits the leftmost subtree, then the root, then the remaining subtrees:

iii. Postorder visits every subtree first and the root last:

Two checks worth the ten seconds. All three lists have 17 entries. Preorder starts at the root and postorder ends at it.

b. i. The rooted tree of the expression (2 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.3. Two superscript characters in the printed expression could not be read at any available resolution, so this answers the plain reading (3 − 4 · 2) − (5 + (5 − 2)) and says in one line what changes otherwise.

The tree. The root is the outer subtraction. Its left subtree is 3 − 4 · 2, whose root is a subtraction with 3 on the left and a multiplication of 4 and 2 on the right. Its right subtree is 5 + (5 − 2), whose root is an addition with 5 on the left and a subtraction of 5 and 2 on the right.

Its value: (3 − 8) − (5 + 3) = −5 − 8 = −13.

If the two marks are exponents, the tree gains two exponent nodes, one above each bracketed group, and the values change; the structure of the answer does not.

Both are read off the tree, prefix by visiting each node before its subtrees and postfix by visiting it after. Neither is the other reversed, and neither is produced by moving the symbols of the infix expression about by eye.

b. vi. Leaves of a quinary tree of height 3 (2.5 marks)

Say what is determined first. A quinary tree of height 3 could have as few as 3 leaves; what m and h fix is the maximum. With m = 5 and h = 3:

leaves = mh = 53 = 125

Cross-check by the other formula. The full quinary tree of height 3 has internal vertices 1 + 5 + 25 = 31, and l = (m − 1)i + 1 = 4 × 31 + 1 = 125. The two agree.

b. vii. Prove the mh bound (3 marks)

Proof, by induction on the height h.

Base case, h = 0. A tree of height 0 is a single vertex, which is both root and leaf: 1 leaf, and m0 = 1.

Hypothesis. Every m-ary tree of height k has at most mk leaves.

Step. Let T have height k + 1. Removing the root leaves at most m subtrees, one per child, each of height at most k, so each has at most mk leaves by the hypothesis. Every leaf of T lies in one of them, so T has at most m × mk = mk+1 leaves. □

When the bound is attained: exactly when the tree is full and every leaf sits at level h. Saying so shows the bound is sharp, not merely true.
