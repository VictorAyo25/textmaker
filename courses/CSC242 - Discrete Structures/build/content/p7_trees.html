Trees and Rooted-Tree Terminology

Two papers ask for the key terminology of a rooted tree, worth 7.5 marks each, and a third asks where trees are used. The vocabulary is the question.

By the end of this section you will be able to define a tree and a forest, prove that a tree has one fewer edge than it has vertices, name every part of a rooted tree with a labelled diagram, and answer the terminology and application questions in full.

The official text has no trees in it. Its twelve occurrences of the word "tree" are all in one Venn diagram about trees growing on a street. What a tree is

Must memoriseTree and forest

A tree is a connected undirected graph with no cycles.

A forest is an undirected graph with no cycles that need not be connected: each of its components is a tree.

Where connected means there is a route between every pair of vertices, as defined in section 6.2. no cycles means no route leaves a vertex and returns to it without repeating an edge. This is the restriction that makes a tree a tree. acyclic is the one-word term for "no cycles", and a forest is exactly an acyclic graph.

Three equivalent definitions, any of which may be quoted. A graph on v vertices is a tree exactly when

  1. it is connected and has no cycles; or
  2. there is exactly one path between every pair of vertices; or
  3. it is connected and has exactly v − 1 edges.

Memory hook: connected, and no way back. Adding any edge to a tree creates a cycle; removing any edge disconnects it. A tree is as connected as a graph can be without being redundant.

Must memoriseThe edge count

A tree with v vertices has exactly

e = v − 1 edges.

Where v is the number of vertices and e the number of edges. the − 1 is the root, or any one vertex you like: every other vertex is reached by exactly one edge.

It is not a separate fact. Section 6.7 gave Euler's formula v − e + f = 2 for a connected planar graph. A tree has no cycles, so it encloses no region and its only face is the outer one: f = 1. Then

v − e + 1 = 2,   so   e = v − 1.

By the Handshaking theorem of section 6.3 the degrees therefore sum to 2(v − 1), which is a useful check on any tree you draw.

Memory hook: one edge per vertex, except the first. A tree of 17 vertices has 16 edges, always.

Rooted trees

Must memoriseRooted tree

A rooted tree is a tree in which one vertex has been singled out as the root, and every edge is then understood as pointing away from the root.

Where the root is chosen, not discovered: any vertex of a tree may be made the root, and different choices give different rooted trees from the same underlying tree. away from the root is what gives every other vertex a unique parent, and it is the whole reason the family vocabulary below makes sense.

Memory hook: pick a vertex and hang the tree from it. Everything else in this section is the vocabulary of what happens once you have.

Must memoriseThe key terminology

Let T be a rooted tree and v one of its vertices.

TermMeaning
RootThe single distinguished vertex. It has no parent, and it is the only vertex that does not.
ParentThe vertex immediately above v on the unique path from v to the root. Every vertex but the root has exactly one.
ChildAny vertex whose parent is v. A vertex may have any number of children, including none.
SiblingsVertices that share a parent.
AncestorsEvery vertex on the path from v up to the root, not counting v itself.
DescendantsEvery vertex having v as an ancestor, that is, everything hanging below v.
Internal vertexA vertex with at least one child. Also called a branch vertex.
LeafA vertex with no children. Also called a terminal or external vertex.
Subtree at vv together with all its descendants and the edges between them; itself a rooted tree with root v.
Level (depth) of vThe number of edges on the path from the root to v. The root is at level 0.
Height of TThe largest level of any vertex, that is, the length of the longest root-to-leaf path.
Ordered rooted treeA rooted tree in which the children of each vertex are given a left-to-right order. The traversals of section 7.3 require this.

Memory hook: it is a family tree drawn upside down. Parents above, children below, siblings alongside, ancestors all the way up, descendants all the way down. Every term means what it means in a family, except that the root is drawn at the top.

Where trees are used

As printed2023/2024 · Q4, third part

Highlight, with a relevant example each, three application domains of Tree models in computing. (4½ marks)

Note on the paper
This part has no letter. After Q4(a) and Q4(b) the paper runs straight into this sentence with its marks but no "(c)", just as Question 4 itself carries no number. It is plainly the third part of Question 4, and it is quoted here exactly as printed, with no letter supplied.

Note the count: three domains with one example each, not the four with two examples that the graph version of this question asks for in section 6.1. Read the numbers before writing.
Worked examplePQ 23/24 Q4, third part

Three domains, each with what the vertices are, what the edges are, and one concrete example. Naming the modelling is what separates this from a list of words.

  1. File systems and hierarchical storage. Vertices are directories and files; edges join a directory to what it contains. The root vertex is the root directory, every file has exactly one parent directory, and there are no cycles, which is precisely why the route from the root to any file is unique. Example: resolving a path name is a walk from the root down to a leaf.
  2. Compilers and expression evaluation. Vertices are operators and operands; the children of an operator are the things it acts on. Example: a compiler parses source code into a syntax tree and then walks it to emit machine code, which is the expression-tree work of section 7.3.
  3. Searching and decision procedures. Vertices are stored keys or states; edges are the comparisons or moves leading from one to the next. Example: a binary search tree finds a record in a number of comparisons proportional to the tree's height rather than to the number of records, which is why the height bound of section 7.2 matters.

A fourth, if the question asks for more: data compression, where a Huffman coding tree gives short codes to frequent symbols by placing them near the root.

Recall and check

1. Define a tree, and give two other equivalent definitions.

2. How many edges has a tree with 17 vertices?

3. What is the difference between a tree and a rooted tree?

4. Define leaf, internal vertex, level and height.

5. Why does a tree have exactly one face?

Answers

1. A connected acyclic undirected graph. Equivalently: exactly one path between every pair of vertices; or connected with exactly v − 1 edges.
2. 16, since e = v − 1.
3. A rooted tree has one vertex singled out as the root, which gives every other vertex a unique parent.
4. A leaf has no children; an internal vertex has at least one; the level of a vertex is its distance from the root; the height is the largest level.
5. It has no cycles, so it encloses no region; only the unbounded outer face remains, and Euler's formula then gives e = v − 1.
