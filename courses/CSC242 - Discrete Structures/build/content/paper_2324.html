a. Bit strings over U = {1, ..., 10} (3 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 2.3. The convention is fixed by the question: the i-th bit is 1 when i is in the set. Ten elements, so ten bits, and position i holds element i.

SetBit string
(i) {3, 4, 5}0011100000
(ii) {1, 3, 6, 10}1010010001
(iii) {2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9}0111001110

Check each by counting the 1s: three, four and six, matching the sizes of the three sets. That is the fastest verification available and it catches a dropped or shifted bit at once.

b. C++ program taking the sets dynamically (7 marks)

Quoted as printed, and answered with a compiled and run listing, in section 11.2. The load-bearing word is dynamically: the sets must arrive through cin at run time, not be written into the source. The program reads how many sets, then for each one reads its size and its members, builds a string of ten '0' characters and sets position m − 1 to '1' for each member m. Its output on these three sets is exactly the three strings above.

c. f ∘ g and g ∘ f, and are they equal? (3.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 4.3. With f(x) = x2 + 1 and g(x) = x + 2, both from the reals to the reals:

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x + 2) = (x + 2)2 + 1 = x2 + 4x + 5
(g ∘ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(x2 + 1) = (x2 + 1) + 2 = x2 + 3

They are not equal, and the question explicitly asks you to say so. One value proves it: at x = 1 the first gives 10 and the second gives 4.

Composition is not commutative, which is the general fact behind it. The 2024/2025 paper sets this identical pair of functions.

d. i. The divides relation on {1, 2, 3, 4} (2 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 3.1. R = {(a, b) | a divides b} means b is a multiple of a. Take each a in turn and list the multiples of a that lie in the set:

1 divides 1, 2, 3, 4
2 divides 2 and 4
3 divides 3
4 divides 4

R = {(1,1), (1,2), (1,3), (1,4), (2,2), (2,4), (3,3), (4,4)}, eight pairs.

Note it is not symmetric: (1, 2) is in R but (2, 1) is not, since 2 does not divide 1. It is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive, so it is a partial order, which is section 3.4.

b. The travelling missionary, minimum distance (10 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.8, where all twelve tours are tabulated. Five cities, every pair joined, with the distances read off the printed map:

Grand RapidsKalamazooSaginawToledoDetroit
Grand Rapids056113167147
Kalamazoo560137133135
Saginaw113137014298
Toledo167133142058
Detroit14713598580

How many tours there are. Starting and ending at Detroit, the other four cities can be visited in 4! = 24 orders, but each tour and its reverse cover the same route, so there are 24 / 2 = 12 distinct tours. Twelve is small enough to list, and the question says minimum, which is a claim about every alternative.

The answer. The shortest tour measures 458 miles:

Detroit, Saginaw, Grand Rapids, Kalamazoo, Toledo, Detroit
98 + 113 + 56 + 133 + 58 = 458

Say why a nearest-neighbour walk is not an answer. Starting at Detroit the nearest city is Toledo at 58, then Kalamazoo at 133, then Grand Rapids at 56, then Saginaw at 113, then home at 98, which totals 458 as well. It agrees here, and that is luck: nearest-neighbour carries no guarantee, so on its own it establishes an upper bound and nothing more. The enumeration is what makes 458 a minimum.

c. Three application domains of tree models (4.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.1. This part has no letter on the paper. Three domains, each with an example, at 1.5 marks apiece.

1. File systems and hierarchical storage. A directory tree has one root, every file and folder has exactly one parent, and there are no cycles, which is precisely a rooted tree. Example: a path such as /home/user/notes.txt is the unique route from the root to a leaf, and the uniqueness of that path is a property of trees, not of graphs generally.

2. Compilers and expression evaluation. A parser turns source code into a syntax tree whose internal vertices are operators and whose leaves are operands, and the compiler then walks it. Example: section 7.3 builds the tree of (3 − 4 · 2) − (5 + (5 − 2)) and reads its postfix form straight off a postorder walk, which is exactly what a stack machine consumes.

3. Searching and decision procedures. A binary search tree keeps data ordered so that each comparison discards half the remaining possibilities, and a decision tree encodes a sequence of tests. Example: the binary search of section 11.2 halves its range each pass, so it reaches an answer in the number of halvings rather than in the number of items.

Two more if the marks look generous: organisational charts and family trees, where the hierarchy is the point; and prefix codes such as Huffman coding, where the tree structure is what makes the code unambiguous.

Question Five

Answer2023/2024 Q5 · 23.5 marks

a. Define the Handshaking theorem (3 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.3.

Statement. In any undirected graph G = (V, E), the sum of the degrees of all the vertices equals twice the number of edges:

Σ deg(v) = 2|E|

Why. Each edge has exactly two ends, and each end adds one to the degree of the vertex it meets, so counting edge ends by vertices and by edges must give the same total.

Two consequences worth adding for three marks. The degree sum is always even, so no graph can have an odd degree sum. And the number of vertices of odd degree is always even, since the odd degrees must pair up to leave an even total.

b. i. Can the pilot visit exactly three of five cities? (3 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 6.3. Model the five cities as vertices and each direct route as an edge. "Visit exactly three from each" means every vertex has degree exactly 3.

Compute. Five vertices, each of degree 3:

Σ deg(v) = 5 × 3 = 15

But Handshaking says this sum must equal 2|E|, which is even, and 15 is odd.

So no such flight plan exists. It is impossible, and the impossibility is not about scheduling or geography: no graph on five vertices can have every degree 3.

The general rule behind it, worth one line: a graph in which every vertex has degree d and there are n vertices requires nd to be even, so n and d cannot both be odd.

b. ii. Support the answer with a diagram (2 marks)

Draw the five cities as five dots and attempt it. Give four of them three edges each and the fifth is forced to have an even number, because the twelve edge ends already placed must pair off. Any attempt leaves one vertex at degree 2 or 4.

Then draw what does work, which shows you understood rather than merely failed: with six cities, every vertex of degree 3 is possible, since 6 × 3 = 18 is even. K3,3 is exactly such a graph, and so is the triangular prism.

d. The convocation chain letter (9.5 marks)

Quoted as printed in section 7.2. Model it as a rooted tree: the first person is the root, and each person who sends the cards on has four children. Those who received but did not send are the leaves, and there are 100 of them. Nobody receives more than one card, which is what makes it a tree rather than a general graph.

The tree is full, because everyone who sends sends to exactly four, so every internal vertex has exactly m = 4 children.

(i) How many have seen the card (6 marks). For a full m-ary tree with i internal vertices, the leaves number l = (m − 1)i + 1, so

100 = 3i + 1
3i = 99, so i = 33

and the total number of vertices is

n = mi + 1 = 4 × 33 + 1 = 133

So 133 people have seen the card, including the first person. Check with the other identity: n = i + l = 33 + 100 = 133. The two agree, which is the verification worth writing down.

(ii) How many sent it out (3.5 marks). The senders are exactly the internal vertices, so 33 people sent the card on, and the first person is one of them.

The trap in the wording: "100 people who read it but did not send it out" describes the leaves, not the whole readership. A candidate who takes 100 as the answer to part (i) has answered a question the paper did not ask.
