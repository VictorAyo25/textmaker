Permutations and Combinations

One question decides which formula you need: does the order of the chosen objects change the answer? If it does, you are permuting. If it does not, you are combining.

By the end of this section you will be able to compute factorials, use the permutation and combination formulas, count arrangements of a word with repeated letters, handle "at least one" selections, and answer the SCHOOL, the six boys and four girls, and the prayer partners questions in full.

Factorial notation

Must memoriseFactorial

Section F.1 introduced this notation. It is restated here because every formula in this part is built from it, and you should not have to turn back mid-question.

For a positive integer n, n factorial, written n!, is the product of every positive integer from n down to 1:

n! = n × (n − 1) × (n − 2) × … × 2 × 1

and by definition

0! = 1

Where n is a whole number that is not negative. ! is read "factorial", so 5! is read "five factorial". It is not an exclamation mark and never punctuation.

Memory hook: count down and multiply. And 0! = 1 is not a trick: there is exactly one way to arrange nothing, namely do nothing, so the count is 1, not 0.

Factorials grow violently. That is the reason counting questions ask for an expression as often as a number: 20! has nineteen digits and no examiner wants it written out.

The cancelling trick, which you will use constantly. Because n! = n × (n − 1)!, a ratio of factorials collapses:

10! / 7! = (10 × 9 × 8 × 7!) / 7! = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720

Never compute 10! and 7! separately and divide. Cancel first, always.

Permutations: order matters

Must memorisePermutation

A permutation is an arrangement of objects in a definite order. An r-permutation of n distinct objects is an ordered selection of r of them, and the number of these is

P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)!

Where n is how many distinct objects are available. r is how many are being chosen and arranged, with 0 ≤ r ≤ n. P(n, r) is also written nPr or nPr, which is the label printed on most calculators.

The special case r = n: arranging all n objects gives P(n, n) = n! / 0! = n!. Every permutation question about "all of them" is just a factorial.

Memory hook: P for position. A permutation cares where each object lands, so AB and BA are two different answers.

TeachWhere the formula comes from

You already have this from the product rule. To fill r ordered positions from n objects with no repeats:

position 1: n ways
position 2: n − 1 ways, since one object is used up
position 3: n − 2 ways
…
position r: n − r + 1 ways

Multiplying gives n × (n − 1) × … × (n − r + 1), which is r factors counting down from n. Writing that as a ratio of factorials:

n × (n − 1) × … × (n − r + 1) = n! / (n − r)!

because dividing by (n − r)! cancels exactly the tail of the product that we stopped short of. So P(n, r) is not a new idea; it is the product rule packaged.

Worked: P(6, 4) = 6! / 2! = 720 / 2 = 360, and by the counting-down form 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 = 360. The two agree, as they must.

Combinations: order does not matter

Must memoriseCombination

A combination is a selection of objects without regard to order. The number of ways of choosing r objects from n distinct objects is

C(n, r) = n! / (r! (n − r)!)

Where n is how many objects are available. r is how many are chosen, with 0 ≤ r ≤ n. C(n, r) is also written nCr or nCr.

Why the extra r! Each unordered selection of r objects can be arranged in r! different orders, and P(n, r) counts every one of those orders separately. Dividing by r! collapses them back into one:

C(n, r) = P(n, r) / r!

Memory hook: C for committee. A committee of three is the same committee however you read out the names, so order is thrown away and you divide by r!.

TrapDeciding which one you need

Ask the question in this exact form: if I swap two of the chosen objects, is that a different answer?

TaskSwap changes it?Use
Arranging letters into a wordyes, TAB is not BATpermutation
Filling the posts of chair and secretaryyes, the two people swap jobspermutation
Picking 4 students for a teamno, the same four peoplecombination
Choosing a subset of a setno, a set has no ordercombination

The wording that gives it away: arrange, order, line up, rank, first and second point to a permutation; select, choose, pick, committee, team, subset, group point to a combination.

One warning about that list. It is a guide, not a law. Read the task, not the verb: a question that says "select" and then gives the chosen people different jobs is a permutation despite the word.

Arranging a word with repeated letters

Must memoriseArrangements with repeats

The number of distinct arrangements of n objects, of which one kind repeats n1 times, another n2 times, and so on, is

n! / (n1! × n2! × … × nk!)

Where n is the total number of objects, letters counted as often as they appear. ni is how many times the i-th repeated object occurs. Objects appearing once contribute 1! = 1 and can be left out.

Why divide. Pretend the repeats are distinguishable for a moment, say O1 and O2. Then all n objects are distinct and there are n! arrangements. But swapping O1 and O2 produces a word that looks identical, so every genuine word has been counted 2! times. Divide by 2! to correct it.

Memory hook: divide out what you cannot tell apart. One factorial on the bottom for each repeated letter, using how many times it repeats.

As printed2025/2026 · Q4(d)

(d) Find out the number of ways that the letters of the word "SCHOOL" can be arranged? (2½ marks)

Note on the paper
A command punctuated as a question, which this paper does in several places. Nothing else is wrong with it, and the quotation marks around the word are the paper's own. The word is the whole difficulty: SCHOOL has six letters and the letter O appears twice.
Break it down
Given
The six letters of the word SCHOOL: S, C, H, O, O, L.
Asked
The number of ways they can be arranged. Arranged means ordered, so this is a permutation.
Marks say
2.5 marks. That is enough for the formula, the substitution and the answer, and not enough to list 360 words. Do not list them.
Teaches
This section, 5.2.
Trap
Answering 6! = 720. The two O's are identical, so half of those 720 orderings spell the same word twice over. The answer is 360.
Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q4(d)
Given
The letters S, C, H, O, O, L. Total n = 6, with the letter O repeated twice.
Find
The number of distinct arrangements.
Formula
n! divided by the factorial of each repeat count.

Step 1. Count the letters and the repeats.

LetterSCHOL
Times it occurs11121

Total letters n = 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 6. One letter repeats, namely O, and it repeats twice.

Step 2. Apply the formula.

arrangements = 6! / 2!
= 720 / 2
= 360

Answer: the letters of SCHOOL can be arranged in 360 ways.

State the reasoning in one line for the marker: if the six letters were all different there would be 6! = 720 arrangements, but swapping the two O's changes nothing visible, so every distinct word has been counted 2! times and 720 must be halved.

Now redo it: how many arrangements has the word LEVEL? Five letters with L twice and E twice, so 5! / (2! 2!) = 120 / 4 = 30.

Selecting with a condition

As printed2025/2026 · Q3(b)

(b) In a group of students, there are 6 boys and 4 girls. Out of 10 students, 4 students have to be selected. Find out how many different ways the students can be selected such that at least one boy should be selected. (5 marks)

Note on the paper
"Out of 10 students" restates what the first sentence already said, since 6 + 4 = 10. That is a helpful redundancy rather than a defect: it confirms the ten students are the whole pool. The phrase to act on is "at least one boy", which is the subtraction rule of section 5.1 in a combination setting.
Break it down
Given
6 boys and 4 girls, 10 students in all. Four are to be selected.
Asked
How many different ways the selection can be made with at least one boy among the four.
Order
A group of four is selected, with no jobs or ranking attached, so order does not matter. This is a combination.
Marks say
5 marks. Enough for the total, the excluded case, the subtraction and a check. Showing the case-by-case method as a verification is worth doing if time allows.
Teaches
This section, 5.2, using the subtraction rule from 5.1.
Trap
Reading "at least one boy" as "exactly one boy" and answering C(6, 1) × C(4, 3) = 24. At least one means one, two, three or four.
Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q3(b)
Given
6 boys, 4 girls, 10 students; choose 4.
Find
The number of selections containing at least one boy.
Formula
C(n, r) = n! / (r! (n − r)!), with the subtraction rule for "at least one".

Step 1. Count all selections of four, ignoring the condition.

C(10, 4) = 10! / (4! × 6!)
= (10 × 9 × 8 × 7) / (4 × 3 × 2 × 1)
= 5040 / 24
= 210

Step 2. Count the selections that break the condition. A selection fails only when it contains no boy, that is, when all four chosen are girls. There are exactly 4 girls, so all four must be taken:

C(4, 4) = 4! / (4! × 0!) = 1

Note 0! = 1 here; that definition is doing real work.

Step 3. Subtract.

at least one boy = 210 − 1 = 209

Answer: 209 different selections contain at least one boy.

Verification by cases, which is the long way and a good check:

BoysGirlsWaysValue
13C(6, 1) × C(4, 3)6 × 4 = 24
22C(6, 2) × C(4, 2)15 × 6 = 90
31C(6, 3) × C(4, 1)20 × 4 = 80
40C(6, 4) × C(4, 0)15 × 1 = 15

24 + 90 + 80 + 15 = 209. The two methods agree.

The case table also shows why subtracting is the better exam move: four combinations and three additions, against one subtraction.

Now redo it: how many selections of four contain at least one girl? Total 210, minus the all-boy selections C(6, 4) = 15, gives 195.

The prayer partners

As printed2025/2026 · Q3(c)

(c) Suppose 10 male and 10 female students needs to pray with one another in a one male-one female order:

(i) How many Prayer sessions will there be? (1 mark)

(ii) How many prayer units are generated here? (1½ marks)

(iii) Calculate the choice of prayer partners. (3 marks)

Note on the paper
Two things to flag. First, "students needs" does not agree in number, and "Prayer" is capitalised in (i) but not in (ii). Those are harmless. Second, and this one matters: the paper never says what a session is or what a unit is, so parts (i) and (ii) can be read more than one way. The answer below states the reading it uses before computing anything, gives the numbers that reading produces, and then gives the other reading's numbers too. Part (iii) is unambiguous and carries the most marks.
Break it down
Given
10 male and 10 female students. They pray in pairs, each pair being one male and one female.
Asked
(i) the number of sessions, (ii) the number of units, (iii) calculate the choice of prayer partners.
Marks say
1, 1.5 and 3. The weight is on (iii), which is a full permutation, so budget your time there. Parts (i) and (ii) are one line each.
Teaches
This section, 5.2, with the product rule from 5.1.
Trap
Answering (iii) with 10 × 10 = 100. That counts the possible pairs, which is part (ii). The choice of partners means assigning every male a different female at once, and that is 10!.
Worked examplePQ 25/26 Q3(c)
Given
10 males, 10 females, praying one male with one female.
Find
Sessions, units, and the number of ways to choose partners.
Rule
Product rule for the pairs, permutation for the assignment.

Step 0. State the reading. Write this on the script before answering, because the paper does not supply it:

A prayer unit is one male paired with one female. A session is one round in which all twenty students are paired at the same time, giving ten units per session. Every male is to pray with every female.

i. How many prayer sessions

In one session each of the 10 males prays with one female, so a session uses up 10 of the possible male-female pairings. Every male must eventually meet all 10 females, so each male needs 10 turns:

number of sessions = 10

ii. How many prayer units

A unit is one male with one female. Choosing the male is 10 ways and choosing the female is 10 ways, and both choices must be made, so the product rule gives

units = 10 × 10 = 100

Consistency check: 10 sessions with 10 units in each is 100 units, which matches. The two answers agree, which is exactly why stating the reading first was worth a line.

iii. Calculate the choice of prayer partners

Now the real work. In a single session, every male is given a different female. Line the males up in a fixed order and hand out the females:

male 1: 10 choices of partner
male 2: 9 choices, since one female is taken
male 3: 8 choices
…
male 10: 1 choice

All ten choices must be made, so the product rule applies:

choices = 10 × 9 × 8 × … × 2 × 1 = 10! = 3628800

In the language of this section, the ten females are being arranged among the ten males, so the count is P(10, 10) = 10! / 0! = 10!.

Answer: 3628800 ways, or 3 628 800 with separators.

The other reading, for safety. If instead a "session" means a single male-female meeting, then there are 100 sessions and 10 units in each round. Whichever reading a marker has in mind, the two numbers on the table are 10 and 100, and part (iii) is 10! = 3628800 either way. Writing your reading down protects the marks.

Now redo it: with 5 males and 5 females, the units are 5 × 5 = 25 and the choice of partners is 5! = 120.

Recall and check

1. State the formulas for P(n, r) and C(n, r).

2. What is 0!, and why?

3. Which is larger, P(8, 3) or C(8, 3), and by what factor?

4. How many arrangements has the word SCHOOL, and why is it not 720?

5. Four students are picked from ten for a team. Permutation or combination?

Answers

1. P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)! and C(n, r) = n! / (r! (n − r)!).
2. 0! = 1, because there is exactly one way to arrange nothing.
3. P(8, 3) is larger, by a factor of 3! = 6: P(8, 3) = 336 and C(8, 3) = 56.
4. 360. The two O's are identical, so 6! counts every word twice and must be divided by 2!.
5. A combination: the team is the same team however the names are ordered.
